Introduction to Services

Zeithaml, Chapter 1

What services are

Services are deeds, processes and performancesZeithaml's definition of a service: acts provided, coproduced or cocreated by one party for another, rather than objects.Three nouns, all of them actions. If you can drop it on your foot it is not a service. Use the wording verbatim. provided, coproduced or cocreated by one entity or person for and/or with another entity or person.

The broader economic definition: all economic activities whose output is not a physical product or construction, is generally consumed at the time it is produced, and provides added value in forms (convenience, amusement, timeliness, comfort, health) that are essentially intangible concerns of its first purchaser.

Four ways the word "service" is used

A standard short-answer question. Keep the four apart and attach one example each.

Service industries and companies
Industries where the core product is a service. Marriott (lodging), American Airlines (transportation), Mayo Clinic (health care).
Service as a product
Intangible product offerings customers value and pay for. Sold by service and non-service firms alike: IBM technology consulting, PetSmart pet training, Macy's gift wrapping and shipping.
Customer service
Service provided in support of a company's core products. Companies typically do not charge for it. On-site, by phone, or via internet chat in real time.
Derived service
Vargo and Lusch: physical goods are valued for the service they provide. A pharmaceutical drug gives medical service, a razor gives barbering service, a computer gives data-manipulation service.

Tangibility spectrum ✱✱✱must know

The tangibility spectrumA continuum classifying offerings from tangible dominant (salt) to intangible dominant (teaching).Shostack, 1977. The trap is writing that services are simply 'intangible'. The point is that it is a continuum, which is why fast food is the standard middle case. is a continuumA range of things that gradually change from one extreme to another, without clear breaks between them.Like a gradient from black to white: every shade in between, and no line where one becomes the other. Salt to teaching runs the same way. that classifies goods and services by how tangible or intangible they are, from tangible dominant to intangible dominant. Pure goods sit at the far left, pure services at the far right.

Tangible dominant →Middle→ Intangible dominant
Salt · soft drinks · detergents · automobiles Fast-food outlets · cosmetics · advertising agencies Airlines · investment management · consulting · teaching

How it works

  • Very few products are purely intangible or totally tangible. Fast food is classified as a service yet includes food and packaging; an automobile is manufactured yet supplies transportation and navigation services.
  • Services tend to be more intangible than manufactured products; manufactured products tend to be more tangible than services.
  • Even intangible-dominant services include tangible components: consumables and durables.
  • Tangible evidence can be used to indicate service quality.

Benefits

  • Helps service providers identify ways to improve service quality.
  • Helps them use tangible cuesThe physical signals customers read to judge a service they cannot otherwise assess: decor, uniforms, equipment, reports, paperwork.The same idea recurs three times in this syllabus: physical evidence in the 7 Ps, the servicescape in gap 2, risk reduction in Lovelock. or develop a corporate image to signal quality where the offering itself cannot be seen or touched.
Why it matters here

Source: Shostack, "Breaking Free from Product Marketing" (1977). The spectrum is also the model for the four characteristics of services below: each of those sits on a continuum in exactly the same way, rather than being a yes/no property.

Services in practice

Service-based economy in Bangladesh

Bangladesh's services sector expanded by nearly 90% over five years to Tk 18.98 trillion, about 54% of the Tk 34.84 trillion nominal GDP in FY2020–21. It spans accommodation, education, financial services, transport, and wholesale and retail trading.

The wider claim behind it: all businesses are service businesses. IBM, General Electric, Apple and Samsung are service providers; most new business models, such as Airbnb and Uber, are built on a services model.

Why services marketing?

  • Service-based economy. Services dominate GDP and employment. In Bangladesh the services sector reached Tk 18.98 trillion, about 54% of nominal GDP in FY2020–21.
  • Service as a business imperative in goods-focused business. GE, Caterpillar, Xerox, IBM and P&G grow through service. Drivers: commoditizationWhen rival products become so alike that customers choose on price alone.Prices fall, and so does the profit on each sale. Service becomes one of the few things left to compete on. squeezing margins, customers demanding solutions, higher service margins, service as a differentiator.
  • Deregulated industries and professional service needs. Airlines, banking, telecom and trucking deregulatedGovernment rules that fixed prices, routes or who was allowed into an industry were removed.Firms that had been protected suddenly had to compete for customers, so marketing started to matter.. Doctors, lawyers, accountants and architects now compete and advertise.
  • Service marketing is different. Packaged-goodsEveryday branded products sold off a shelf: soap, cereal, toothpaste.They can be standardised, sampled and put in a box. None of that works for a service, which is why the old skills did not carry over. skills did not transfer. New concepts were required.
  • Service equals profits. Service quality drives loyalty and satisfaction, and therefore profit.

…but "service stinks"The complaint that service is getting worse, not better.The standard objection to everything in the list above. Customers say they are promised more and handed less.

The counter-argument: firms offer tiered serviceSplitting customers into levels and giving each level a different standard of service.The most profitable customers get the fast lane. Everyone else waits, which is why service can feel worse even as firms spend more on it. so many customers get less than before. Self-service and technology-based service spreads. Expectations rise and are applied to every firm. Organizations cut costs. Firms pay lip serviceSaying something matters while doing nothing about it.The firm calls service a priority in public, then leaves staff untrained and underpaid., failing to train and compensate employees.

Service and technology

Technology creates new service offerings, new ways to deliver service, engages both customers and employees, gives services global reach, and is itself a service (the internet). The dark side: customers and employees may resist it, human contact is lost, and the payback on technology investment is uncertain.

Characteristics of services

Four characteristics, each with resulting marketing implications. This is the most examinable single object in the chapter, so reproduce it.

Nuance that earns marks

Each characteristic sits on a continuum, like the tangibility spectrum: the goods/services difference is not black and white. Note also the newer view that "all products are services" and economic exchange is fundamentally about service provision.

Services marketing mix: the 7 Ps

The marketing mixThe controllable elements a firm uses to satisfy and communicate with customers.The 4 Ps are McCarthy, 1960; the extra three are Booms and Bitner, 1981. Naming both sources is cheap marks. is the set of elements an organization controls that can be used to satisfy or communicate with customers. The traditional mix is the 4 Ps: product, place (distribution), promotion, price.

Exam answer · why are the 4 Ps extended to 7 Ps in services marketing?

Three reasons, each traced back to a characteristic of services:

1. Simultaneous production and consumption. Customers are present in the firm's "factory", interact directly with personnel, and are actually part of the production process, so people must be managed as a mix element. Delivery staff perform real-time promotion whether or not their job is defined that way.

2. Intangibility. Customers have nothing to see or touch, so they hunt for any tangible cue to judge quality, so physical evidence must be managed.

3. Services are processes, not objects. The operational flow the customer experiences is itself evidence they judge the service by, so process must be managed.

The three are included as separate elements because they are particularly salient for services, they are within the firm's control, and any of them may influence both the initial purchase decision and later satisfaction and repurchase.

The three added elements:

People
All human actors who play a part in service delivery and thus influence the buyer's perceptions: the firm's personnel, the customer, and other customers in the service environment. For consulting, counselling or teaching, the provider is the service.
Physical evidence
The environment in which the service is delivered and where firm and customer interact, plus tangible components that facilitate performance or communication: brochures, letterhead, business cards, reports, signage, equipment, web pages, and the servicescapeThe physical setting in which the service is delivered and where firm and customer interact.Bitner's term. It sits in gap 2, not gap 3: a wrong servicescape is a design failure, not a delivery one..
Process
The procedures, mechanisms and flow of activities by which the service is delivered, consumed and cocreated. Standardized vs. customized, simple vs. complex, degree of customer involvement. Compare Southwest (no-frills, standardized) with Singapore Airlines (customized, empowered staff).

b. The nonownership framework: benefits without ownership

Lovelock and Gummesson: services involve a form of rental through which customers obtain benefits. You pay rent for using or accessing something for a defined period instead of buying it outright. Five broad categories, every one ticked in the photocopy:

1. Rented goods services
Temporary right to use a physical good you prefer not to own: boats, fancy dress costumes, combine harvesters.
2. Defined space and place rentals
Use of a defined portion of a larger space: a suite in an office building, a seat in an aircraft, a table in a restaurant.
3. Labor and expertise rentals
Hiring others to do work you choose not to, or lack the expertise, tools or skills for: cleaning a house, car repair, surgery, management consultancy.
4. Access to shared physical environments
Indoor or outdoor environments shared with other customers for a fee: museums, theme parks, trade shows, gyms, ski resorts, golf courses, toll roads.
5. Access to and usage of systems and networks
Renting the right to participate in a specified network: telecommunications, utilities, banking, insurance, specialized information services.

Categories combine: a taxi rents you both a driver and a vehicle.

d. Services pose distinct marketing challenges

Marketing concepts from manufacturing are often not directly transferable where no transfer of ownership takes place. The Gaps Model of Service Quality

Zeithaml, Chapter 2

The customer gap

The customer gapThe difference between what a customer expected and what they perceived they received.An outcome, never a cause. In a case question you must also name which provider gap produced it. is the difference between customer expectationsThe reference standards a customer brings into the encounter: what they believe should or will happen.Split them by source when answering: marketer-controlled (price, advertising, promises) versus not (needs, word of mouth, competitors). and customer perceptionsThe customer's subjective assessment of the service as actually experienced.Subjective, so they can be factually wrong and still count. That is why managing perception is as legitimate as improving delivery..

Customer expectations
Standards or reference points customers bring into the service experience: what a customer believes should or will happen.
Customer perceptions
Subjective assessments of actual service experiences.

Sources of expectations split into marketer-controlled factors (pricing, advertising, sales promises) and factors the marketer can barely affect (innate personal needs, word-of-mouth communications, competitive offerings). Closing this gap is the goal of the entire book.

Types of customer gap (from the lecture slides)

Customer service gap
Between what customers expect and what they receive.
Value gap
Between what customers perceive the value of the product or service to be and what the company believes it is.
Customer experience gap
Between what customers expect from their experiences with a brand and how well the brand believes it is meeting those expectations.

Factors contributing to customer gaps: a customer's background, lifestyle, personality, demographics and advertising; prior experiences with the product or service; market trends; product messaging.

The provider gaps

To close the all-important customer gap, four provider gaps, the gaps occurring within the organization providing the service, must be closed and kept closed.

Gap 1 · The listening gap

Between customer expectations of service and company understanding of those expectations. Firms fail because they lack an accurate understanding of what customers expect. Key factors:

  • Inadequate customer research orientation: insufficient research, research not focused on service quality, inadequate use of market research.
  • Lack of upward communication: little interaction between management and customers, poor communication between contact employees and managers, too many layers between contact personnel and top management.
  • Insufficient relationship focus: lack of segmentation, focus on transactions rather than relationships, focus on new customers rather than relationship customers. (Relationship marketing vs. transactional marketing.)
  • Inadequate service recovery: no encouragement to listen to complaints, failure to make amends, no recovery mechanisms for service failures.

Gap 2 · The service design and standards gap

Between company understanding of customer expectations and the development of customer-driven service designs and standards. Customer-driven standards differ from conventional performance standards: they are based on pivotal customer requirements visible to and measured by customers, not on company concerns such as productivity. Key factors:

  • Poor service design: unsystematic new service development process, vague undefined designs, failure to connect design to service positioning.
  • Absence of customer-driven standardsOperating standards written from what customers require and can observe, not from internal efficiency goals.'Answer within 20 seconds' qualifies. 'Handle 40 calls an hour' is a productivity target in disguise.: no customer-driven service standards, no process management focused on customer requirements, no formal process for setting service quality goals.
  • Inappropriate physical evidence and servicescape: tangibles not in line with customer expectations, servicescape design that fails customers and employees, inadequate maintenance and updating.

Tool that closes it: service blueprintingA diagram of the whole service process that separates what the customer sees from what happens behind the scenes.Chapter 8's tool, but the answer to a gap 2 question. Its line of visibility is the servuction model's front/back stage boundary..

Gap 3 · The service performance gap

Between customer-driven service standards and actual service performance by employees. Standards must be backed by resources and enforced, with employees measured and compensated against them. Key factors:

  • Deficiencies in human resource policies: ineffective recruitment, role ambiguity and role conflict, poor employee–technology job fit, inappropriate evaluation and compensation, lack of empowerment, perceived control and teamwork.
  • Customers not fulfilling roles: customers lack knowledge of their roles and responsibilities; customers negatively impact each other.
  • Problems with service intermediaries: channel conflict over objectives, performance, costs and rewards; difficulty controlling quality and consistency; tension between empowerment and control.
  • Failure to match supply and demand: failure to smooth peaks and valleys, inappropriate customer mix, overreliance on price to smooth demand.

Gap 4 · The communication gap

Between service delivery and the provider's external communications. Promises made through advertising, the sales force and other communications raise expectations, the standard against which quality is judged. Key factors:

  • Lack of integrated services marketing communications: treating each external communication as independent, excluding interactive marketingThe marketing that happens inside the encounter itself, between contact staff and the customer.Completes the services triangle with external and internal marketing. Gap 4 opens when the three disagree. (marketing between contact people and customers) from the plan, no strong internal marketing program.
  • Ineffective management of customer expectations: not managing expectations through all forms of communication, not adequately educating customers.
  • Overpromising: in advertising, in personal selling, through physical evidence cues.
  • Inadequate horizontal communications: between sales and operations, between advertising and operations; differences in policies and procedures across branches.
  • Inappropriate pricing: high prices that raise expectations; prices not tied to customer perceptions of value. Customers often have no internal reference price for a service before purchase.
Technology across the model

Technology affects every gap: social media research and responding to customer tweets (gap 1); technology-enabled design and standards (gap 2); simultaneous chat sessions and self-service technologies that empower employees and customers (gap 3); online word of mouthCustomer-to-customer communication about a service.It appears at both ends of the syllabus: it shapes expectations before purchase and is an outcome after. State which you mean. and borderless reach that reshape expectations (gap 4).

Two traps

Don't stop at the customer gap. It is the outcome, not the cause. A full answer names the provider gap and the specific key factor behind it.

Recall in one line each

  • Gap 1 listening · Gap 2 service design and standards · Gap 3 service performance · Gap 4 communication.

The three-stage model of service consumption ✱✱important

Service consumption divides into three principal stages. The nature of the steps varies by whether the service is high-contact (great degree of interaction between service employees and customers, typical of people-processing services) or low-contact (little to no face-to-face contact, common for information-processing services delivered at arm's length).

01

Pre-purchase

  • Awareness of need
  • Information search
  • Evaluation of alternatives
  • Purchase decision
02

Service encounter

  • Request service from chosen supplier, or initiate self-service
  • Service delivery by personnel or self-service
03

Post-encounter

  • Evaluation of service performance
  • Future intentions

Stage 1 · Pre-purchase

The four steps are need awareness → information search → evaluation of alternatives → purchase decision. The first two are quick. Everything else in this stage sits inside evaluation of alternatives, and it unfolds in one chain: what the customer is able to judge (attributes) decides how much they fear choosing wrongly (perceived risk), and that fear sets the standard they arrive with (expectations).

Begins with need arousalThe moment a customer becomes aware of a need, which starts the pre-purchase stage.Three triggers: unconscious, physical, external. Only the third is under a marketer's control., the prospective customer's awareness of a need. Needs may be triggered by unconscious minds (personal identity and aspirations), physical conditions (hunger), or external sources (a firm's marketing activities).

Information search follows. The alternatives that come to mind form the evoked setThe small group of brands a customer actually considers, drawn from memory or from search.Not every option available. Getting into it is a communications problem; winning inside it is positioning. (also called the consideration set), the set of products or brands a customer may consider in the decision-making process, derived from past experience or external sources.

With an evoked set in hand the customer moves to evaluation of alternatives. Services make this step hard, and the difficulty has one source: how much of the offer can be judged before buying it. Lovelock sorts that into three kinds of attribute.

Service attributes: the three types

Search attributes
Tangible characteristics customers can evaluate before purchase: style, color, texture, taste, sound. Clothing, furniture, cars and foods are high in search attributesFeatures a customer can evaluate before buying: colour, style, price, location.Goods are rich in these and services are not, which is the real evidence for 'service marketing is different'.; so are a restaurant's location, type of food and price.
Experience attributes
Cannot be evaluated before purchase; the customer must "experience" the service: reliability, ease of use, customer support. Vacations, live entertainment, many medical procedures.
Credence attributes
Characteristics customers find hard to evaluate even after consumption; the customer is forced to trust the task was done at the promised level: kitchen hygiene, complex surgery, legal advice, consulting.

All products sit on a continuum from easy to evaluate to difficult to evaluate. Most physical goods sit left (high in search attributes); most services sit centre-to-right (high in experience and credence attributesFeatures a customer cannot confidently judge even after consumption: a diagnosis, an audit, legal advice.Why professional firms lean on credentials and guarantees. The customer buys the signals instead of the work.). Do not overgeneralize: an experienced customer evaluates differently from a first-time user.

Perceived risk: seven types

The harder a service is to evaluate before purchase, the higher the perceived riskThe customer's sense of possible loss from a purchase they cannot fully assess in advance.Seven types and the order is easy to lose. Two F's, two P's, then the rest: Functional, Financial, Temporal, Physical, Psychological, Social, Sensory.. Customers are risk averse and, everything else equal, choose the service with the lower perceived risk.

Type of riskCustomer concern
FunctionalUnsatisfactory performance outcomes: "Will this training course give me the skills I need?"
FinancialMonetary loss, unexpected costs: "Will repairing my car cost more than the estimate?"
TemporalWasting time, consequences of delays: "Will I have to wait in line before entering?"
PhysicalPersonal injury or damage to possessions: "Will I get hurt if I go skiing at this resort?"
PsychologicalPersonal fears and emotions: "Will the consultant make me feel stupid?"
SocialHow others think and react: "What will my friends think if they learnt I stayed at this cheap motel?"
SensoryUnwanted effects on any of the five senses: "Will my room smell of stale cigarette smoke?"

How customers reduce risk: seek information from trusted personal sources; use the web to compare offerings and read independent reviews; rely on a firm with a good reputation; look for guarantees and warranties; visit facilities or try the service, examining tangible cues; ask knowledgeable employees about competing services.

How firms reduce customers' perceived risk: encourage previews through brochures, websites and videos; encourage visits to facilities in advance; offer free trials for services high in experience attributesFeatures that can only be judged during or after consumption: a meal, a flight, a haircut.Free trials work here and nowhere else, since they cannot resolve a credence attribute at all.; advertise; display credentials; use evidence managementDeliberately organising every tangible signal a customer meets so they add up to the intended image.Berry and Bendapudi's term. Treat it as the operational form of 'tangible cues', with a checklist attached., an organized approach presenting coherent evidence of the company's targeted image and value proposition, including furnishings, equipment, facilities, employee dress and behavior; institute visible safety procedures; give online access to order status; offer service guarantees.

Risk is what the customer wants to avoid. Expectations are the standard they will judge against, formed during the same evaluation, carried into the encounter and used again in stage 3 to decide whether they were satisfied.

Service expectations: four components

Desired service
The "wished for" level, a combination of what customers believe can and should be delivered, given their personal needs. Influenced by explicit and implicit promises, word of mouth and past experience.
Adequate service
The minimum level of service customers will accept without being dissatisfied.
Predicted service
The level customers actually anticipate receiving. It directly affects how customers define "adequate serviceThe minimum level of service a customer will accept without being dissatisfied.The floor, not the target. The examinable bit is the asymmetry: adequate moves easily with circumstance, desired barely shifts." on that occasion, and is often situation-specific.
Zone of tolerance
The range between desired and adequate service, the extent to which customers are willing to accept variation. Performing below it causes frustration; exceeding the desired level should surprise and delight. Within the zone, customers don't pay explicit attention to performance.

The zone widens or narrows with competition, price and importance of specific attributes. Adequate service levels move readily; desired levels move up only slowly. Predicted service is probably the most important level for the consumer choice process.

Purchase decision: after comparing attributes, assessing perceived risk and forming desired, adequate and predicted expectations, the customer selects the preferred option. Frequently purchased services are decided quickly; complex decisions involve trade-offs, with price often the key factor.

A service encounterAny period in which a customer interacts directly with a service provider.Duration is irrelevant to the definition: a 15-second call and a four-day hospital stay both qualify. is a period of time during which a customer interacts directly with a service provider. Some are very brief (a taxi ride, a phone call); others extend over a longer time frame and involve multiple actions (a hospital visit lasting several days). High-contact services supply a greater array of clues to service quality than low-contact services.

The two steps of this stage, requesting the service and delivery, are both made of encounters, so the whole stage answers one question: what happens inside an encounter and what can the firm control there? The five concepts below answer it in order. What an encounter is worth (moments of truth), how much of it is face to face (contact levels), which parts of the operation the customer can see (servuction system), how that visible part is staged (theater), and who is expected to do what (role and script theories).

1 · Service encounters are "moments of truth"

Richard Normann borrowed the metaphor from bullfighting: perceived quality is realized at the moment of truthThe instant of contact at which the customer forms their judgement of quality.Normann took it from bullfighting, where the phrase means the kill. Quote Carlzon's arithmetic: 10 million customers, 5 staff, 15 seconds., when the service provider and the service customer confront one another in the arena. At that moment they are very much on their own: it is the skill, motivation and tools of the firm's representative and the expectations and behavior of the client which together create the service delivery process.

In bullfighting the life of the bull or the matador is at stake; in services it is the life of the relationship. The goal of relationship marketingBuilding and retaining long-term customer ties rather than maximising individual transactions.Always define it against transactional marketing. In gap 1 the failure is chasing new customers over current ones. is to prevent one unfortunate (mis)encounter from destroying a mutually valued long-term relationship.

Jan Carlzon (SAS) used the metaphor to transform SAS from an operations-driven business into a customer-driven airline: 10 million customers × 5 employees × 15 seconds = 50 million "moments of truth" a year, the moments that ultimately determine whether SAS succeeds or fails.

2 · Levels of customer contact ✱✱✱must know

Figure 2.7 groups services into three levels of customer contact, representing how much customers interact with service personnel, physical service elements, or both. Contact level is really a spectrum, but it is useful to examine the high and low ends.

LevelWhat it meansExamples
High contactInteraction throughout delivery; exposure to the provider is physical and tangible. Emphasizes encounters with service personnel.Nursing home · haircut · four-star hotel · good restaurant · airline travel
Medium contactPartial interaction: the customer may drop off, collect, or meet the provider briefly, without being present throughout.Retail banking · budget hotel · dry cleaning · car repair · movie theater · management consulting
Low contactLittle if any physical contact; contact at arm's length through electronic or physical distribution channels. Emphasizes encounters with physical service elements.Telephone banking · insurance · cable TV · internet banking · mail-based repairs · internet-based services
High-contact services
Customers enter a service "factory": a motel is a lodging factory, a hospital a health treatment factory, an airplane a flying transportation factory, a restaurant a food service factory. Because each industry "processes" people rather than inanimate objects, the marketing challenge is making the experience appealing in terms of both the physical environment and interactions with personnel. Customers are exposed to many physical clues: exterior and interior of buildings, equipment and furnishings, appearance and behavior of personnel, and other customers.
Low-contact services
Contact takes place at arm's length through electronic or physical distribution channels, a fast-growing trend in a convenience-oriented society. Many high- and medium-contact services are transforming into low-contact ones as customers undertake more self-service, transact by mail, telephone and internet, or buy information-based services from websites rather than brick-and-mortar facilities.
The point of the chart

Traditional retail banking, person-to-person phone banking and internet banking deliver the same basic transactions, such as a funds transfer, yet sit in very different parts of the chart. Contact level is a design choice, not a property of the service itself. The same customer may alternate between high- and low-contact channels of one provider, and the nature of their encounters varies accordingly.

3 · The servuction system

Coined by Pierre Eiglier and Eric Langeard (service + production) to describe that part of the service organization's physical environment that is visible to and experienced by customers. It consists of a technical coreThe back-stage part of the operation where inputs are processed and the service is produced.Customers do not care what happens here until it leaks forward, which is why the misread kitchen order is the textbook illustration., invisible to the customer, and a service delivery systemThe visible front stage where the service is assembled and handed over: premises, equipment, staff, other customers.High-contact services have a large one; low-contact services push nearly everything backstage. How much is visible is the design lever., visible to and experienced by the customer.

Technical core
Where inputs are processed and the elements of the service product are created, such as the restaurant kitchen. Typically back-stage (back office) and invisible. What goes on back-stage usually does not interest customers, but if it affects front-stage quality they notice: a kitchen that reads orders wrongly upsets diners.
Service delivery system
Where the final "assembly" takes place and the product is delivered, the visible part of the operations system: buildings, equipment, personnel, and possibly other customers. The front-stage (front office) is like a live theater where the experience is staged.

The model also shows Customer A and Customer B interacting: other customers present during the encounter can create value (a pleasant environment, interesting people) or destroy it (someone blocking your view in a movie theatre). Firms have to "engineer" all interactions.

The proportion visible to customers varies with contact level: high-contact services have a substantial visible component and many moments of truth to manage; low-contact services keep most of the operations back-stage, with front-stage limited to mail and telecommunications.

4 · Theater as metaphor for service delivery

Because service delivery is a series of events customers experience as a performance, theater is a good metaphor, particularly for high-contact providers and businesses serving many people at once.

  • Service facilities are the stage on which the drama unfolds. The setting can change from one act to another: airline passengers move from terminal entrance to check-in to boarding gate to aircraft. Some stages have minimal props (a taxi), others elaborate ones (resort hotels).
  • Personnel are the cast, playing roles as actors in a drama, supported by a back-stage production team. Costumes matter: hotel doormen's uniforms, UPS brown, Disney's grooming standards and its rule against beards except in costumed roles.

5 · Role and script theories

The servuction model is static: it describes a single encounter, one moment of truth. Real service processes are a series of encounters (reserving → checking in → flying → collecting bags), so role and script theories are needed to understand, design and manage both employee and customer behavior.

Role theory
Grove and Fisk define a role as "a set of behavior patterns learned through experience and communication, to be performed by an individual in a certain social interaction in order to attain maximum effectiveness in goal accomplishment." Satisfaction and productivity depend on role congruenceThe degree to which each party performs the role the encounter expects of them.Grove and Fisk. It cuts both ways: a customer who will not play their role damages quality as surely as an employee who will not., the extent to which each person acts out his or her prescribed role. Customers too must "play by the rules."
Script theory
A service scriptThe learned sequence of actions staff and customers follow through an encounter.Why switching providers feels costly: an unfamiliar script is a genuine barrier. Useful in a loyalty question. specifies the sequence of behavior employees and customers are expected to learn and follow. Employees receive formal training; customers learn scripts through experience, communication and designed education. The more experience, the more familiar the script, and unwillingness to learn a new script can be a reason not to switch providers. Deviations frustrate both sides.

Tightly scripted dramas (formal upscale restaurants) reduce variability and ensure uniform quality; scripts are more flexible for highly customized services: designers, educators, consultants. The dental example (Figure 2.9) scripts 31 steps across three players: patient, receptionist and dental hygienist.

The two complement each other: role theory says what a professor and a student are; script theory says what specific actions each takes and in what order. Excellent service marketers proactively define, communicate and train employees and customers in both.

Stage 3 · Post-encounter

Customers evaluate the service performance they experienced and compare it with their prior expectations.

Two steps: evaluation of service performance and future intentions. Nothing new is introduced here, which is the point. The comparison standard is the predicted service level formed back in pre-purchase, so this stage is where the chain that started with attributes finally pays out as satisfaction, loyalty or switching.

Satisfaction is an attitude-like judgment following a consumption experience. The essential determinant is the confirmation or disconfirmation of preconsumption expectations, measured against the predicted service level:

Positive disconfirmation
Service is better than expected.
Simple confirmation
Service is as expected.
Negative disconfirmation
Service is worse than expected.

Customers are reasonably satisfied as long as perceived performance falls within the zone of toleranceThe band between desired and adequate service within which variation passes unnoticed.Widens and narrows by customer and occasion. Price narrows it sharply. A premium fare buys a much thinner zone., above the adequate service level. As perceptions approach or exceed desired levels customers are very pleased, leading to repeat purchase, loyalty and positive word of mouth. Falling short leads to complaints, suffering in silence, or switching providers.

Are expectations always the right comparison standard?

No. In noncompetitive markets, or where switching costs are prohibitive, defining satisfaction against prior expectations is risky: if expectations are low and delivery meets that dismal level, customers hardly feel they received good quality. Better then to use needs or wants as the comparison standard. With high-credence services, customers use tangible cues and experience factors as proxies, producing a halo effectLetting one attribute the customer can judge colour their assessment of the quality they cannot.Why hospital food ratings track perceived clinical quality: customers judge what they are able to, then generalise. where perceptions of quality are driven by process and tangibles rather than core attributes.

Customer delight

Oliver, Rust and Varki: delight is a function of three components: (1) unexpectedly high levels of performance, (2) arousal (surprise, excitement) and (3) positive affect (pleasure, joy, happiness). High satisfaction alone is only positively disconfirmed expectations plus positive affect. Delight therefore requires focusing on what is currently unexpected, more than the "zero defects" strategy of just avoiding problems.

The caution: once customers have been delighted, their expectations are raised, and it takes more effort to delight them next time. Fornell and colleagues warn that continually trying to exceed expectations reaches the point of diminishing returns.

Recall in one line each
  • Three stages: pre-purchase → service encounter → post-encounter.
  • Attributes: search (before) · experience (during) · credence (never fully).
  • Three levels of customer contact: high · medium · low; personnel at one end, physical elements at the other.
  • Seven risks: functional, financial, temporal, physical, psychological, social, sensory.
  • Expectations: desired · adequate · predicted, with the zone of tolerance between desired and adequate.
  • Encounter cluster: moments of truth · high/low contact · servuction system · theater metaphor · role and script theories.
  • Servuction = technical core (back-stage, invisible) + service delivery system (front-stage, visible) + Customer A and B.
  • Satisfaction = confirmation/disconfirmation; delight = high performance + arousal + positive affect.
