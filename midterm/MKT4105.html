Development and Role of Selling in Marketing

Slides: Sales Management Module 1 · Book: Jobber and Lancaster, Chapter 1

This chapter sets up the whole course. It asks what selling is, what a modern salesperson actually does all day, what makes someone good at it, and where selling sits inside marketing. Get these ideas straight and the later chapters (planning, buyers, settings, international) are applications of them.

What is selling

The slides define selling as responding to a consumer's needs and wants through planned, personalized communication in order to influence purchase decisions and ensure satisfaction. It is worth reading that slowly, because each word is doing work. Selling is not "talking people into things." It starts from a need the customer already has, and it ends with the customer being satisfied, not just having paid.

Planned
The salesperson prepares. They learn the product, the customer and the likely questions before the conversation, rather than improvising.
Personalized
Selling is one-to-one. Unlike an advertisement, the message is adjusted to the person in front of you, in real time.
Influence
The salesperson helps the customer decide. That is a legitimate influence: giving information, answering objections, showing how the product solves the problem.
Satisfaction
The sale is not the end. A satisfied customer comes back and tells others; a dissatisfied one does neither. So the point of selling is repeat business.

The book adds the history. Selling used to be seen as a low-status job for smooth talkers, and the "hard sell" gave it a bad name. That image is out of date. Modern selling is a professional activity: salespeople are trained, work with customer data, and are judged on the long-term value of the relationships they build rather than the number of orders they take this week.

Characteristics of modern selling

Figure 1.1 in the book lists six things a modern salesperson does. Notice that "take the order" is not one of them. The order is the result; these six are the work.

Customer retention and deletion
Most companies earn most of their sales from a small number of customers, so keeping those customers is worth more than finding new ones. At the same time, some customers cost more to serve than they bring in. Modern salespeople are expected to know which is which, to invest in the valuable ones, and to let the unprofitable ones go, often to a cheaper channel such as telemarketing.
Database and knowledge management
The salesperson works from a customer database: who bought what, when, what they asked about, what went wrong. Knowledge is stored and shared, not kept in one person's head.
Customer relationship management
The job is to manage a relationship over years, not to win a transaction. That means knowing the customer's business, anticipating needs, and being the person they call first.
Marketing the product
Salespeople now take part in marketing decisions: which segments to target, how to position the product, what the competition is doing. They are marketing's eyes and ears in the field.
Problem solving and system selling
Rather than selling a product, the salesperson diagnoses the customer's problem and puts together a solution, often a system of several products and services that work together. A company buying a computer network is buying hardware, software, installation, training and support as one solution.
Adding value / satisfying needs
Find out what the customer needs first, then show how the offer meets that need. The salesperson adds value by making the customer's decision easier and better.

Success factors for salespeople and sales managers

The book's Table 1.2 ranks ten factors that make a salesperson successful. The ranking matters: listening comes first, and closing, the skill people associate most with selling, comes ninth. Good selling is mostly finding out what the customer wants.

  1. Listening skills. Hearing what the customer actually needs, not what you expected them to say.
  2. Follow-up skills. Doing what you promised after the meeting: sending the quote, checking the delivery, calling back.
  3. Ability to adapt sales style from situation to situation. A hospital buyer and a corner-shop owner need different approaches.
  4. Tenacity: sticking to the task. Most sales take several contacts. Giving up after the first no loses the sale.
  5. Organisational skills. Planning calls, territories and time so that effort goes where the business is.
  6. Verbal communication skills. Explaining clearly, in the customer's language.
  7. Proficiency in interacting with people at all levels of an organisation. From the receptionist who controls access to the director who signs.
  8. Demonstrated ability to overcome objections. Treating "it is too expensive" as a request for information, not a refusal.
  9. Closing skills. Knowing when and how to ask for the order.
  10. Personal planning and time management skills. Time with customers is the scarce resource; everything else should protect it.

The slides add a memory aid from a Forbes article, S E A. Sincerity: listen without an agenda; the conversation is about the customer's needs, not yours. Ethics: do not try to talk someone into something; listen to what they actually want. Asking: serve the customer by asking questions that help them make a wise buying decision. Win-win relationships are built by remembering that it is about what the other person wants.

The product life-cycle

One marketing idea matters so much for selling that the slides carry the figure. Most products follow a pattern over time in sales and profit, the product life-cycleThe pattern most products follow: introduction, growth, maturity, decline.Profit turns positive after introduction, peaks in maturity before sales do, and shrinks under competition. Advertising matters most early; personal selling matters more as the market matures., and what the salesforce should do changes with the stage.

Introduction
Sales grow slowly. Dealers must be persuaded to stock the product, customers to notice and try it, sometimes to learn how to use it (the microwave oven changed how people cooked). Launch costs are heavy, so the product usually makes a loss.
Growth
Sales take off as word of mouth and advertising work. Dealers now ask to stock it. Profits appear, and a new product can charge high initial prices, which the book calls market skimmingLaunching at a high price to take the maximum profit early, then lowering it.Works in the growth stage when a new product can command a premium..
Maturity
The market is saturated. Few new buyers remain; most sales are repeat purchases. Competitors have arrived, attracted by the profits, and profit peaks and then starts to fall even while sales are still high.
Decline
Sales fall, margins are squeezed further, customers move to newer products, and dealers begin to destock.

Sales Strategies: The Place of Selling in the Marketing Plan

Slides: Sales Management Module 2 · Book: Jobber and Lancaster, Chapter 2

Chapter 1 said that marketing strategy sets the direction for selling. This chapter is about how that strategy is made, what the sales function contributes to making it, and how a sentence in a boardroom plan becomes something a salesperson does on Monday morning. The book's opening point: sales activities only work inside an overall strategic marketing plan. Without one, sales effort competes with the rest of marketing instead of complementing it.

GOSPA

GOSPAGoals, Objectives, Strategies, Projects, Actions: a planning method that links what the organisation wants to what each person does today.Each level is the means to the one above it. The slides' version of the book's MOST. is a planning and performance-management method. Its purpose is alignment: making sure that what the organisation wants at the top is connected, step by step, to what each team does day to day. It stands for Goals, Objectives, Strategies, Projects, Actions, and it is read from the top down. Each level answers "how?" for the level above it.

Goals
Long-term aspirations that define where the organisation wants to be. Broad and directional: “become the leading supplier of packaging in South Asia.”
Objectives
Specific, measurable targets that translate the goal into outcomes you can check. “Grow packaging revenue 20 percent in the next financial year.” If you cannot measure it, it is a goal, not an objective.
Strategies
The approaches chosen to achieve the objectives and the way resources are allocated between them. “Win the ten largest garment exporters as accounts.”
Projects
Structured initiatives that turn a strategy into deliverable pieces of work with owners and deadlines. “Build a key-account team of four by March.”
Actions
The day-to-day tasks that move projects forward: the calls made, the proposals written, the visits completed.

The value of the framework is that any action can be traced upward. If a salesperson cannot say which project, strategy and objective a task serves, the task probably should not be done. The book's own version is MOST: mission, objective, strategy, tactics, again from the general to the specific.

The planning process book only

The book explains planning with a central heating system. You set the temperature you want (objectives). You decide what has to happen to reach it (operations). You install and arrange what is needed (organisation). You switch it on (implementation). You read the thermometer (measuring results against standards). And when the room is too hot or too cold, the thermostat corrects it (re-evaluation and control). Planning is a loop, not a line: the last step feeds the first.

Building a marketing plan follows a set of logical steps, even though every real situation is different.

Business definition
First ask “what business are we in?” and answer in terms of the customer needs served, not the products made. Volvo is in the business of family transport; BMW of fast transport that also confers status. A microcomputer maker might be in rapid problem-solving. Defining the business this way forces the whole plan to start from customers, and it is closely tied to branding.
Situation analysis, or marketing audit
A thorough review of where the company stands. The internal audit looks at the size and growth of the market, customer needs and buying trends, the current marketing mix, and competitors (their strategy, performance, strengths, weaknesses and likely next moves, plus potential new entrants). The external audit scans the wider environment with PESTPolitical, Economic, Socio-cultural, Technological: the four broad outside forces a firm scans.Extended to SLEPT (Legal), PESTLE (Environmental) and STEEPLE (Ecological). Same tool, longer list.: political, economic, socio-cultural and technological forces. The list has grown over the years: SLEPT added legal factors, PESTLE added environmental ones, STEEPLE added ecological ones. Both audits need top management's backing, because they feed corporate planning as well as marketing planning.
SWOT
Strengths and weaknesses (internal) and opportunities and threats (external), pulled together from the audits. The TOWS matrix turns them into actions: use strengths to seize opportunities, fix weaknesses that expose you to threats.
Objectives, strategies, tactics
The plan then runs as a hierarchy. Objectives say where the firm intends to go. Strategies say how it intends to get there; several may be evaluated for each objective but only one is used. Tactics say the precise route: what specifically will be done. One strategy usually has several tactics.

Advertising and selling are complementary

A common view among sales managers, especially in industrial markets, is that money spent on advertising is money that should have gone to the salesforce. The evidence contradicts it. Advertising and selling do different jobs, and the relationship should be coordinated, not competitive.

What advertising does for the salesperson in an industrial market: it builds the corporate reputation and the product's credibility, so the salesperson does not arrive as a stranger; it creates awareness among prospective clients, which makes the first call easier; it explains a new product's features before the sales contact, so the visit can go straight to the customer's questions; and it generates leads through return coupons and other response mechanisms, so the salesperson calls on people who have already shown interest.

The book's numbers make the case. Given adequate frequency, advertising can reduce the overall cost of selling to customers who have seen it by up to 30 percent. Conversely, a company that does not advertise, selling to customers who have seen its competitors' advertising, may find its selling costs raised by as much as 40 percent. The practical rule: salespeople should use the advertising themes in their own presentations, so the customer hears one consistent message, instead of treating advertising as a rival for budget.

Push and pull book only

The book's chapter also covers the promotional mix, the blend of advertising, personal selling, sales promotion and public relations a company uses, and what decides the balance: the type of product market, the step the buyer has reached in the buying process, the stage in the product life-cycle, and whether the company runs a push or a pull strategyAiming marketing effort at the final consumer so they ask for the product, and demand pulls it back through the channel.Relies on advertising. Consumers ask retailers, retailers ask wholesalers, wholesalers ask the producer.. Push and pull come back in Chapter 4 under sales promotions, so learn them here.

A push strategy aims the marketing effort at the channel of distribution. The idea is that if wholesalers and retailers can be persuaded to stock the product, they will in turn make sure it is brought to the final customer's attention. So a push strategy leans heavily on personal selling and trade promotion: salespeople calling on the trade, discounts and incentives for stocking.

A pull strategy aims at the final consumer instead, relying on advertising to create awareness and interest. If enough consumer demand is created, consumers ask retailers for the product, retailers ask wholesalers, and wholesalers ask the producer. The product is pulled through the channel by demand at the far end. Most companies use both, in different proportions; a new consumer brand launched with heavy television advertising is running pull, while the salesforce persuading supermarkets to give it shelf space is running push.

Planning works when a broad goal is translated into a clear chain: objective → strategy → tactics. The objective says what outcome is wanted. The strategy says the approach. Consumer and Organisational Buyer Behaviour

Slides: Chapter 3 · Book: Jobber and Lancaster, Chapter 3

You cannot sell well without knowing how the buyer decides. A salesperson who understands the steps a customer goes through, who else is involved, and what the customer is weighing can put the right information in front of the right person at the right moment. Consumers and organisations decide differently, so the chapter treats them separately, then looks at what has changed in how organisations buy, and finishes with relationship management.

How organisational buying differs from consumer buying

An organisational buyer is a person buying on behalf of a company, hospital, school or government department. The slides list eight differences from a consumer buying for themselves. Each one changes what the salesperson has to do.

Fewer organisational buyers
A handful of organisations may make up most of a market. In the UK, a few supermarket chains buy most of the groceries sold, so losing one account can be a disaster. Each customer gets far more individual attention than any consumer would.
Close, long-term relationships
Buyer and seller work together over years, not one transaction. Suppliers may be involved in designing the buyer's product, and switching is expensive for both sides. Selling becomes managing a relationship.
Organisational buyers are more rational
Organisations buy on performance, cost and delivery, and often have to justify the decision to others. Emotion and personal liking still enter, but the sales argument has to be built on economic criteria.
Buying may be to specific requirements
Products are often made to the buyer's specification, especially in industrial markets. The salesperson may need to bring engineers to the meeting and to negotiate what the product will be, not just its price.
Reciprocal buying
“I buy from you if you buy from me.” A car maker may buy steel from a company that buys its trucks. Rare among consumers, common between firms, and it can shut out a supplier with a better product.
Organisational selling and buying may be more risky
Contracts are large and a wrong decision can stop a production line or cost the buyer their job. Buyers therefore look for ways to reduce risk: a supplier's reputation, references, trial orders.
Organisational buying is more complex
Several people take part, over several stages, following formal procedures such as tenders and approved-supplier lists. The salesperson has to find out who those people are.
Negotiation is often more important
Price, delivery, credit and specification are argued rather than read off a label, because the sums are large enough to be worth arguing over.

Two organisational tools the slides define belong to this rational way of buying. Value analysisA systematic review of design, production and purchasing to cut a product's total cost without hurting its function.Cheaper components, standard parts, less cycle time, automation. is a systematic review of how a product is designed, made and bought in order to cut its total cost: designing to looser tolerances where they do not matter, switching to lower-cost components, standardising parts across product platforms, shortening production cycle time, and automating to strip labour cost out. A buyer who thinks in life-cycle costs will accept a higher price for a machine that is cheaper to run, and a salesperson who can show that arithmetic sells the dearer machine.

Consumer buyer behaviour

An understanding of customers can only be obtained by answering five questions: who is important in the buying decision, how do they buy, what are their choice criteriaThe factors a buyer uses to judge and compare alternatives.Economic (performance, reliability, price), social (status, belonging) and personal (how the product fits the buyer psychologically)., where do they buy, and when do they buy. The rest of the consumer half of the chapter answers the first three.

Who: the buying centre

Even a consumer purchase can involve several people. The book's example is a family buying a car: the child who wants one starts the process, a friend who knows about cars influences the choice, one parent decides, another may pay, and the whole family uses it. The people involved form a buying centreEveryone in an organisation who takes part in a buying decision. Also called the buying centre.Bonoma and Webster's six roles: initiators, users, deciders, influencers, buyers, gatekeepers., and each plays one or more roles.

Initiator
The person who begins the process of considering a purchase. Information may be gathered by this person to help the decision.
Influencer
The person who attempts to persuade others in the group concerning the outcome of the decision. Influencers typically gather information and try to impose their choice criteria.
Decider
The individual with the power and/or financial authority to make the ultimate choice.
Buyer
The person who conducts the transaction: calls the supplier, visits the store, makes the payment and effects delivery.
User
The actual consumer or user of the product.

The book's warning is that the salesperson must identify the roles and sell to each accordingly. A car salesperson who spends the whole visit talking to the teenager who wants the car, while the parent who will pay stands to one side unconvinced, has talked to the initiator and ignored the decider.

How: the decision process

Need identification / problem awareness
The consumer notices a gap between where they are and where they want to be: the old car keeps breaking down, or a friend's new phone shows what theirs cannot do. The salesperson can help here by pointing out a problem the customer had not noticed, or the cost of leaving it.
Information gathering
The consumer looks for ways to solve the problem: talking to friends, reading reviews, visiting shops, listening to salespeople. The more expensive and unfamiliar the purchase, the longer this stage lasts. The salesperson's job is to be a trusted, useful source.
Evaluation of alternative solutions
The options are compared on the consumer's evaluative criteria (see Figure 3.2 below). The salesperson helps the customer compare, and makes sure the criteria on which their product wins are in the customer's mind.
Selection of an appropriate solution
The consumer chooses. This is where perceived risk is highest and where guarantees, trial periods and reassurance matter most.
Post-purchase evaluation of the decision
After buying, the consumer judges whether it was the right choice. If the product falls short of expectations they feel cognitive dissonance, the discomfort of doubting a decision already made. Follow-up calls and good service reduce it and win the next sale.

Between evaluation and selection sits the evaluation system, Figure 3.2. It explains how a comparison turns into a decision.

Take a consumer choosing a car. Their evaluative criteria are the things they judge on: fuel economy, safety, price, looks. About each car they form beliefs: "this one is safe but thirsty." The beliefs, weighted by how much each criterion matters to them, add up to an attitude toward each car, an overall liking or disliking. The strongest attitude becomes an intention to buy. A salesperson can work on any link in the chain: change a belief (show the safety test results), change the weight of a criterion (stress that safety matters more than looks when children are in the car), or add a criterion the customer had not considered.

What criteria

Choice criteria are the various attributes and benefits a consumer uses when evaluating products and services. They can be economic, social or personal.

Economic criteria
Performance, reliability, price, running costs. What the product does and what it costs.
Social criteria
Status and the need for social belonging. A car is chosen partly for what it says about its owner to other people.
Personal criteria
How the product or service relates to the individual psychologically. Does it fit their self-image? Does it make them feel a certain way?

The salesperson needs to find out which criteria this customer is using and sell on those, not on the ones the salesperson happens to find interesting. Three groups of factors affect how the process runs and which criteria dominate: the buying situation, personal influences and social influences.

Social class
Groupings of people by occupation, income and education that tend to share tastes and buying patterns. The book notes that the link between class and buying is weaker than it was, but still useful.

Organisational buyer behaviour

To understand how an organisation buys, the book says you need to answer three questions, and it uses them to organise the whole topic.

Structure: who takes part

An essential point is that the buyer, or purchasing officer, is often not the only person who influences the decision, or who actually has the authority to make the ultimate decision. The people involved form the decision-making unit, or DMU. The first five match the consumer buying centre. The sixth, the gatekeeper, is new: someone who controls the flow of information, such as a secretary who decides which salespeople get an appointment, or a purchasing manager who decides which suppliers' brochures reach the engineers. A salesperson who cannot get past the gatekeeper never reaches the decider, and one who mistakes the buyer for the decider may win the buyer over and still lose the order.

Process: the buy phases

The pattern of information getting, analysis, evaluation and decision-making is the organisational decision-making process. Robinson, Faris and Wind described it as seven buy phases.

1 Recognition of a problem (need)
Something inside or outside the organisation signals a need: a machine breaks down, a new product needs a new component, a salesperson points out a better way. Suppliers who help the buyer see the problem get in early.
2 Determination of characteristics, specification and quantity
The organisation works out what will solve the problem and how much of it is needed. In a technical purchase the specification is written here, and a supplier who helps write it often finds the specification suits their product.
3 Search for and qualification of potential sources
The buyer looks for suppliers who could meet the specification and screens out those who cannot. Reputation, size, location and past dealings decide who makes the list.
4 Acquisition and analysis of proposals
Qualified suppliers are asked to quote or tender. The proposals are compared on price, quality, delivery and service.
5 Evaluation of proposals and selection of supplier(s)
The DMU chooses. Many organisations deliberately choose more than one supplier so that they are not dependent on one.
6 Selection of an order routine
The details of how the order will be placed, delivered, invoiced and paid are settled. Routine to the buyer, but a chance for the supplier to make itself easy to deal with.
7 Performance feedback and evaluation
The buyer checks whether the product and the supplier performed as promised. A good evaluation is what turns a new task into a straight re-buy next time.

The first is creeping commitmentThe way a buying organisation becomes gradually tied to one supplier through that supplier's early involvement and technical help.Get in at phase one and the later phases lean your way.: as the buying organisation moves through the phases it becomes increasingly committed to a particular supplier through that supplier's involvement in the process and the technical assistance it provides. A salesperson who helps write the specification in phase 2 is very hard to remove by phase 5. The lesson is to get in early, because a supplier who arrives at phase 4 is competing on price against a rival who has already shaped the decision. The second is the financial leaseA long-term lease that pays off the whole value of the asset over the contract; total payments usually exceed the purchase price.Contrast an operating lease, which is shorter and cancellable., one of the order routines a buyer may choose: a longer-term arrangement that is fully amortised over the term of the contract, with lease payments that in total usually exceed the purchase price of the item. It spreads the cost and can make an expensive purchase affordable, which is why salespeople offer it.

Content: the choice criteria

This aspect of organisational buyer behaviour refers to the choice criteria used by members of the DMU to evaluate supplier proposals. The slides list six.

Quality
Does the product meet the specification, consistently? Many buyers now demand supplier quality certification such as ISO 9000 before they will even consider a proposal.
Price and life-cycle costs
Not the sticker price alone but what the product will cost to own and run over its life. A cheaper machine that breaks down more often is dearer.
Continuity of supply
Can this supplier deliver reliably, every time? A stopped production line costs far more than a slightly higher price, so buyers value dependability and often split orders to be safe.
Perceived risk
How badly could this go wrong, and how would it look? Buyers avoid decisions that could embarrass them, which favours known suppliers over unknown ones with better products.
Office politics
Internal rivalries may affect who supports which supplier. A proposal backed by one department may be opposed by another for reasons that have nothing to do with the product.
Personal liking or disliking
Buyers are people. They prefer to deal with salespeople they like and trust, and that preference tips close decisions.

The last two are why "organisations are rational" is only mostly true. The salesperson's task is to find out which criteria matter to which DMU member and to show how the proposal meets them, in the buyer's own terms.

Factors affecting organisational buying

Three factors shape how an organisation buys in a given case, and therefore how the salesperson should approach it. Figure 3.6 puts them together.

The buy class

Buy classes are buying situations distinguished by four characteristics: newness of the problem to the decision-makers, the number of alternatives to be considered, the uncertainty inherent in the buying situation, and the amount of information needed to make a buying decision. Robinson, Faris and Wind identified three.

New task purchase
A buying situation in which the customer has no previous experience with the purchase of a product within a given category. The problem is new, alternatives are many, uncertainty is high and a lot of information is needed. The customer buys the same goods on the same terms from the same supplier, routinely. Little information is needed and the decision may be automatic. Products can be classified according to four types. Materials to be used in the production process, such as steel, and components to be incorporated in the finished product, such as an alternator, are product constituents: they become part of what the organisation sells. The importance of the purchase

A purchase is likely to be perceived as important to the buying organisation when it involves large sums of money, when the cost of making the wrong decision (for example lost production) is high, and when there is considerable uncertainty about the outcome of alternative offerings. The more important the purchase, the more people are involved, the longer it takes, and the more the salesperson must do to reduce the buyer's sense of risk.

Sales Settings

Slides: Chapter 4 · Book: Jobber and Lancaster, Chapter 4

A sales setting is the situation you sell in: who the customer is, what is being sold, and through what route. The same salesperson would work very differently selling steel to a factory, biscuits to a supermarket chain, or insurance to a family. The chapter starts with the forces changing every setting, then walks through the settings themselves (channels, industrial, resale, services), then the activities that support selling: sales promotions, exhibitions and public relations.

Environmental and managerial forces that impact on sales

The book groups the forces changing selling into three: behavioural (what customers do), technological (what tools exist) and managerial (how companies respond). Together they explain why the salesperson's job has moved from taking orders to managing customers.

Behavioural forces

Rising consumer and organisational buyer expectations
Customers keep raising the bar on quality, customer service and value. Sales efforts must be fine-tuned with continuous improvement, because what delighted a customer last year is expected this year. Technological advances have created new higher customer expectations, and salespeople must work cross-functionally to meet them. After a run of corporate scandals, customers also require more transparency, and companies must work toward ethical selling.
Customer avoidance of buyer-seller negotiations
Many customers dislike haggling with a salesperson. Some companies train their salesforce as negotiators with high-pressure selling, and the result is displeasure. Others use the opposite approach: fixed prices, no pressure and full book value for the trade-in. Saturn and Daewoo are the examples.
Expanding power of major buyers
A growing dominance of major players, especially retailers, means a few customers can dictate terms. They demand and get special services: key account management, just-in-time inventory control, category managementManaging a whole product category, not single brands, to maximise the retailer's return from the shelf.Big retailers demand it from suppliers. (the retailer and supplier jointly managing a whole product category on the shelf) and joint funding of promotions.
Globalisation of markets
Companies now sell in many countries, and the major challenge is the coordination of global sales teams that require special terms of sale, technical support, pricing and customisation of products. Cultural and ethical issues must be considered. Coca-Cola, Colgate-Palmolive, Avon, 3M, P&G, Samsung and Siemens are the examples.
Fragmentation of markets
Driven by differences in income levels, lifestyles, personalities, experiences and race, markets are fragmenting to form market segments. One product and one pitch no longer fit everybody, so the salesforce needs different offers for different segments.

The Internet, e-commerce and teleshopping through cable television. Routine orders that once needed a salesperson's visit are placed online, freeing the salesforce for the sales that need a person.

Managerial forces

Employing direct marketing techniques
Computer stations (kiosks) have reduced the need for a salesforce in some settings, and telemarketing and direct marketing are now used as selling strategies alongside or instead of field sales.

Use of intranets between employees, suppliers and customers enhances the exchange of information, and updates the field salesforce about product specifications, competitor news and price updates. It also links sales and marketing, which Chapter 2 showed is where many companies go wrong.

The Chartered Institute of Marketing (CIM) offers qualifications at introductory, professional and postgraduate levels with the aim of enhancing sales professionalism, skills and competencies. Selling is becoming a profession with credentials.

Protecting the goods in transit and storage, and making them easy to handle, count and display.

Appraising sales channels

When selecting or reappraising channels, the company must take into account seven factors.

The market
The market must have the potential for purchase. Channel compatibility with similar products and norms in the marketplace is important, because buyers expect to find a product where they usually look for it. And the company must maintain the status and image associated with the channels: a luxury brand in a discount store damages itself.
Channel costs
Generally, short channels are the costliest, because the manufacturer takes on transport, warehousing and salesforce management. But a short channel increases market share and profits, and provides more effective control over how the product is sold.
The product
Longer channels are used for low-cost, low-technology items with narrow product lines, where no single manufacturer can afford to reach every small outlet. Shorter channels suit complex products with wider product lines, where the customer needs advice and the manufacturer needs control. Complementary products can share a channel.
Profit potential
A balance must be struck between channel expense and gross margin. Shorter channels have higher profit margins but channel costs will be higher, and vice versa.
Channel structure
The choice of distributive intermediaries is governed by the members and characteristics (size and so on) in that channel. The difficulty or ease of entry to channels determines whether a push or a pull strategy is needed: if retailers will not stock the product, the manufacturer must create consumer demand to pull it in.
Product life-cycle
New products need intensive distribution to get noticed; an established product with after-sales service needs moves to selective distribution.
Non-marketing factors
Mainly the amount of finance available. Innovative products are often sold through middlemen because the firm cannot afford its own channel, and firms selling abroad often use a foreign agency for the same reason, without its commercial standing being the point.

Whatever channel is chosen, a manufacturer also chooses how widely to distribute. There are four types.

Direct
The manufacturer does not use a middleman and sells and delivers direct to the end customer.
Selective
The manufacturer sells through a limited number of middlemen who are chosen because of special abilities or facilities to enable the product to be better marketed. Cars, where the dealer must be able to service them.
Intensive
Maximum exposure at the point of sale is needed and the manufacturer sells through as many outlets as possible. Confectionery, soft drinks, newspapers.
Exclusive
The manufacturer sells to a restricted number of dealers, often one per area, in return for the dealer's full effort. Luxury watches, some fashion brands.

Industrial, commercial and public authority selling

A number of characteristics in these types of markets distinguish them from consumer markets: fewer customers, concentrated markets (often geographically, around an industry), complex purchasing decisions and reciprocal trading. Chapter 3 covered the buying side. What the seller must also understand is the customer's type of production, because it decides what is bought, in what quantities and how often.

Job (unit or project) production
An item is produced or constructed to individual customer requirements: ships, tailor-made suits, a hospital. Every purchase is a new task, so buying is careful and slow and the salesperson works closely with the customer's designers.
Batch production
A number of products or components are made at the same time, but not on a continuous basis: books, furniture, clothes. Buying is periodic and tied to the batch schedule.
Flow (mass or line) production
Continuous production of identical or similar products in anticipation of sales: motor cars, washing machines. Materials and components are bought in large regular quantities, and continuity of supply is everything.
Process (continuous) production
The production unit has raw materials coming into the manufacturing process and a finished product emerging at the other end: chemicals, brewing, plastics. The plant never stops, so a missed delivery is very costly and supplier reliability is the main criterion.

Selling services

In Britain the service sector has grown tremendously over recent years, so much so that it is now primarily a service rather than a manufacturing economy. Public services have become more marketing orientated and accountable. Due to the abstract nature of services they are hard to sell, so service providers must be highly trained in sales techniques and negotiations, the image of the service organisation is also important, and trust is essential in services marketing. You cannot inspect a haircut or a holiday before you buy it, so you buy the provider's reputation.

The book gives five special characteristics of services, each of which makes selling harder.

Intangibility
A service cannot be seen, touched or tried before purchase. The salesperson must make it concrete through evidence: testimonials, guarantees, demonstrations, the look of the premises.
Simultaneously produced and consumed
A service is made and used at the same moment, in the customer's presence. The person delivering it is part of the product, which is why frontline staff matter so much.
Not standardised
Two haircuts, two consultations or two flights are never identical, because people deliver them. Quality varies, and the seller must manage that variation.
Cannot be stocked
An unsold seat or an empty hotel room cannot be put in a warehouse for tomorrow. Selling has to match demand to capacity now.
Perishable
The same point from the other side: once the moment has passed the service is lost. Hence last-minute discounts and off-peak pricing.

Because of these characteristics, services marketing adds three Ps to the usual four (product, price, place, promotion).

People
Frontline people are the backbone of a service. Their selection, training and attitude are the product.
Process
Consistency and quality must be well planned and managed, because they cannot be inspected into the service afterwards.
Physical evidence
The physical facilities, equipment, uniforms and materials that let the customer judge an intangible service before and while buying it.

Public relations

PR is short for public relations and refers to the strategic communication from an organisation to the public to maintain or cultivate public image and respond to public discourse. The publics are every group whose opinion matters to the organisation: the community, employees, government, the financial community, distributors, consumers, opinion leaders and the media. PR is also defined by what it is not: it is not free advertising (it is not bought space and cannot be controlled), it is not propaganda (it must be truthful) and it is not publicity (publicity is one tool it uses).

The objectives of public relations show how wide its reach is: attract sales inquiries, reinforce customer loyalty, attract investors, attract merger partners or smooth the way for acquisition, attract better employees, minimise competitor advantage while you catch up, open a new market, launch a new product, reward key people with recognition, bring about favourable legislation, change perceptions about the company, and alter the image that the company projects about itself.

Effective PR depends on three things: setting specific objectives that are capable of evaluation, fully integrating the PR function into the organisation, and selecting the right personnel to carry it out. A PR manager needs personal integrity, communication skills, organisational ability, a strong personality, team play and sound judgement. External PR can be bought from freelance writers and consultants, the PR department of an advertising agency, a PR subsidiary of an advertising agency, or independent PR consultants who usually specialise in a class of business.

Balance of payments and balance of trade

The balance of paymentsA country's record of all economic transactions with the rest of the world over a period, usually a year.Three accounts: current, capital and financial. They always sum to zero. (BOP) is a systematic record of the economic transactions between the residents of one country and the rest of the world during a specific period, usually one year. It tells us how money is coming into and going out of a country through international economic transactions. It is built from three accounts.

Current account
Transactions in goods, services, income and current transfers. Trade in goods (exports and imports of physical products), trade in services (tourism, shipping, banking), income (wages and investment income flowing in and out) and transfers (remittances from workers abroad, foreign aid).
Capital account
Capital transfers and the transfer of non-produced, non-financial assets, such as debt forgiveness or the sale of patents and land. Usually the smallest of the three.
Financial account
Records changes in ownership of national assets and liabilities: direct investment (a foreign company building a factory), portfolio investment (buying bonds and shares) and other investments (bank loans and deposits).

The three accounts always sum to zero, because every transaction has two sides. If a country buys more from abroad than it sells, the difference has to be paid for somehow, and the payment shows up as foreigners acquiring the country's assets. In the slides' example a current account deficit of $40 billion is balanced by a financial account surplus of $40 billion: the country is using capital inflows to finance its imports and investments.

The balance of tradeExports of goods minus imports of goods.A deficit is not automatically bad if the imports are machinery and technology that raise future capacity. A surplus is not automatically good if it reflects weak demand at home. is narrower: only the goods line, exports of goods minus imports of goods. A deficit means imports exceed exports; a surplus means the reverse.

Is a trade deficit always bad? No. A trade deficit may occur because the country is importing machinery, technology, raw materials and capital equipment, and these imports may increase future productive capacity: the country is buying the means to produce and export more later. Similarly, a trade surplus is not automatically good if it reflects weak domestic demand or insufficient investment, a country that exports because its own people cannot afford to buy.

Visible tradeInvisible (nonvisible) trade
Trade in physical, tangible goods: garments, cars, computers, machinery, rice, pharmaceuticals, electronicsInternational trade in services and other non-physical economic activities: banking and insurance, tourism, transportation, consultancy, IT services and telecommunications, education, software

The book adds the British context. Britain has imported more physical goods than it exported for over a century; the gap was covered by invisible exports, the earnings of the City of London, shipping and tourism. When invisibles stopped keeping pace with the visible deficit, the overall balance went into deficit. That is why the book treats selling overseas as a national as well as a company concern: every export sale helps the balance.

Absolute and comparative advantage

Why do countries trade at all, rather than each making everything it needs? Two ideas answer that. A country has an absolute advantageBeing able to produce something with fewer resources than another country.Bangladesh making 100 shirts per worker against Austria's 70. when it can produce a product using fewer resources, or at a lower cost or more efficiently, than another country. Comparative advantageBeing relatively better at something, measured by opportunity cost, even if another country is better at everything.Each country should specialise where its opportunity cost is lower; then both gain from trade. Why firms go international

Countries trade because of comparative advantage; firms go abroad for more concrete reasons. The slides give ten.

Market expansion
The domestic market may be too small. A Bangladeshi software company may expand into India, Singapore, Europe and North America because there are not enough customers at home to grow further.
Increase sales
Foreign markets provide additional customers for a product the firm already makes. The extra volume is often close to pure gain because development costs are already paid.
Increase profit
A product may have a higher profit margin in another country, because competition is weaker or customers will pay more.
Access to resources
Firms may seek raw materials, skilled labour, technology, capital or suppliers that are unavailable or expensive at home.
Lower production costs
Some countries provide lower labour costs, economies of scale from serving a bigger market, and lower input costs.
Diversification of risk
If demand falls in one country, sales in another country may continue. A firm in several markets is less exposed to one economy's recession.
Competitive pressure
A company may internationalise because competitors are already operating globally and will use their scale against it at home.
Follow customers
Suppliers often internationalise when their major customers expand overseas, so as to keep serving them. A car-parts maker follows the car maker.
Technology and knowledge
Internationalisation can provide access to new technologies, innovation and international talent.
First-mover advantage
Entering a foreign market early may allow a company to establish brand recognition, distribution networks and customer loyalty before rivals arrive.

The book's own list overlaps and adds a few. A product may simply be unavailable at home: Britain cannot grow rubber, so it imports it. Product differentiation means Britain both imports and exports cars, because buyers want different cars. Firms export to counter recession at home, to use spare production capacity, to sell products that are obsolete in the home market but wanted elsewhere (the book's example is flypaper), and to gain economies of scale that also let them cut prices at home. Underneath all of it, the strongest incentive is simply demand backed by purchasing power: somebody abroad wants the product and can pay for it.

Organisation for international selling book only

The book distinguishes three depths of international involvement. Exporting is a peripheral activity, typically under 20 percent of turnover, handled by an export department while the company stays domestic in outlook. International marketing is a strategic decision: the company changes its organisation and marketing mix for foreign markets and treats them as central. Multinational marketing means manufacturing plants and offices around the world, operating in each country as a national company: Shell, Ford, Coca-Cola, Microsoft, McDonald's. Whatever the depth, the book insists a senior manager should own exporting, or it stays a sideline.

Distributors

Specialist firms that handle the whole export process on behalf of a manufacturer who does not want to do it itself.

The company's own sales or manufacturing operation abroad. Full control, full cost, full risk.

Choosing an agent means checking when it was founded and its history, what other products it represents (competing? complementary?), the territory it covers, its standing in the trade, whether its way of working fits yours, its financial and staff resources, and its ability to provide technical and after-sales support. Once chosen the agent must be trained, visited regularly, and helped to negotiate. The book's warning is that an agent's success can be its downfall: if it builds the market well, the principal may be tempted to drop it and go direct. Fairness, and the principal's reputation with other agents, call for a long-term agreement or a run-down clause that compensates the agent. Direct selling abroad demands language and cultural understanding and patience, since in many cultures the first "no" is rarely final.

International pricing, freight and tariffs

International pricing is more complicated than domestic pricing because the final price may include production cost + shipping/freight + insurance + tariffs + taxes + distributor margins + exchange-rate effects + other costs. Although the product's original price is $100, the importer effectively faces a cost of $125 before considering any margins.

A tariff is a tax imposed by one country's government on goods and services imported from a foreign country. It increases the price of the good and encourages homegrown brands to thrive. Tariffs are calculated in three ways.

Ad valorem tariff
Value-based customs calculation: a percentage of the product's value, for example 20 percent. The more expensive the good, the more tariff it pays.
Specific tariff
Quantity-based duty application: a fixed amount per unit regardless of value, for example $10 per imported PC.
Compound tariff
Combines value and quantity: for example 10 percent of value plus $5 per unit.

Who actually pays a tariff? Follow the slides' example.

An Indian exporter sells a pair of trousers to a US importer for $10. The US government imposes a 10 percent tariff on Indian products, so the importer has to pay $10 plus $1, making the effective price $11. After adding expenses and profit the trousers are sold for $20, and a US consumer will most likely pay more for them: someone who paid $19 before now pays $20. There are two exceptional cases where the consumer does not pay. The exporter may absorb the tariff by lowering its price to keep the business, or the importer may reduce its profit margin to keep retail prices stable. In both cases the tariff is paid, just by a different link in the chain.

The graph shows the same thing for a whole market. Without a tariff the domestic price equals the world price, domestic firms supply Qs, consumers demand Qd, and the gap is filled by imports. A tariff lifts the price consumers face to the world price plus the tariff. At the higher price domestic firms supply more (Qs') and consumers buy less (Qd'), so imports shrink to the narrower gap. The shaded rectangle over the imports is the government's tariff revenue. Domestic producers gain, the government gains, and consumers lose more than the other two gain together, which is the economist's case against tariffs.

Why do governments impose them anyway? The slides draw the chain. The tariff raises the cost of imports. Consumers are put off by rising costs and import less. Domestic industries see an influx of internal producers and jobs are created, and government revenue increases. So the pros are that a tariff protects local industries in the short term, generates government revenue and gives trade leverage in negotiations. The cons are higher consumer prices, the risk of retaliatory measures from other countries, and strained trade relations. The book adds two other import controls: quotas, a numerical limit on how much may be imported, sometimes auctioned to importers, and import licences. It also notes that freight, packaging for long sea journeys and containerisation all enter the export quotation, which can be quoted anywhere from an ex-works price (at the factory gate) to delivered to the customer's door.

Transfer pricingThe price one part of a company charges another part, in another country, for goods or services.Set to move profit toward low-tax countries and minimise import duty, which is why tax authorities watch it closely. is an accounting practice that represents the price that one division in a company charges another division for goods and services provided. In the slides' example the UK parent, Software UK, provides sales, marketing, services and brand to its US subsidiary, Software US, at an agreed transfer price of total cost plus 15 percent: $1 million becomes $1,150,000. Why does it matter internationally? Because tax rates and import duties differ by country. A company can move components at low transfer prices into a country with high import duties, and book high transfer prices in a country with high corporation tax, so that profit appears where it is taxed least. Customs and tax authorities watch transfer prices closely for exactly that reason.

Selling in Japan book only

The book ends with eight rules for selling to Japanese organisations, and they double as a case study in how culture changes the sale. Describe your organisation in detail, ideally in Japanese, because the buyer wants to know who they are dealing with before what they are buying. Manage meetings Japanese style: be introduced by a mutual acquaintance, never be late, bring a small gift from your country. Recognise that decisions are made by middle management by consensus, not by the president you meet first. Do not push for a close; they will take time, and they will not say no to your face, so a vague answer usually means no. Use Japanese wherever possible. Keep presentations low key; the hard sell reads as disrespect. Build the relationship through courtesy visits and social events, because the relationship comes before the order. Dress conservatively.
