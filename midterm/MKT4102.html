The Scope and Challenge of International Marketing

Cateora, Chapter 1

This chapter answers four questions. Why does every company now have to think internationally? Why international markets matter

The book begins with a confession. Twenty-five years ago the authors predicted that the world was becoming one single economy. They did not see 9/11 coming, or SARS, or the 2008 financial crash, or Brexit, or Trump, or COVID-19, or the war in Ukraine. World trade fell 11 percent in 2009, 13 percent between 2014 and 2016, and about 10 percent in 2020.

The lesson they draw is simple: expect the unexpected. You still plan and you still forecast, but international markets will surprise you. The company that survives is the flexible one. Flexibility comes from two things: strong relationships with partners abroad, and a spread of different businesses, so that one shock does not sink everything.

Even so, four long-term trends will keep pushing business across borders, whatever the bumps along the way:

  • The growth of the World Trade OrganizationThe international organization, created in 1994, that sets trade rules for its 164 member countries, hears disputes and issues binding decisions.The key contrast with GATT: GATT was an agreement with advisory panels; the WTO is an institution whose decisions members must follow. It still has no police, but the winner of a case may hit back with sanctions. and of free trade agreements between countries. More rules, fewer barriers.
  • Developing countries in Latin America, Asia and eastern Europe accepting the free market system, so more places are open for business.
  • The Internet, mobile phones and other global media making national borders matter less to customers.
  • The need to manage the world's resources and environment for future generations, which no single country can do alone.

The result: every American company is international, whether it wants to be or not, because what happens abroad affects how it does at home. Being international "is no longer a luxury but a necessity for economic survival."

Competition now comes from everywhere

Many familiar "American" brands are foreign owned. 7-Eleven and Firestone belong to Japanese companies. Budweiser is Belgian. Ben & Jerry's and Smith & Wesson are British. Purina and Gerber are Swiss. Smithfield Foods and the Waldorf Astoria are Chinese. Foreign direct investmentWhen a company from one country builds or buys a business in another country, rather than just selling to it.Foreign firms own more than $4.6 trillion of business in the United States. This is why the book says every American company is now international whether it likes it or not., meaning foreign companies owning businesses in the United States, is worth more than $4.6 trillion, mostly from Britain, Japan, the Netherlands, Canada and France.

The good news runs the other way too. A study of 1,250 U.S. manufacturers found that companies operating in many countries grew twice as fast as purely domestic ones and made higher profits. Intel earns 78 percent of its revenue outside the United States; Exxon and Apple earn about 60 percent abroad.

What international marketing is

The book's definition: international marketing is the performance of business activities, including pricing, promotion, product, and distribution decisions, across national borders.

Read that definition next to the definition of ordinary marketing and you will notice only one difference: the words across national borders, or as the book also puts it, in more than one country. The ideas of marketing are the same everywhere. Whether you are selling "in Dimebox, Texas, or Dar es Salaam, Tanzania," the job is the same job.

So where does the difficulty come from? Not from the ideas. It comes from the environment within which marketing plans must be implemented: the surroundings. Abroad you meet unfamiliar problems and much more uncertainty, and you need a wider range of strategies to cope. The marketer's task is to shape the controllable elementsThe parts of marketing the company itself decides: product, price, promotion, distribution channels and research.Called controllable because the firm can change them. The book counts five, and students often forget research is one of them., the things the company decides (product, price, promotion, distribution, research), to fit the uncontrollable elementsOutside forces the company cannot change and has to work around: competitors, politics, laws, how customers behave, the level of technology.At home there is one set of these. Abroad there is one set for every country you enter. That is the extra difficulty of international marketing., the things the company cannot change (competitors, politics, laws, customer behavior, level of technology), so that the company reaches its goals.

If the exam asks: "The marketer's task is the same in Dimebox, Texas, or Dar es Salaam." Discuss.

Answer yes and no. Yes: the task and the principles of marketing are the same everywhere. No: the environment is not the same, and adapting to that environment is what makes the international job different. Both halves are needed for full marks.

The international marketing task

At home a marketer deals with one set of uncontrollable forces. Abroad there are at least two levels of uncontrollable uncertainty: the forces at home, plus a separate set of forces for every foreign country entered. Exhibit 1.3 draws this as rings around a centre.

Controllable elements
The centre of the diagram. These are the marketing decisions the company makes itself: price, product, promotion, channels of distribution (how the product reaches the customer) and research. The company can change them, over the long run and usually over the short run too.
Domestic environment uncontrollables

The outer rings, one for each country. The self-reference criterion and ethnocentrism

The key to success abroad is adaptationConsciously expecting the outside forces at home and abroad to affect your marketing plan, and adjusting the plan to reduce their effect.The book calls this the key to success abroad. The two things that block it are the self-reference criterion and ethnocentrism.: consciously expecting the uncontrollable forces at home and abroad to affect your marketing plan, and adjusting the plan to reduce their effect. Two habits of mind get in the way.

Self-reference criterion (SRC)
The unconscious tendency to judge other cultures by your own culture's values, experiences and knowledge. When we meet a problem in another culture we react by instinct, using what worked at home, and the result is often a bad decision. The word unconscious matters: you cannot switch the SRCThe unconscious habit of judging other cultures by your own culture's values and experiences.It is unconscious, so you cannot simply decide to stop doing it. That is why the book gives a four-step method to catch it. It leads to misunderstandings and bad decisions abroad. off just by deciding to, so you need a method to catch it.
Ethnocentrism
The belief that people in your own company, culture or country know the best way to do things. It became a special problem for American managers after the boom years of the 1990s, and it is a problem in general whenever managers from rich countries work in poorer ones.

The book's example of the SRC is personal space. Americans stand at a distance that feels right to them without thinking about it. Someone from a culture where people stand closer moves in; the American steps back; each one decides the other is rude. Both are victims of their SRC. The SRC can make you miss a difference altogether, make you dismiss a difference as unimportant, or make you react in a way that offends. Refusing food or drink is polite in the United States. In Asia or the Middle East it is an insult.

Failures caused by the SRC: Starbucks opened a shop inside Beijing's Forbidden City and took six years to realize the mistake. Zara sold a striped blouse with a yellow six-pointed star that looked like the badge Jews were forced to wear under the Nazis. Successes from setting the SRC aside: McVitie's sold its biscuits in Japan individually wrapped in gift boxes at three times the price, because in Japan biscuits are gifts. Unilever changed its detergent for Brazil: a simpler formula, plastic packaging instead of paper because clothes are washed in rivers, and small cheap packs. McDonald's created the Maharaja Mac with two mutton patties for India, where beef is not eaten. Vicks became Wicks in Germany because the original spelling sounds like a crude German word.

Cross-cultural analysis: four steps to remove the SRC

01

Define the problem or goal in home-country cultural traits, habits or norms

Write down the problem as your own culture sees it. This makes your assumptions visible.

02

Define the problem or goal in foreign-country cultural traits, habits or norms

Ask people from the target country to describe the same problem. Make no value judgments: do not decide which view is better.

03

Isolate the SRC influence

Compare the two descriptions. Where they differ, your own assumptions are complicating the problem. Look at those differences carefully.

04

Redefine the problem without the SRC influence

Now state the problem again with your assumptions removed, and solve it for the best business result.

The book's own example: an American sales manager in Tokyo cancelled the daily early-morning sales meeting because his company did not hold such meetings at home. Sales fell sharply. Japanese salespeople are motivated mainly by pressure from their peers, and the meeting was where that pressure worked. When the meetings came back, so did the sales. One more warning from this section: a similarity you fail to notice does no harm, but "the one difference that goes undetected can create a marketing failure."

  • Tolerance means understanding that people differ and accepting and working with them anyway. You do not have to adopt their ways, "but you must allow others to be different and equal." A culture that is less punctual is not less productive, only different.
  • Knowledge of cultures and history explains behavior that would otherwise look irrational. Latin American suspicion of foreign investment, or China's reluctance to open fully, both make sense once you know the history (Chapter 3 tells it).
  • Knowledge of market potential and trends lets a marketer spot an opportunity long before competitors notice it.

How does a company build global awareness into itself? Three ways. Choose managers who have already shown it. Build personal relationships abroad, because market entry is often made easier by existing social ties. And, "perhaps the most effective approach," have senior managers and board members from many different countries. The book notes that American managers value this last approach less than managers elsewhere do.

Stages of international marketing involvement

Once a company decides to go abroad, it must decide how deeply. Research finds three things push companies to go international faster: having high-technology or strong marketing skills; having a small home market but a large factory; and having managers with good personal networks abroad.

The book describes five stages of involvement. Two warnings before the list. The stages are not a ladder: a company can start at any stage and can be in more than one at the same time. And high-technology firms with short product lives often skip straight to treating the whole world, home included, as one market from day one.

01

No direct foreign marketing

The company makes no effort to sell abroad, but its products get there anyway: through trading companies, through foreign buyers who come to it, or through domestic wholesalers who sell abroad without telling it. Often an unexpected order from the company's website is what first sparks interest in foreign markets.

02

Infrequent foreign marketing

The company sells abroad only when it has extra stock, because production or home demand has swung. As soon as home demand recovers, foreign sales stop. Nothing changes in the company's organization or products. Few firms do this today, because customers abroad want a long-term relationship and the profits are small.

03

Regular foreign marketing

The company sets aside permanent production capacity for foreign markets and serves them through middlemen or its own sales force or subsidiaries. Its focus is still the home market, but products may be adapted, and foreign profits stop being "a bonus" and become something the firm depends on. The book's example is Meter-Man, a 25-person Minnesota company making agricultural measuring tools. It started exploring exports in 1989 and now makes a third of its sales in 35 countries: "going international has become critical to our existence."

04

International marketing

The company is fully committed. It looks for markets all over the world and plans production, often outside the home country, specifically for those markets. It has become an international or multinational company. The example is Fedders, the largest U.S. maker of room air conditioners. Its home market was seasonal and no longer growing, so it entered China through a joint venture, Fedders Xinle, and redesigned its product as a lightweight split unit, because in China an air conditioner is a status symbolThings that stand for something else in a culture, especially language and the arts.The third element of culture. A color, a number, a pattern or a word can carry meaning that outsiders do not see, which is where many marketing mistakes come from., not just a box that cools a room.

05

Global marketing

The biggest change is in how the company thinks. It treats the whole world, including its home country, as one market. Customer groups are defined by income, by how they use the product, or by other things that cut across countries, not by national borders. Companies often make this shift when more than half their sales come from abroad. When Coca-Cola reorganized in the mid-1990s, the United States became just one part of one of six international divisions: almost half of revenue, but only about 20 percent of profit.

The first two stages are reactive: the company is responding to orders, not planning to expand. Stages three, four and five match the three strategic orientations that come next.

Two exam questions that look alike

Global company versus multinational company: a multinational treats each country separately; a global company treats the world as one market. Global Commerce Causes Peace

The chapter opener. Boeing has customers in more than 150 countries and 140,000 employees in more than 65; its 787 airliner uses parts from Australia, France, India, Italy, Japan, Russia and Sweden. Five billion people own mobile phones, led by Samsung, Apple and Huawei. What it shows. International trade does more than make money. To trade, people from different countries have to work together, and the personal relationships and understanding that grow from this are the foundations of peace and prosperity.

If it is the question. If asked whether trade promotes peace, use Boeing as the big-company example and SodaStream as the small-company example. Then add the book's own honest caveat: 20 to 30 percent of Boeing's business is military sales.

CB 1.3

Risks and Payoffs in International Marketing

After the 2009 crash, a U.S. government program lent about half a billion dollars each to two electric car makers, Tesla and Fisker. Fisker built about 2,400 cars and went bankrupt in 2013; a Chinese buyer later revived the brand as the $130,000 Karma Revero. If the exam asks: why does the balance of payments always balance when the balance of trade does not?

Because the balance of payments is a double-entry record of every transaction, including borrowing and investment, and those flows fill whatever gap trade leaves. The balance of trade is only one line inside it, the goods line of the current account, and that line can be positive or negative.

Protection logic and illogic

People who want protection give many reasons. The book boils them down to twelve: (1) protecting an infant industryA new industry in a country that is too young and small to compete yet against established foreign firms.Protecting it for a while so it can grow is one of only three arguments for protectionism that economists accept. Which of the twelve do economists accept?

Only three: infant industry, national defense, and industrialization of underdeveloped countries. A fourth, conservation of natural resources, is gaining acceptance. Temporary protection to help an industry adjust can be defended, but in practice it almost always becomes permanent and breeds inefficiency. If the exam asks you to support or refute each argument, this is the answer key.

Who pays for protection? Consumers, and they do not know it. A study of 21 protected American industries found consumers pay about $70 billion a year extra in higher prices. The cost of saving one job came to about $170,000, more than that worker earns in wages and benefits. The Trump tariffs cost the average household an extra $250 in 2019. Protection is popular when unemployment is high, but it "rarely leads to renewed growth in a declining industry."

The evidence on the other side, in the book's Exhibit 2.6: from 1990 to 2020, what consumers could buy with their money grew more than 40 percent in the United States and Canada, 19 percent in Mexico, and over 1,000 percent in China. The two big American steps toward free trade were NAFTA in 1994 and giving China permanent normal trade status (PNTR) in 2000, which was followed by China joining the WTO in 2001. The authors find no evidence that NAFTA cost American jobs. The widening gap between rich and poor Americans, they argue, came from decisions made inside the country about taxes, wealth and labor relations, not from trade.

Tariffs

A tariff is a tax that a government charges on goods entering the country. It is used to raise money, to discourage imports, or both. In the United States tariffs, also called customs duties, come in three types:

Ad valorem duties
A percentage of the value of the imported goods. A 10 percent duty on a $100 item is $10.
Specific duties
A fixed amount per unit: per kilogram, per litre, per item. The price of the goods does not matter.
Compound duty
Both at once: a fixed amount per unit plus a percentage of value.

What tariffs do, in the book's three-part list. They increase inflation, privileges for special interests, government control of the economy, and the number of tariffs, because one country's tariff invites another's in return (reciprocityDoing to another country what it does to you. Barriers in history

Every kind of barrier above has already been tried on a large scale. These are the book's own historical examples, useful whenever a question asks why protectionism persists or what it costs.

Navigation Acts, 1651
England forced its colonies to trade only with English ships, to shut the Dutch out. A shipping rule used as an import policy. It was one of the grievances behind the Boston Tea Party of 1773, where American merchants were trying to take control of distribution away from Britain.

Cobden Treaty, 1860
The opposite move. Britain and France cut or removed tariffs on each other's goods. The deal introduced most-favored-nation status, meaning each side promised the other the best terms it gave anyone, and it led in time to the multilateral agreements of the twentieth century.
Hawley-Smoot Tariff Act, 1930
The average American tariff on more than 20,000 goods rose above 60 percent. 60 countries retaliated with high tariffs of their own. World trade stopped, and a recession became the Great Depression. This is the strongest single argument against protectionism and the reason GATT was created.

Arab oil producers refused to sell to countries that had supported Israel. It shocked the industrial world into understanding how global supply and demand had become. An embargo run by sellers rather than buyers, showing the tool cuts both ways.
The pasta war
The modern miniature of the same logic: a 40 percent American tariff on European spaghetti, retaliation on walnuts and lemons, then fertilizer, paper and beef tallow, and a settlement only when the original subsidy was dropped.

History and Geography: The Foundations of Culture

Cateora, Chapter 3

The idea of this chapter: to understand why people in a country behave as they do, you need to know two things that most marketers ignore, its history and its geography. History explains attitudes. Geography explains what products work, where people live, and how goods move.

Historical perspective in global business

The chapter defines culture as a society's accepted basis for responding to external and internal events: the shared way a group of people reacts to what happens to it. To read behavior in a country, a marketer needs its history and geography.

History gives a nation its sense of "mission": how it sees its neighbors, its place in the world, and itself. It shapes attitudes toward government and business, toward the relationship between bosses and workers, and toward foreign companies. It also affects practical business decisions: where to invest, how to enter a market, even whom to hire. A former American ambassador to Japan, James Day Hodgson, put the rule simply: anyone doing business in another country should know at least the encyclopedia version of that country's past, out of politeness if nothing else.

China: why the leadership is wary of foreign influence

The book says American history "really begins with China." The Boston Tea Party was a protest not only against a British tax but against Britain's ban on American traders dealing directly with the Chinese port of Canton. Three episodes from Chinese history explain attitudes that still shape business there today:

First Opium War and the Treaty of Nanjing, 1839 to 1842
In the early 1800s Britain was buying huge amounts of Chinese tea and paying in silver, so silver was draining out of Britain. The British East India Company found a product China would buy in return: opium grown in British India. It was easy to ship, valuable for its weight, and addictive. The silver drain reversed. When the Chinese emperor ordered the opium stocks in Canton destroyed, Britain sent warships, sank Chinese boats and blockaded the ports. Under the Treaty of Nanjing China gave Britain Hong Kong and $21 million and opened the ports of Xiamen, Fuzhou, Ningbo and Shanghai to foreigners. The book's key sentence: China recognized for the first time that it had lost its status as a great power.
Second Opium War, 1857 to 1860
The first treaty did not settle the real issue, which was foreign access to Chinese trade. In the second war British and French forces destroyed the emperor's summer palace in Beijing. The new treaty gave foreign traders still more freedom and specifically allowed Christian missionaries to preach throughout China.
Taiping Rebellion, 1851 to 1864
The humiliation by foreigners made the Chinese lose faith in their own government, and rebellion followed. Its leader, Hong Xiuquan, had failed the Confucian civil-service exam four times. He met Protestant missionaries, began having visions, declared himself the brother of Jesus, and was crowned "Heavenly King." His followers fought their way north to Nanjing. Foreigners, who disliked his version of Christianity and his attacks on Shanghai, raised an army against him. Between 20 and 40 million people died, making it the deadliest civil war in human history. (The 1949 Communist Revolution killed "only" 2 million.) The result today: China's leaders remain wary of foreign influence in general and of religious movements in particular.

History is subjective

History matters, but history according to whom? Every historical event is seen through the viewer's own biases and self-reference criterion, so two historians from different cultures record the same event differently. Even maps show this: maps sold in the United States put the United States in the centre; maps sold in Britain put Britain there.

The book's worked example is Mexico's love-hate relationship with the United States. Why did Mexico for so long require Mexicans to own a majority of any foreign investment? Because Mexicans see the United States as a threat to their political, economic and cultural independence. Most Americans are baffled by this. Geography and global markets

Geography is "the study of Earth's surface, climate, continents, countries, peoples, industries, and resources." It is an uncontrollable force that most marketers pay too little attention to. Knowing geography is not memorizing capital cities. It is understanding how a country's culture and economy were shaped by the land it had to live on.

Geography, nature and economic growth

Poor countries suffer far more from natural disasters than rich ones. Compare Haiti's slow recovery from its 2010 and 2021 earthquakes with Japan's fast recovery after 2011. Big engineering projects can help or harm: China's Three Gorges Dam displaced 1.3 million people and traps the silt that used to fertilize farmland downstream; India's Narmada dam project displaced tens of thousands. Resources

Where minerals and energy are found is a geographic accident, and no country's supply matches its needs. Oil and gas provide more than 60 percent of the world's energy. The United States produced all its own oil until 1942 and imported 66 percent of it by 2011; with fracking, dependence should fall to 50 to 60 percent by mid-century. Countries differ hugely in how efficiently they use energy: Japan needs less than 5 ounces of oil to produce $1 of output, China about 80, mostly because of outdated technology. New resource contests are starting: lithium in Bolivia, nickel in New Caledonia. Renewable energy grew tenfold in three years.

World trade routes and communication links

Trade routes tie the world together. They overcome distance, natural barriers, missing resources and differences between peoples. Routes laid down by the 1500s still matter today. Spanish ships carried silver from Mexico to Manila and came back with Chinese silk, which was unloaded in Mexico and carried overland to the Atlantic. Today the Panama Canal, now being widened, does that crossing, and Latin America's ties to Europe and Asia date from that era. Most trade still moves between Europe, North America and Asia, but the fastest-growing routes are between developing countries. China is building an overland "Silk Road" to Europe. Melting Arctic ice is opening a Northwest Passage that may one day compete with the canal.

Communication is the foundation of all trade: you need to know where goods are and where they are wanted, and to send messages instantly across distance. Each new technology, the telegraph, the telephone, television, satellites, mobile phones, computers, the Internet and now blockchain, created new ways of doing business, and companies that did not adapt disappeared. The book expects the Internet and mobile revolutions to be no different, with the biggest changes still to come. Its small example: the Corinth Canal in Greece, opened in 1893, replaced the old practice of dragging ships across a 6-kilometre strip of land.

Three pictures of culture
Hofstede calls it the software of the mind, a tool for solving problems. Edward Hall calls it an invisible barrier that managers keep bumping their heads against without seeing it. Ambassador James Day Hodgson calls it a thicket: hard to get through, but with effort and patience you usually can.

Culture touches every part of marketing: pricing, research, promotion, distribution channels, the product itself, its packaging and its styling. And it works in both directions. A marketer adapts to culture, but a marketer's work also becomes part of the culture. The book's formula: markets are the result of the three-way interaction of a marketer's efforts, economic conditions, and all other elements of the culture. Whenever a marketer introduces something new, the marketer becomes an agent of change, because "the use of something new is the beginning of cultural change."

Evidence that culture shapes what people buy

  • Birthrates. Singapore's birth rate jumped in 1976, 1988 and 2000. Those were Years of the Dragon, considered lucky. Sellers of diapers, then toys, then school places felt the wave in successive years. Japan's birth rate fell 20 percent in 1966, the Year of the Fire Horse, because women born in that year are believed to have unhappy lives and possibly to murder their husbands. It comes round every 60 years, so the next one is 2026.
  • What people consume (Exhibit 4.2). The Dutch spend the most on cut flowers. Germans, Britons and Japanese eat the most chocolate. Spaniards and Japanese eat the most fish. Italians eat the most pasta, which Marco Polo brought from China in 1270. The French and Italians drink the most wine. Spain halved its cigarette consumption in six years after banning smoking in public in 2011, "a huge cultural shift that the world seldom sees."
  • What follows from it (Exhibit 4.3). Countries that eat fish rather than red meat have less heart disease. Japan has the world's highest death rate from stomach cancer, blamed on salty foods like soy sauce and on a bacterium that spread in the hard years after the war, and therefore Japan has the world's most advanced stomach cancer treatment. Culture shapes not just what a country buys but what it produces.

Geography
Defined broadly: climate, landscape, plants, animals and even microbes. Jared Diamond argues that new ideas spread faster east to west than north to south, because the climate stays similar (along the Silk Road, not across the Sahara or through Panama), and that this, plus nastier germs, explains why Europe and Asia came to dominate. Philip Parker finds a strong link between latitude and income per person, so temperature itself can be a way of dividing up markets. After each new disease, HIV, SARS, COVID-19, new rituals appear almost at once.
History
Tobacco as the Virginia colony's cash crop shaped American trade policy. The Declaration of Independence was influenced by The Wealth of Nations, published the same year. The 2003 Iraq war produced Mecca Cola, Muslim Up and ColaTurka as protest brands. Germany's distrust of propaganda after World War II still limits what marketers may do there. The post-war baby boom still drives American spending patterns.
The political economy
How politics and economics are organized. Four systems competed for the twentieth century: colonialism, fascism, communism, and democracy with free enterprise. Fascism fell in 1945, colonialism after the war, communism in the 1990s. Politics and law shape a country's institutions and values; Chapters 6 and 7 go deeper.
Technology
Which invention most changed American institutions and values in the last century? Not jet planes, television or the Internet. The Pill. Birth control freed women to build careers and freed men to spend more time with children; more than half of American marketing majors are now women. The Catholic Church banned it in 1930 and again in 1968. But technology does not fix everything: American healthcare has the most advanced technology and Americans still die younger than people in many other rich countries.

Elements of culture

Culture is made of five elements: values, rituals, symbols, beliefs and thought processes. Products, distribution and promotion all have to be designed with each of the five in mind.

Individualism/Collectivism Index
How much people prefer to look after themselves rather than their group. High IDV is an "I" mentality: individual initiative is rewarded. Low IDV is a "we" mentality: the individual gives way to the group. In collectivist cultures people belong from birth to strong, tight groups that protect them in return for total loyalty.
Power Distance Index
How much inequality between the powerful and the powerless a culture accepts. High PDI cultures are hierarchical: power comes from social position, manipulation and inheritance, outsiders are distrusted, and those in power are entitled to privileges. Low PDI cultures value equality and believe power should come from knowledge and achievement.
Uncertainty Avoidance Index
How well a culture tolerates uncertainty and ambiguity. High UAI cultures dislike ambiguity, distrust new ideas, feel anxious, follow rules and want absolute truths. Low UAI cultures accept disagreement and unusual behavior, take risks, and test ideas against experience rather than authority.

Rituals

Rituals are patterns of behavior that people learn and repeat. Big ones: weddings (in Delhi the groom arrives on a white horse), funerals, graduations, the Muslim pilgrimage to Mecca (2 million a year), Hindu prayers at Varanasi (60 to 80 million pilgrims). Small ones: dinner in Madrid starts at midnight and lasts three hours; Japanese shop staff bow to you at the door; in Spain the doctor talks to you at her desk, not in an examination room. Rituals coordinate how people interact and "let people know what to expect." Business negotiation is a ritual too.

Symbols: language and aesthetics

Symbols are things that stand for something else. The anthropologist Edward T. Hall said that culture is communication, with its own "languages" of time, space, things, friendships and agreements. We start learning to read these symbols at birth.

Language
In some countries language is a political matter: France has a government agency fighting English words, and language disputes have threatened to split Canada. For advertising copywriters the problem is not literal translation but idiom and symbolic meaning. The Spanish word tambo is a roadside inn in Bolivia, a dairy farm in Argentina and a brothel in Chile. Papua New Guinea has 750 languages whose speakers cannot understand each other.
Linguistic distance
How far apart two languages sit on the family tree of languages. Counting from English: German 1, Danish 2, Spanish 3, Japanese 4, Hebrew 5, Chinese 6, Thai 7. Doing business across a wide language gap costs more, and countries far apart in language trade less and hold different values; as distance from English grows, individualism falls. English has one word for "you", while Mandarin (ni and nin) and Spanish (tu and usted) have polite and familiar forms that mark status. So as English spreads, the idea of equality spreads with it.
Aesthetics
A culture's sense of beauty, shown in its arts, folklore, music, drama, dance, dress and cosmetics. Customers use it to say who they are. Japanese prefer earth tones; Chinese prefer red and yellow. The mistakes: McDonald's chose the Lindsay tartan for a Scottish promotion and outraged Clan Donald. In Japan the crane is lucky but the word for four sounds like the word for death, so teacups are sold in sets of five. Migros, a Swiss retailer, printed Hitler's face on coffee creamers by accident. Nike named a shoe "Black and Tan," the name of a British force that terrorized Ireland, and put a pattern on an Air Max sole that looked like the Arabic word for God. A British Krispy Kreme promotion was called the KKK. Martin Gannon's cultural metaphors, American Football, the Spanish Bullfight, India's Dance of Shiva, the Japanese Garden, are "fine fodder for advertising."

Beliefs

Many beliefs come from religion, but many are not religious at all, and what Westerners call superstition can be central to life elsewhere: ghosts, fortune telling, palm reading, blood types, phases of the moon. Chinese prices end in 8, the number that means prosperity; the Beijing Olympics opened on 8 August 2008 at 8:08 pm. Thought processes

Thought processes are the ways people reason, and these differ across cultures too. Culture matters more in quick judgments than in long deliberations, and it even affects how people perceive smells. Richard Nisbett's book The Geography of Thought found that Asians look at the whole picture and remember both background and foreground, while Westerners focus on the main object in front and remember little of what was behind it. This difference between focus and big picture predicts differences in values, preferences and expectations, "pertinent to Japanese selling in Jacksonville or Belgians selling in Beijing."

Cultural change

Culture is alive, "a living process," and it changes. Yet it is also conservative and resists change. The book admits this sounds like a contradiction. Cultures that are more open to change tend to belong to more successful economies. Change can come from war (Japan after 1945), from disaster, or, most often, from a society solving problems that changes in its surroundings have created. Culture, in this view, is "the accumulation of a series of the best solutions to problems faced in common." Societies find these solutions by accident, by inventing them, and above all by borrowing them.

Cultural borrowing
Taking ideas and habits from other cultures and making them your own. The book's example is an American woman's breakfast: an orange from the Mediterranean, a melon from Persia, waffles made by a Scandinavian method from wheat first grown in Asia Minor, maple syrup from Native Americans, eggs from a bird first tamed in Indochina, bacon cured by a northern European method, a newspaper printed in letters from the Middle East on paper invented in China, and thanks given to a Hebrew god in a language from India and Europe, after which she declares herself "100 percent American." And she is right, because each borrowed thing was changed to fit until it became uniquely American. Culture is learned, and this inheritance is what separates humans from other animals.
Similarities: an illusion
Sharing a language or a heritage does not mean a product or a message will transfer. Britons and Americans both speak English, but lift versus elevator, a bathroom being a room where you bathe, hoovering rather than vacuuming, and a film title like The Spy Who Shagged Me that means nothing in America. Edward Hall warns that Britons and Americans actually have a harder time, because they assume they are alike. There is no such thing as a "European consumer." Regions of the United States differ; the two halves of reunified Germany still differ. Never assume that "if it sells in one country it will sell in another."
Resistance to change
Whether people accept something new depends on two things: how interested they are in the subject, and how disruptive the new thing is to their present values and behavior. When Europe industrialized quickly, attitudes toward time and toward women working changed, so instant foods and labor-saving appliances that would once have been resisted were welcomed. Leaders resist too: China's Hu Jintao called for more "Chinese cultural production" to fight Western dominance, and Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin openly scorn Western values.

Planned and unplanned cultural change

Through most of history, cultural change just happened. Increasingly it is the result of a deliberate effort by an institution people trust: planned changeDeliberately working to change the parts of a culture that resist your product, so people accept it faster.Two steps: first find out which cultural beliefs are in the way, then work to turn those obstacles into reasons to buy. Because you are changing a culture on purpose, the book says you are responsible for the results.. The method that social planners use to introduce new crops or sanitation works for marketers too. First, find out which cultural factors conflict with your innovation and cause resistance. Then work to change those factors, turning obstacles into reasons to accept it. A marketer introducing something new has two choices: wait for the culture to change, or spur the change along. Fidelity Investments set up a tent outside Tokyo's Shinjuku station and handed commuters brochures and demonstrations of share trading in Japanese; even so, the change came too slowly for most foreign firms. The book describes three strategies:

01

Cultural congruence

Sell products similar to ones already on the market, in a way that fits existing cultural norms as closely as possible, so there is almost no resistance. A great deal of successful marketing involves no cultural change at all.

02

Unplanned change

Introduce the product and hope for the best. The Japanese diet is the example. American occupation troops brought bread, milk and steak. Fat and protein now exceed vegetables in the Japanese diet. Burger King bought the Morinaga Love chain, home of the salmon burger, and now sells Whoppers there. The Japanese put on weight and then flocked to health clubs and low-fat foods. Nobody planned any of it.

03

Planned change

Deliberately set out to change the parts of the culture that resist your marketing goal. In the Japanese example: identify why people resist a change of diet, then work to overcome that resistance, so acceptance comes faster.

A marketing strategy is judged by the culture in one of three ways: accepted, resisted or rejected. "All too often, marketers are not aware of the scope of their impact on a host culture." If you set out to change a culture on purpose, you have some responsibility for the consequences. The book's discussion questions push on this. One asks for examples of functional (helpful) versus dysfunctional (harmful) innovations. Another asks you to defend the claim that a multinational has no responsibility beyond making a product that is safe and works. A third asks you to name a product whose introduction may cause harm, and to explain how to remove the harm and still make a profit.

Human Universals: The Myth of Diversity?

The anthropologist Donald Brown lists hundreds of things all humans do. We use metaphors. We have status and roles. We are ethnocentric. We make art. We form groups that are hostile to outsiders. We imitate outsiders and resist them at the same time. We keep sex private. We show emotion with our faces. We return favors. We use mood-changing drugs. We overestimate how objective our own thinking is. We fear snakes. We recognize debts in exchange. And we trade and transport goods. The last two make us "the exchanging animal."

What it shows. A counterweight to the rest of the chapter. Culture's influence is everywhere, but we are still one species with shared habits. These universals are why marketing principles travel across borders even when the details of applying them do not.

If it is the question. Use it to balance any answer that leans too hard on "everything is culturally relative," and to explain Chapter 1's claim that the principles of marketing are universal while the environment is not.

CB 4.2

Culture, Genes, and Take-out Slides

The neuroscientist Joan Chiao found that about 80 percent of East Asians carry a version of a gene that is linked to depression in white people, yet East Asians show no such effect. Across 29 countries, nations where this gene is common were twice as likely to be collectivist, and those were the places where infectious disease had historically been worst: staying close to the group protected carriers. A separate story: three of the four Japanese infielders who have played in American Major League Baseball were seriously injured by American "take-out slides" aimed at the fielder. Japanese runners are taught to slide low so as not to hurt the fielder.

What it shows. Geography (disease) shapes genes and cultural values together, so culture is not upbringing alone. The baseball story is collectivism (protect the other player) meeting "anything goes" individualism on a playing field.

If it is the question. Cite it when explaining the origins of culture, especially geography including microbiology, and as evidence that value differences have real physical consequences, not just survey results.
