The Nature of the Pricing Process

Slides: Chapter 01, Introduction to pricing strategies and practices · Book: Hanna and Dodge, Chapter 1 · plus Kotler on value-based pricing and Smith on who sets price

Three questions. What is a price, and who decides it? What five things must you know before setting one? And what are the three basic ways to arrive at a number?

Why pricing matters

The book opens with McKinsey's Marn and Rosiello: "The fastest and most effective way for a company to realize its maximum profit is to get its pricing right." The right price raises profit faster than selling more units. The wrong price shrinks it just as fast. Many managers leave money "on the table" without knowing it.

Getting it wrong in either direction costs money. Too high, and demand falls. Worse, as IBM found, a high price creates a price umbrellaWhen a leader's high price leaves room underneath it for competitors to come in cheaper and take customers.IBM is the book's example. A price that is too high does not just lose sales; it invites competition.: room underneath it for competitors to come in cheaper and take share. Too low, and you have an opportunity costMoney you could have had but gave up. In pricing: the gap between what the market would have paid and the lower price you actually charged.Setting a price too low does not show up as a loss on any statement, which is why the book calls it money 'left on the table'.: the gap between what the market would have paid and what you charged. That money never appears in any account.

Both extremes have worked. Fort Howard Paper, Dell and MCI built a positional advantageA position in the market that competitors find hard to copy, won either by low price or by being different.Dell and MCI won it with low prices; Maytag, John Deere and Federal Express won it with differentiation and charge a premium. Both routes work. on low prices. Maytag, John Deere and Federal Express built one on being different, and charge a premium. Good pricing "bridges the gap between internal costs and external market demand." A firm's options are set by four things: its cost structure, its competitive status, its marketing strategies and its organizational capabilities.

Critical factors in the pricing decision

Five things a marketer must understand before setting a price. The slides list the same five.

1 · Effective cost information

Know where costs come from and how they are shared out. Not only manufacturing costs: marketing, distribution, design and technology too. Many firms still spread overhead on the basis of direct labour, which distorts costs now that support, marketing and design cost far more than labour. Two better approaches. Treat every activity as support for making and delivering products, so all costs are product costs. Or separate scale-related costsCosts that fall as you make more of the same thing.The book's rule of thumb: they drop 15 to 25 percent per unit every time volume doubles. Contrast with variety-related costs, which rise 20 to 25 percent each time variety doubles., which fall as you make more (15 to 25 percent per unit each time volume doubles), from variety-related costsCosts that rise as you offer more different versions, models or options.Every time variety doubles, these rise 20 to 25 percent. Old accounting systems hide this by spreading overhead on direct labour, so a busy product line looks cheaper than it is., which rise as you offer more versions (20 to 25 percent each time variety doubles). A firm that does not separate them thinks its crowded product line is cheaper than it is.

2 · Customer valuation

Do not compete on price alone. Think in terms of total valueEverything the customer gets for the price, not just the physical product: installation, delivery, support, after-sale service, and symbolic things like prestige.The book's point: customers pay for the whole package, so a marketer should compete on total value rather than on price alone. as the customer sees it: the whole package. That includes installation, delivery, technical support and after-sale service, and symbolic features such as prestige and status. Different market targetsThe customer groups a company chooses to serve. Also called segments.Different groups value the product differently and will pay different prices, which is where the profit from pricing separately comes from. weigh the pieces differently. Measuring perceived value is hard: competing offers are hard to compare objectively, product qualities keep changing, and customers distort features into their own idea of value. So accept the criteria customers actually use, and monitor them continuously.

3 · Market targets

Groups of customers differ, so the market can be split into segments, and segments can get different products at different prices. That is where extra profit comes from: some segments will pay more for a version of the package. The book's example is the San Luis Sourdough Company, which sells bread to stores on three tiers: 97 cents wholesale for plain delivery; $1.02 if the store may return unsold day-old bread; another 3 cents if the company also stocks the shelf, bar-codes each bag and accepts returns.

The limit is the Robinson-Patman ActA U.S. law that makes it illegal to charge different buyers different prices for the same product, unless the difference can be justified.The three defences: the products are different, the costs of serving the buyers are different, or the buyers do not compete with each other. It comes up again with quantity, seasonal and promotional discounts in Chapter 7., which makes it unlawful to charge different buyers different prices for the same product. Three defences: the products are different; the same product costs different amounts to sell to different groups; or the customers do not compete with each other.

Three major pricing approaches

The slides group all pricing methods into three families. Cost-based comes from the book; the other two are from Kotler.

Cost-based pricing

Cost-based pricingSetting price by adding a desired profit to what the product costs to make and sell.Simple and common with small firms and regulated utilities, but it ignores what customers will pay, so the price can end up too high or too low. starts from what the product costs and adds the profit you want. Three cost ideas: fixed costA cost that stays the same no matter how much you produce, such as rent or salaries. Also called overhead.Spread over more units it gets smaller per unit, which is why volume matters so much to cost-based prices., or overhead, does not change with output (rent, salaries); variable costA cost that rises and falls directly with how much you produce, such as materials.Fixed plus variable equals total cost. changes with output (materials, packaging); total cost is the two together. Spread fixed cost over more units and cost per unit falls, so the volume you assume matters a lot.

The book's warning: this ignores the market. Cost plus a return can land too high (volume falls, customers drift to substitutes) or too low (the firm earns less than it could). Either way the firm never asked how the market would respond. It survives where it fits: small firms; regulated industries such as utilities and car insurance, where a government body approves prices and stands in for the market; and price-inelastic markets such as petrol, where a rise in crude oil is simply passed through at the pump.

The goal is pricing powerThe ability to raise or hold prices without losing market share.Escaping price competition is the whole point of value-added pricing.: the ability to escape price competition and hold higher prices and margins without losing share.

Competition-based pricing

Competition-based pricing starts from what competitors charge for similar products and positions at, above or below them. Chapter 7 covers it as the "meeting competition" objective: price is a given and the firm manages its costs to fit.

Constraints on pricing practices

Beyond costs and customer valuations, four things limit pricing: the industry, the structure of the market, the economy, and the law. A surprising rule: the more competitive the industry, the easier the pricing decision, because the market sets the price. So firms in competitive industries turn to nonprice competitionCompeting on anything other than price: advertising, positioning, customer satisfaction, selection.The book's observation: the more competitive an industry, the more firms turn to this, because competing on price alone leads nowhere.: advertising, positioning, customer satisfaction. Category-bustingA retailer offering so huge a selection in one category that customers stop comparing on price.Selection and availability take the pressure off price. Think of a store with 200 kinds of running shoe. is the retail version: offer so wide a selection that choice takes the pressure off price.

Economic conditions amplify choices. It is hard not to raise prices in good times and cut them in a recession, but doing either weakens the firm's relationship with the market. Global firms face several economies at once. And the law now reaches beyond competition into foreign trade, public policy and national interests.

Ethics in pricing

Pricing can conflict with social values because the seller wants the best long-run return, which may be above or below what the market would set today. High prices draw more comment, since they suggest excess profit. Low prices are questioned too, since they suggest less value or a trick. The dispute is really about the gap between cost and price, and critics quote profits in absolute rather than relative terms. Four practices the book says the public questions:

Proliferation of product models
Basic, better, deluxe, premium. Each step costs the customer noticeably more, but the extra features do not seem worth it. The suspicion: the range exists to nudge people upward, not because each version adds real value.
Branded versus generic
Nearly identical products, but the branded one costs far more. Medicines are the standard case: the same chemical, a well-known name on the box, several times the price. The customer feels they are paying for the name.
Replacement parts
The car is priced competitively; the spare parts cost far more than they seem worth. Foreign cars are the book's example. The owner is trapped and has to pay.
False obsolescence
A "new" model at a higher price, but what changed is a colour, a name, a minor feature. The old model is made to look out of date so people replace it.

What the four share: the customer sees a gap between cost and price but no matching gap in value. Whether each is actually wrong is debatable. The exam point is that the book says the public questions them.

Two practices are simply deceptive. A false referenceQuoting an 'original price' or 'comparable value' that misleads, or was never real, to make the current price look like a bargain.Unlike arguments about profit, this one is clear cut: it is deceptive and often illegal. uses "original price," "previously priced at" or "comparable value" to mislead; the reference may be invented. Bait and switchAdvertising a low price to bring customers in, then pushing them toward a more expensive product.Illegal when that is the purpose. The book's answer to both this and false reference is that customers can stop buying from firms that do it. advertises a low price to bring customers in, then steers them to something dearer; illegal when that is the purpose. The book's remedy: customers can stop buying from firms that do it, and firms can publish a statement of pricing principles.

Market Interpretations of Price

Slides: Chapter 2 · Book: Hanna and Dodge, Chapter 2. The slides follow the book section by section.

Chapter 1 looked at price from the company's side. This chapter looks at it from the customer's side: what a price tells people, and how they use it to judge quality, sort products and spot bargains. One idea runs through it all: price is information, not just a cost.

Two roles of price: allocation and information

The chapter opens with a sports economist: there is "no relationship whatsoever between ticket prices and owners' costs." Tickets are priced at what the market will bear. Owners cannot pass on star salaries until fans value the game more. Price is set by perception, and it does two jobs.

Allocation role
Price decides who can buy and how much, so it sets total demand. Pricing that expands the market lets more people enjoy what society produces. Pricing that shuts people out splits society into haves and have-nots.
Information role
Price sends signals about quality and about the status of owning the product. With deals and promotions, it becomes a powerful stimulus to buy.

Price as part of the marketing mix

Price is the only element of the mix that brings money in; product, promotion and distribution all cost money. (Pricing below competitors is an implicit expense too, since it gives up profit.) Effective pricing is judged by positioning and by total profit over the product's life.

The book's Table 2.1 shows how quality and price combine to affect return on investmentProfit as a percentage of the money invested.Finance executives judge prices by it. Target return pricing in Chapter 7 is built around hitting a set ROI.. High quality at a low price earns the highest return. High quality at a high price is a close second at 34 percent, more than twice the 15 percent of low quality at a low price. Worst is medium quality at a medium price: 2 percent. Quality is what pays, and quality with a competitive price pays most.

The elements are synergistic: together they produce more than the sum of the parts, if each supports the others. Three kinds of fit:

Consistency
The logical fit. A low price on a premium product is inconsistent; so is selling a premium product through cheap stores. Premium products carry excellent quality, prices above competitors and selective distribution.
Integration
Elements actively working together. Heavy advertising plus a high price: the margin pays for the advertising, and the advertising justifies the price (Ralph Lauren, Cadillac, Pella, Perrier). Also promotional pricingTemporary price cuts, rebates, cash-back deals and similar incentives used to boost sales for a while.Retailers use it for store traffic; manufacturers use it to win market share and get people to switch brands. (a lower price with advertising) and front-load pricing (an initial price that includes extended service).
Leverage
Use each element where it earns the most. If rebates build share better than advertising, invest in rebates. If price cuts start giving diminishing returns, shift the money. A discount is an investment measured in sales dollars not collected. Beer and soft drinks leverage price.

Price as purchasing information

A price looks like the one hard fact about a product. To the customer it is interpreted, and subjective. Customers use it in three ways: to signal quality, value and status; to sort products into categories; and to form impressions, especially the impression of a bargain.

Price as a marketing stimulus

Price cuts raise sales, and so do new products launched at low prices. Promotional pricing is a major competitive tool: short-term cuts, cash-back and rebates, value pricing, premiums and contests. Consumer interpretations of price

People's Express and MCI changed their industries with deeply discounted, no-frills service. Competitors answered not with lower prices but by packaging price with quality and customer service. People's Express went out of business; MCI had to change repeatedly to match AT&T. Both forgot that price is only one of several differences customers see, and that it has more than economic meaning. Competing only on low price is rarely sustainable.

Time implications

Customers treat delay as a time cost, so products that are readily available command a premium. Atlas Door delivers industrial doors in weeks against an industry norm of four months, and gets a premium for it. Norwalk Furniture cut delivery to three weeks against two to three months and grew sales and dealers. Industrial buyers running just-in-timeA system where a customer keeps almost no stock and relies on suppliers delivering exactly when needed.It makes fast, reliable delivery worth more than a low price to industrial buyers. (JIT) systems rank on-time delivery above price. Shortening delivery time can beat cutting price.

Psychological implications

Because customers judge quality by price, a genuinely better product must cost more than lesser ones, or it will be read as lesser. Wilson's Sting tennis racket looked and played like the Prince Graphite but was priced $100 lower thanks to a manufacturing innovation. The low price told buyers it was worse, and it sold poorly until Wilson repositioned it for people who wanted graphite but thought they could only afford aluminium.

Some pay more for quality; others pay more as insurance against a cheap brand turning out badly, since they cannot judge quality themselves. Firms known for high service can charge more for the same reason. Customers also sort brands into price linesGroups of prices that customers sort products into, such as economy, mid-range and premium.Chapter 2 gives four: economy, moderate, prestigious, luxurious. and hold a range they consider "fair" for a product; a price pushed outside it works less well than expected. Even the form matters: $9.95 says bargain, $10 says quality.

The pricing environment

Pricing objectives

A pricing objective is what the company wants its prices to do for it. You would expect every company to have one written down. Most do not. Ask an executive and they will say "profit," but look at what the company actually does and you often find other goals at work: keeping market share, keeping the factory busy. Even firms that claim to maximize profit usually settle for a profit that is "good enough." A famous study of twenty large American companies (Lanzillotti) found that their prices were really aimed at one of five goals.

1 · Earn a target return
The goal: make a set percentage profit on the money invested, or on sales. How it works: target return pricingSetting price by adding to cost the markup needed to hit a set return on investment or on sales, assuming a standard sales volume.Works as a measure for a whole product line, not for single products, because different products deserve different returns.. Decide the profit you want, work out what markup per unit gets you there at the sales volume you expect (the standard volumeThe normal, expected level of sales used to spread costs and set targets.Target return pricing bases both its cost figures and its profit goal on it.), and add that markup to each product's cost. "Investment" means the owners' money plus long-term debt. Where it works: as a check on a whole product line. Where it fails: on individual products, because you may deliberately price one item high to make the line look premium and another low to bring customers in, so they cannot all earn the same return. Two further headaches: do you value your assets at what you paid or what they are worth now, and how do you split the target across products that differ in investment, share, image and competition? Expecting the same return from every product every year is a mistake.
2 · Win or hold market share
The goal: a bigger, or at least a safe, slice of the market, because big-share firms are usually the most profitable. Offensive version: cut price to win share, and let the volume drive costs down until the low price is profitable. Henry Ford did it with the Model T against his engineers' advice and took half the market; Black & Decker did it in power tools. Defensive version: a small firm far behind the leader just tries to hold enough share to survive. The reverse: for a dog in a dying market, let share fall on purpose and take the short-term profit.
3 · Meet the competition
The goal: stay in line with everyone else. Price at about the industry average, a little above or below for any special feature. What it really means: the firm has no price policy of its own. The market gives it the price, and it works backwards, cutting costs until it can make money at that price. That in turn decides what materials, features and marketing it can afford. Who does it: small firms that prefer to follow the leader, and large firms selling standardized products, such as commodities, where nothing sets them apart.
4 · Maximize profit book only
The goal: the highest profit possible. Economics assumes every firm wants this, and in a competitive market it is even healthy, because high profits attract new competitors who bring prices back down. The reality: firms rarely admit to it, because it sounds greedy. And Cyert and March argue they do not actually do it: they practise satisficingAiming for a satisfactory profit rather than the maximum possible.Cyert and March's term. The book argues most firms that claim to maximize profit actually satisfice, because true maximizing is impossible to calculate., aiming for a satisfactory profit rather than the maximum. The book agrees that true maximizing is impossible in practice. A firm with many products cannot even tell how a change in output affects the cost of one of them, let alone which of a thousand possible actions would squeeze out the most profit.
5 · Keep prices stable
The goal: steady prices over the long run, even when material or labour costs wobble. How it works: the firm charges what it considers a fair price rather than the most the market would pay, and changes prices rarely and by small amounts. Who does it: large, well-known companies in the public eye. Why: steady prices build trust and goodwill and make the firm look concerned for the public.

A quick way to hold the five: two are about money (target return, maximize profit), two are about position (market share, meet competition), one is about reputation (stabilize prices).

Pricing strategies

A pricing strategy is the firm's price position relative to competitors. A high-price strategy differentiates the product and builds a quality image. A low-price strategy aims for market share by penetrating markets. Either shapes the rest of the mix. Six named strategies: two high-price, then four low-price in rising order of aggression.

01

Skimming

Charge the highest possible price in the short run. The product is an innovation or a desirable variation, so it enjoys a temporary monopoly and demand is price inelastic at first. Short run, because competitors will arrive and end the premium. Why: R&D and launch promotion are expensive and early production runs cost more per unit, so high margins recover the investment fast; the high price builds a quality image; and it is easier to lower a price later than to raise it. SkimmingLaunching at the highest price the market will bear, then lowering it over time as competitors arrive.Works for innovative products with inelastic demand. It recovers R&D fast, builds a quality image, and it is easier to cut a price later than to raise one. also segments the market: each price cut brings in the next segment. Dealers like the high markup. Best for conspicuous products (digital watches at launch), where quality differences are large or hard to judge, and for designer labels.

02

Prestige pricing

Unlike skimming, the high price stays high for the product's whole life, because the price itself is part of why people buy. Luxury cars, cameras, perfumes, liquor: much of the satisfaction is owning something expensive. Cutting the price has repeatedly reduced sales: new buyers gained are fewer than upper-income buyers lost. Rising incomes have dulled price sensitivity, and the book suggests renaming it quality pricing, which points the customer at how the product satisfies rather than at status.

03

Penetration pricing

A low price to enter a market and win a large share. Needs a market that rewards low prices and the capacity to meet the demand created. 04

Expansionistic pricing

Penetration taken further: a very low price to build a mass market where demand is highly elastic. Toyota and Honda entered the United States with standardized low-cost Corollas and Civics, then added dearer models. Preemptive pricing (stay-out pricing)

A low price that makes the market unattractive to would-be competitors. Best when the firm has no patent, no differential advantage and entry is easy. Delaying rivals buys time to build share, cut costs and become the recognized original. A price set low, usually below cost, to eliminate competitors, accepting losses until they are gone, then raising prices. Discounts and allowances

A manufacturer depends on its distributors, who negotiate hard for terms. To pay them for their services and keep their cooperation, manufacturers offer discounts off list price. Five kinds: quantity, trade (functional), seasonal, promotional and cash. Each has its own purpose, and a buyer who qualifies can receive all five in one transaction.

Quantity discounts

A quantity discountA lower price for buying more, based on units or on dollar value.Saves the seller shipping, billing and handling costs. Legal only if it can be justified by those cost savings. is the simplest idea in the chapter: buy more, pay less per unit. It can be measured in units or in dollars, and given as a percentage off, as free extra units, or as a fixed amount off. Both sides gain. The seller ships one big order instead of many small ones, so it saves on shipping, order processing and billing, runs the factory more evenly, and ties up less money in stock. The buyer gets a lower price per unit.

There are two kinds, and the exam likes the difference. The question to ask is: what is the discount measured on?

Noncumulative quantity discount
Measured on: one single order. Buy 25 tons today and you get the 25-ton discount, whatever you bought last month. Purpose: make each order bigger. Why the seller likes it: one large shipment genuinely costs less to sell, store, process and deliver than several small ones. The book's example, a linoleum maker's schedule: 1 to 5 tons, no discount; 6 to 10 tons, 2 percent; 11 to 20 tons, 3 percent; 21 to 30 tons, 4 percent; over 30 tons, 5 percent. Setting the first break: put it where the seller's saving from a bigger order is about equal to the discount that pays the buyer for holding the extra stock. A schedule with breaks placed to favour a few big buyers looks like discrimination.
Cumulative quantity discount
Measured on: everything the buyer purchases over a period, usually a year, added up. Small orders are fine; the discount grows as the year's total grows. Purpose: loyalty. It rewards the buyer for staying with the same supplier all year. When it suits: products buyers will not stock in bulk. Fashion that changes fast, technology that dates old stock, perishables, anything expensive to store. The seller stops caring about order size and cares about the year's total instead.

Now the legal part. Charging different buyers different prices for the same product is exactly what the Robinson-Patman Act forbids, and a quantity discount does exactly that. So when is a discount allowed? Think of it as three rules for a seller.

Rule 1 · You may discount only when it saves you money
The rule: a discount is legal up to the amount the buyer's behaviour actually saves you. Allowed: a buyer orders in bulk, which saves you shipping and order-handling costs, and you pass those savings on. This is the cost-savings defence, and the Federal Trade Commission accepts it for selling and physical distribution costs because shipping records prove them. Not allowed: made-up savings on the factory side. You cannot say "the first batch has already paid for the plant, so the extra units cost us nothing and we can sell them cheap." Courts require overhead to be spread evenly over every unit, so every product carries its share of the fixed costs.
Rule 2 · The "match the competitor" excuse is one-time only
The rule: if a buyer tells you a competitor will sell to them for less, you may drop your price to that buyer to keep them. This is the good-faith defenceArguing that you cut your price for one buyer only to match an equally low price a competitor offered that buyer.Accepted case by case, but it cannot justify a whole discount system.. "Our competitors are cheap, so everyone gets the low price forever" is not good faith, and the courts have refused to let the defence cover a whole discount schedule.
Rule 3 · Different prices are not a crime by themselves
The rule: unequal prices to different buyers are not automatically illegal. A discount schedule breaks the law only if someone sues and proves that it lessened competition or injured other suppliers. Allowed: charging a big buyer less than a small one, as long as no one can show the market was damaged by it. Not allowed: unequal pricing that provably drives competitors out or ruins the market.

Pricing of Services

Services are priced differently because they are different: you cannot see one before you buy, store one for later, or make two exactly alike. This chapter covers the six characteristics that cause the trouble, how to cost a service, and five strategies and five tactics for pricing one.

Characteristics of services

The chapter opens with Marshall McLuhan: "We say airline when we mean air transportation... services are processes, not objects." A service cannot be grasped or pointed at; it has to be experienced. It is sold first, then produced and consumed. Goods and services mix: goods give tangibility to services (a souvenir from a show), services augment goods (customer service with a car). Six differences create special problems:

Intangibility
The fundamental difference. Every product mixes tangible and intangible; a service is dominated by the intangible. A diamond's worth is in cut, clarity, colour and carat, and the customer can picture it. Travel counselling is information and arrangements; the only tangible parts are tickets and forms. Products sit on a scale: travel is more intangible than interior decorating, which is more intangible than a home security system. Pricing implication: the more intangible the service, the less customers can compare it with competitors, so the seller has more latitude in pricing. A financial database has more freedom than a maintenance service. The cost: value is harder to communicate and the price harder to justify.
Simultaneous production and consumption
Goods are produced, sold, then consumed. Services are sold, then produced and consumed at once. So there is usually no inventory (information services excepted), and the customer is part of production. Either the provider goes to the customer or the customer comes to a location. Pricing implication: when the service travels, fixed costs are recovered through a service-call charge or hookup fee; when the customer travels, location costs are handled as in a factory.
Perishability
Services cannot be stored. Tonight's empty airline seat or motel room cannot be sold tomorrow, so supply and demand are hard to match. Peak demand: hire part-timers, work overtime, serve regulars first, turn business away, subcontract. Low demand: cut prices, offer other services that use idle resources, sell harder. Each adds cost and lowers profit contribution, and the cost of handling a peak rarely equals the cost of filling a trough. Pricing implication: the marketer works with two estimates of profit contribution. For market-oriented pricing use the lower one to find break-even; for cost-plus use the higher direct cost.
Heterogeneity
Performance varies by provider, customer and day. The more labour-intensive the service, the more it varies, because staff use judgment. Hair stylists have wide latitude; airlines and spectator sports are standardized. Pricing implication: the more customized the service, the less its costs can be standardized. One answer is standard costs for a menu of options, priced by the package chosen. But customers decide the price-value trade-off, and no two customized services look alike to them: lawn treatment options are standard across firms, but the advice, care and call-backs of the person doing it are not, and those add value. Where staff influence is low, as with software, price options separately and in packages (Table 13.1 below).
Complexity and divergence
Two ways of describing the shape of a service, explained in the next section. ComplexityThe number of steps, options and sequences in delivering a service.Medical care is complex; lawn care is simple. Adding services increases complexity; specializing reduces it. is how many steps and options the service has. DivergenceHow much a service varies because staff use judgment and adapt to each customer.More divergence means more customization at a higher price; less means uniform service at a lower price and higher volume. is how much those steps change from one customer to the next.

Complexity and divergence

Any service can be written out as a list of steps. A haircut is a short list: sit down, discuss, cut, finish, pay. A hospital stay is a long one with many branches. The book uses two words for the shape of that list.

Complexity is how many steps, options and sequences the service contains. Medical care is highly complex. Lawn care is simple. The more a service does, the more complex it is.

Divergence is how much the steps vary from one customer to the next, because the person delivering the service uses judgment and adapts. A hair stylist decides something different for every head, so styling is highly divergent. An airline check-in is the same for everyone, so it has low divergence. A service can be complex but not divergent (a standard blood test panel, many steps but always the same ones) or simple but divergent (a personal shopper).

Why a pricing chapter cares: a firm can choose to move either dial up or down, and each move points to a different price.

More complexity
The firm adds services or enhances the ones it has, to earn more from each customer. Barbers became salons by adding colouring, manicures and skin care. The price of each added service, sold as part of the package, has to come in below what a specialist would charge for it alone, or customers will go to the specialist.
Less complexity
The firm specializes and does fewer things. Doing one thing well reads as expertise, and expertise supports a premium price.
More divergence
The firm gives its people more freedom to tailor the service to each customer. That is customization, and customers pay more for it. The price is set by what the market will pay for a tailored service over the standard version.
Less divergence
The firm makes the service the same for everyone. That allows volume and economies of scale, especially where equipment is involved, as with an airline or a dry cleaner. The price comes down and the service becomes more widely available.

The short version to remember: adding steps means bundling them cheaply, removing steps means charging for expertise, adding variation means charging for customization, and removing variation means charging less and selling more.

Pricing decisions in services

Service marketers must deliver features and benefits at an attractive price. Attractive means either a competitive price, lower than competitors' for the same service, or a premium price more than offset by added value. Whatever the service, customers judge three things: quality (does it achieve the promised result?), consistency (is it the same every time?) and reliability (does the provider always deliver what was promised?). An attractive price is not a low one; it is strong value against those three.

Cost considerations

Service pricing has traditionally been cost-based. Because ordinary accounting does not value the people who deliver a service, service firms invented human-resource costing. The job-jacketA costing method that charges each client for the time actually spent on their account, plus a share of overhead and idle time.Common in law and consulting. The weakness: time is a poor measure of skill, since a better lawyer may take fewer hours. method charges each account for the time spent on it, plus a share of overhead and unused time in proportion to that time.

Two difficulties. Is time a fair measure of effort? A more competent person finishes faster and so charges less than a slower, less competent one; time standards, as in car repair, help only a little. And fixed costs are hard to allocate when volume swings. Many firms sidestep both by charging a multiple of direct time costs. The deeper problem: a cost-based price may not match what the customer thinks the service is worth. Too high loses sales; too low loses profit, and perhaps sales, because customers expected more. Even equipment-intensive services, where allocation is easier, get supply and demand wrong: buying the wrong capacity (airlines and jumbo jets), not adding capacity, unbalancing primary and support activities at a resort, or undercutting themselves with cheaper services that parallel dearer ones.

Market considerations

Because service costs are hard to tie to units, and because price strongly affects how often a service is used, service prices are more often based on perceived value than on cost. Successful service firms innovate constantly: each new feature adds value, and adding associated goods adds tangibility (Walt Disney does both). Value pricing estimates what the market thinks the service is worth, so innovations must match the target market's concerns. ServiceMaster keeps enhancing its contract housekeeping for cost-conscious hospitals without cutting quality. Rural/Metro, a fire-protection and ambulance provider, competes on price and service level with remote-controlled equipment, non-union firefighters and prevention. Where possible, break the service into parts: which are essential, which can go, which additions would add value. Holiday Inns kept quality, dropped the extras of expensive hotels, and improved quality through consistency.

Service pricing strategies

Price is "undoubtedly the most troublesome aspect" of marketing services, because both value and cost are hard to pin down. So service firms often just follow their major competitors, matching or slightly undercutting, which produces price tiers: an upscale restaurant and an economy one. Beyond following, five strategies.

Cost-based pricing

Cost-plus pricing charges the cost of the service plus a set percentage. Common in law, consulting and medicine, where the firm assumes it delivers distinctly better quality and that clients choose it for that. Costs also dominate when the service has high material content, which makes it more tangible (repair, installation and maintenance are priced labour plus materials), and when it is equipment-intensive, since equipment brings economies of scale (multi-screen cinemas, jumbo jets, 24-hour data processing). Entry barriersThings that make it hard for new competitors to enter a market, such as heavy equipment costs or technology.Where barriers are high, costs matter more than the market in setting service prices. push the same way: heavy equipment (excavation), many locations (airport car rental) or technology (data processing) keep competitors out, so cost matters more than the market. A data-processing firm uses cost-plus; a dry cleaner does not. The limitation is the usual one: it ignores what customers will pay.

Demand fluctuation pricing

Demand for services swings outside the firm's control. Differential pricingCharging different prices to different customers or at different times for the same service.For services it appears twice: lower off-peak prices to shift demand, and different prices by willingness to pay as a tactic. charges less in non-peak periods to shift demand out of the peaks and perhaps raise total demand: matinees, weekend phone and hotel rates, off-peak electricity. The risk is that the lower price cuts total profit. Creating off-peak demand can produce incremental losses if handling the shifted demand costs more than it earns, as with airlines' cheaper Sunday fares.

Developmental pricingA special low-priced version of a service created for off-peak periods to bring in extra demand.Market-oriented, not cost-oriented. Only the extra costs of serving the extra guests need to be covered. The risk is that peak business just moves to the cheap period. creates a special version of the service for off-peak times, such as a resort's off-season package. Its purpose is to increase demand. Basic costs are already covered by peak-season prices, so the off-season price need only cover the incremental cost of the package. The added profit can raise profitability or be used to lower peak prices. The risk is a flip-flop: if the conventions that filled July and August move to the cheap season, the resort does the same business for much less revenue. The slides' rule: off-peak pricing must create or shift demand, and the extra contribution must exceed the incremental cost.

Capacity pricing

This one is for services that run on expensive equipment: airlines, hotels, data centres, resorts. The problem is that the equipment costs the same whether it is busy or idle, so the cost of serving one customer depends entirely on how many others turn up. The book's numbers make it concrete. A data-processing centre needs its equipment to earn $1,000 a day. On a slow day it handles 4,000 transactions, so each one has to carry 25 cents of equipment cost. On a busy day it handles 20,000, so each one carries only 5 cents. Same machine, same day rate, a fivefold difference in cost per transaction. An airline flying 30 passengers in 120 seats has the same problem.

So what price do you charge? If you price for the slow day you are far above competitors on every other day. If you price for the busy day you lose money whenever it is quiet. Capacity pricingSetting a price for an equipment-based service that earns the desired return on the equipment while staying competitive, given how much of the capacity gets used.Steps: find the competitive price, split costs into equipment return and people costs, then work out how many units you need to sell at that price. is the book's way out: instead of asking "what does a transaction cost?", ask "at the price the market will accept, how many transactions do we need to earn our return?" Three steps.

Step 1. Find the competitive price
Look at what competitors charge for the equivalent service. It is usually a range, not one number. In the example, competitors charge between 7.5 and 9 cents a transaction, and the biggest one charges 8.2.
Step 2. Split your daily cost in two
One part is the return you need on the equipment. The other is the people cost of running it. Then ask of each part: is it essential, could it be cut, could productivity reduce it? In the example the equipment return is $1,000 a day, or $900 as an absolute minimum; the people cost is $200 a day, or $180 if a 10 percent productivity gain is achieved. So the daily total to cover is somewhere between $1,080 and $1,200.
Step 3. Divide
Daily cost to cover, divided by the competitive price per transaction, gives the number of transactions you need per day to break even at that price. At the best case ($1,080 to cover, 9 cents a transaction) you need 12,000. At the worst case ($1,200 to cover, 7.5 cents) you need 16,000.

Now management knows something useful: with competitive prices, the centre must handle between 12,000 and 16,000 transactions a day to be worth running. If demand is usually above that, price at the market rate and relax. If it is usually below, the answer is not a higher price, which the market would refuse, but more volume: off-peak discounts, longer hours, productivity improvements, or extra capacity. The book's Table 13.2 shows the same thinking at a resort whose golf course is full every July afternoon: rather than build a second course that would lose money the rest of the year, it opens earlier, leases a nearby course, runs a shuttle and discounts morning play.

Benefit pricing

Every method so far has started from cost, at least in part. Benefit pricingPricing a service by what its benefits are worth to the customer, with no link to what it costs to provide.If the benefit price is too low to pay, add features that justify more. Customers judge benefits through two things they can see: the physical setting and the contact staff. throws cost out. Its starting assumption is that what a customer is willing to pay for a service has nothing to do with what it costs to provide. The price comes from the benefits the customer perceives, full stop.

That sounds like it could go wrong, and the book says what to do when it does. If the price customers will pay does not cover your costs or your desired return, you do not raise the price, because they will not pay it. You add benefits until the service is worth more to them. One financial-information firm did exactly that: its data service alone would not command the price it needed, so it added free consultations and guaranteed a high level of accuracy, and the price followed.

How do you work out what the benefits are worth? The book gives a sequence.

Define the service and why it exists
Write down what the service does for the customer and what need it satisfies. Everything else follows from this.
Define what the customer actually experiences
A customer never sees the back office. They see two things: the physical support, meaning the equipment, materials, decor, layout and location, and the contact personnelThe employees a customer actually deals with during a service.One of the two visible parts of a service, with the physical setting. They shape most of the customer's sense of value., the people they deal with. Almost all of the customer's sense of value comes through these two, so both must match the price. An expensive restaurant needs decor and service to match its prices, or the customer feels cheated. Decide what each contact person does and how they hand the customer on to each other. And if a machine replaces a person, as when banks introduced ATMs, teach the customer why that is better, or they will see it as less.
Compare each benefit with competitors
Go through the observable features one by one. Where you have a clear advantage, that feature earns a premium. Where you match competitors, it earns the market price. Where you are behind, it must be priced below the market.
Put a price on each benefit and add them up
Estimate what each feature is worth. Sometimes the market tells you directly: if one motel includes breakfast and a competitor does not, the difference in room rates is the price of breakfast. Where you cannot isolate a feature, group features together or estimate from the usual markup on cost. The sum is the price of the service.
If the margin is too thin
Two options. Deliver the same benefits more cheaply, through productivity or lower-cost ways of providing them. Or sell more volume, remembering that more volume strains scheduling and can add cost. One fix for that is to make scheduling itself a visible benefit, with communication equipment and personal booking, and charge for it.

The difference between the two methods in one line: capacity pricing takes the market price as given and asks how many customers you need; benefit pricing takes the customer's valuation as given and asks what you must add to earn the price you want.

Pricing tactics for services

Tactics are the same for services as for goods. Five are named:

Differential pricing
Different prices for the same work according to each customer's willingness to pay. The most common tactic in pest control.
Discount pricing
Temporary reductions to increase business, attract trial or build a low-cost image. A restaurant discounts early meals so new customers sample the food and return at full price.
Guaranteed pricing
Payment linked to achieving specified results.
Loss-leader pricing
A reduced price to win the first order, hoping for further business at the regular price.
Offset pricing
A low price on the core service and relatively high prices on the necessary extras.

Pricing During Inflationary and Shortage Conditions

Slides: Module 05, Bangladesh context · Book: Hanna and Dodge, Chapter 14. The slides follow the book and add Bangladesh examples.

Everything so far assumed stable costs. This chapter asks what a manager does when costs rise fast and inputs are scarce. The answer is not just "raise prices." It is a programme: better information, honest accounting, a deliberate decision about who absorbs the cost, protection in contracts, cost cutting, and careful handling of government, customers and dealers.

Why inflation and shortages are a marketing problem

The chapter opens with Kotler and Levy on demarketingMarketing designed to reduce demand, used when a firm cannot supply everyone.Kotler and Levy's term, in the chapter's opening quote: excess demand is as much a marketing problem as excess supply.: "excess demand is as much a marketing problem as excess supply." When there is not enough to go round, a company still has to decide which customers to serve and how. Inflation and scarcity are permanent features of business, the book says: world demand for raw materials keeps growing, commodity prices spiral, and developed countries depend on developing ones for basics like oil. The main worry is the push on inflation.

Two explanations. Cost-push inflationPrices rise because the cost of materials, labour or capital rises and gets passed on.One of the two explanations of inflation. For pricing, inflation is a trap. Wages and materials rise, so firms pass the increases into prices, then find volume falls. If competitors delay their increases, the firm loses customers. If everyone raises prices together, customers switch to substitutes. Shortages add uncertainty about future costs and supply. Pricing based on stable historical costs stops working. Survival depends on recognizing the changes quickly and adapting. The rest of the chapter is the list of responses.

Cost and information management

The first response is better information, not higher prices.

Continuous monitoring of costs

A costing system built for stable times fails when costs move fast; extrapolating historical costs distorts profit. The firm needs to see cost changes for each product, product group, market, key customer and operation, tracked by cost centreA part of the business whose costs are tracked separately.Under inflation you need to see costs move in each centre, not rely on old averages.. A manufacturer should watch material usage, output, direct wages, labour productivity and machine utilization regularly. The slides' Bangladesh example: a food manufacturer tracks packaging, edible inputs, transport and production costs separately before revising a price.

Dynamic costing and rolling budgets

Computer systems can compare the current purchase price of every component with its last price, daily or monthly. The result is either a revised price list or a decision to accept lower margins for a while. Some firms build a multiplier factorA number built into the base price that can be adjusted regularly as costs change.A simple price-protection device. Change the multiplier, not the whole price list. into the base price and adjust it periodically. Others use rolling or progressive budgets, extended every month so new cost and demand data is always in. In Bangladesh, monthly or shorter rolling reviews beat an annual budget in volatile periods.

Information systems to monitor the environment

Management needs to know where the economy is heading, to decide whether to expand, cut back or diversify, and what materials, labour and energy will cost and whether to substitute. It must also watch customers: demands for more quality, greater price consciousness, preference for energy-saving products, interest in safety. Flexible manufacturing, shorter runs, just-in-time inventory and close monitoring of competitors' prices, discounts and promotions all matter more; the book cites car rebates, airline fare changes and grocery's shift from promotional to price discounts. The slides add a Bangladesh checklist with five sides: input (supplier quotes, import costs, exchange rate, fuel, wages), market (demand, purchasing power, substitution, basket size), competition (prices, promotions, pack sizes, channel margins), operations (capacity utilization, wastage, productivity, inventory, lead times), and decision (price change, cost reduction, product change, or nothing?).

Cost reduction

Normally profit is kept up through higher prices, lower costs, more volume, or a mix. Under inflation, increases cannot be fully passed on and prices cannot rise fast enough, so cost reduction becomes the main pricing strategy, especially to hold price and protect volume. The book's list:

  • Shorten the product line. Drop the "losers" so their materials go into profitable items. Weak products earn a little but eat a disproportionate share of resources and management time. General Electric competes only where its position allows a given level of profit.
  • Find lower-cost substitutes, as long as quality reputation is protected. Car makers replaced steel with aluminium, plastics and rubber.
  • Simplify the product. Redesign around standard components and reduce model changes. Allocation of strategic commodities and quantity restrictions hit operations directly; export controls shrink the market, import controls may open domestic opportunities, environmental controls raise costs. Monetary and fiscal policy affect demand indirectly. Know the rules, anticipate changes, prepare contingency plans.

Centralizing pricing decisions

Centralizing pricingMaking pricing decisions at head office rather than leaving them to individual salespeople or regions.Gives control and speed, which flexible pricing needs. A price response to a competitor may have to be made in days. gives control, consistency and speed. It matters most under flexible pricing: a decision to cut prices in response to a competitor must be made promptly or share is lost. The Bangladesh version: central pricing rules, with field teams given limited, defined authority for local conditions.

Public information and customer communication

Customers resist changes they do not understand, especially in price. Explain actions promptly and give advance notice; this builds the image of a concerned participant in the economy. Advertising can show customers how to get more use from the product and how to use scarce materials well, which reduces resistance to higher prices. Explain rather than let rumours do it; in Bangladesh, communicate price and pack changes consistently through retailers and official channels.

Closer relationships with resellers

Monitor goods through the channel and tell dealers about policy changes promptly. The salesperson carries the price change: explaining why costs rose, how the company cut its own costs to limit the increase, and what cheaper substitutes it offers. Helping resellers with their own problems turns shortages into goodwill. Review cooperative advertising, credit terms, discounts, freight and service policies; inflation may be the moment to negotiate longer contracts and fewer discounts. And listen to what dealers report about customer and competitor reactions.

Avoiding scarcity tactics

Some manufacturers exploit the psychology of scarcityThe way buyers change behaviour when they believe a product is running short: hoarding, panic buying, paying more.Using it deliberately can create a real shortage and damage inventories, sales and trust. The book says avoid it.: when buyers think a product is running short they hoard and panic-buy, which can create a real shortage and push prices above normal. Doing this deliberately damages inventories, production, sales and profit in the long run. The slides pose the discussion: is a higher price always unethical in a shortage, and when does cost recovery become exploitation?

A need for a pricing strategy during inflation

A firm without a deliberate inflation and shortage pricing policy is "at the mercy of every shortage." Without one, firms scramble for scarce materials at any price and hope to charge more, and the result is lost volume, or government legislation to curb the trend. It is hard to resist "demanding what the traffic will bear," but the cumulative effect can endanger the economic system itself. So every company needs an inflation pricing plan, and producers of essential materials may need industry-wide cooperation on pricing that serves the economy's social needs. The slides' final principle: a deliberate policy, not opportunistic reaction to each shortage.

Recall in one line each

  • First response is information: monitor costs by cost centre, dynamic costing, multiplier factors, rolling budgets, environmental intelligence.
  • FIFO overstates profit under inflation (paper profits); LIFO charges near-current costs. Inflationary accounting revalues assets; traditional depreciation assumes replacement costs the same.
  • Absorb (protect volume, thinner margin) or pass on (protect margin, risk share). Often absorb part. Flexible pricing = short-run, aggressive cuts to hold share, where fixed overhead is high.

  • Cost reduction: shorten the line · substitutes · simplify · keep customers ($1 versus $6) · improve quality (hidden plant 25 to 35 percent) · review packaging.
