Why Psychology Moves Price

Smith, Chapter 5 · part 1 of 3

Influencing price acceptance

How customers judge value and price is shaped by many factors, and a lot of them are psychological, some probably rooted in evolution. The module's stance is practical: rather than trying to make buyers more logical, it is usually more efficient to work with the way they already think. That means four jobs:

Four jobs
  • Understand the decision biases in purchasing behaviour
  • Find ways to reduce psychological dissonance
  • Understand the limits of pricing power
  • And so make it easier for customers to decide to buy

The brain as a predictive machine

Neuroeconomics suggests the brain constantly predicts what to expect. Shown a chair you have never seen, you still guess its function, weight and rough price, from very little information. The circuits doing this turn information into impressions, preferences, judgements and predictions, and therefore into willingness to payThe most a customer is prepared to pay for something.The psychological effects work by shifting it.. People are cognitive misers: they save mental effort by relying on heuristics, mental shortcuts, to decide faster.

The slogan

It is your brain too. Use yours, don't fix theirs. Some effects lower price sensitivityHow strongly a customer's buying reacts to a change in price.Every effect in this module either raises or lowers it. and can be steered by the firm, which builds pricing power. Others warn the firm where a pricing practice will backfire.

The seventeen effects

The whole module hangs on one map, repeated through the slides. Learn the three groups first, then the effects inside each.

True economic costs

The first four effects look psychological but are really rational. Each comes from a hidden cost, usually an opportunity costThe value of the best alternative given up, such as the time spent shopping.The true economic cost effects all rest on it., that a sensible buyer would weigh.

1 Shared cost effect

Price sensitivity falls when the customer pays with someone else's money. A business traveller reimbursed by the company cares less about the fare; that is why airline loyalty schemes let airlines charge a little more, since the firm pays but the traveller chooses. Rebates work the same way to a smaller degree, because part of the price is effectively paid back.

Four funds of money
1Own money, on themselvesCareful about getting the most utilityThe satisfaction or benefit a person gets from something.Classical economics assumes people maximise it. for each unit of money spent.
2Own money, on someone elseStill price sensitive, but now trying to please the recipient and get the satisfaction of giving. The meaning of utility changes.
3Someone else's money, on themselvesFocused on benefits, and much less price sensitive.
4Someone else's money, on someone elseBuying for a business, say. Without oversight and the right incentives, neither price nor benefit sensitive.

The industrial lesson: because business purchases spend other people's money, they involve several decision makers and several decision criteria.

2 Switching costs

Economic switching costs are all the costs, direct and opportunity, of moving from one supplier to another. They arise from product-specific investments the buyer has made, which can be monetary or psychological. The key line: product-specific investments decrease price sensitivity for existing customers.

Sources
Complementary goodsBuy an iPod, then a docking station for it. The dock makes the next player likely to be an iPod too.
LearningTime spent mastering Dreamweaver would be lost by moving to Pagemaker.
Engineering costsA chip designed into a product: changing it means re-engineering, re-certification and other direct costs.
Brand loyaltyThe purely psychological cost of leaving a brand you trust.

3 Expenditure effect

The bigger the price tag, the more it pays to shop around. Searching and comparing take time and effort that could be spent elsewhere. On an expensive item that effort is small next to the possible saving, so buyers compare hard; on a cheap item the effort stays the same while the saving shrinks, so they don't bother. Price sensitivity is higher when the purchase is a larger share of the budget. In business markets, a larger or more strategically important purchase draws more scrutiny and so more price sensitivity.

The book's caveat

The effect is not always consistent. Told that a $39.99 microwave is $5 cheaper two miles away, more people make the trip than for the same $5 off a $499.99 TV. Prospect theory explains why: $5 feels bigger against a small price.

4 Difficult comparison effect

Making products and prices hard to compare raises search costs and the buyer's uncertainty. That discourages switching and lowers price sensitivity.

Three areas
Incumbent vs new entrantIncumbents blur price and benefits so switching takes more information gathering and feels riskier. Entrants do the opposite and make the comparison explicit. AT&T's base price plus a list of add-ons against Vonage's single flat fee is the textbook case.
Brands vs genericsBranded and generic aspirin have chemically identical active ingredients, yet buyers trust the brand and pay more for it. The brand itself makes comparison harder. Whole Foods: Quaker Oats $3.49, its own 365 brand $2.99.
Size changesIn a Prague pub a large beer (0.5 L) costs 20 CzK and a small (0.3 L) 14 CzK. The small looks cheaper, but costs 4.7 CzK per decilitre against 4.0 for the large. A higher unit price behind a lower-looking price.

Perceptual Challenges

Smith, Chapter 5 · part 2 of 3

Effects 5 to 9 are purely psychological: no hidden cost explains them.

5 Price endings in 9s

If nothing psychological were going on, each digit would end a price about 10% of the time. Count real prices and 9 dominates by far, with far fewer 5s and 0s. The effect even reaches past the last digit. Of $24.99, $28.53 and $29.99, the lowest sells most, as theory predicts, but $29.99 outsells $28.53 even though it is dearer. That kink contradicts the downward-sloping demand curve.

Four reasons
MeaningA 9 ending signals a low or discounted price, which lifts demand. The flip side is that it can suggest lower quality, which is why galleries price in round numbers and poster shops end in 9.
AccessibilityRound prices ending in 0 or 5 are easy to store and recall, which makes comparison shopping easier. Odd endings are harder to remember, so they quietly reduce comparison and price sensitivity.
EncodingWe read numbers left to right and the left digit matters most. A busy buyer reads the first digit and drops the rest, so $9.99 registers as much cheaper than $10. This is the underestimation effectPerceiving $9.99 as much cheaper than $10 because the left digit is read first.Part of the encoding explanation for 9 endings..
FramingA 9 ending feels like a small discount off the next round number, a little gain that nudges the purchase.
Two extras from the book book only

The old story that odd prices stopped cashiers pocketing exact change is discredited: it would make every odd digit common, not just 9. And the habit is cultural: post-Soviet Poland favoured 5s, and Japan and other Asian markets favour 8, a lucky number.

6 Fairness effect

Seen as fair
Prices within the range the customer expects from past experience of the category.
Seen as unfair
Large price increases, read as profit taking or as exploiting weaker customers; and prices that vary randomly between customers. Amazon was hit by this in 2005 when shoppers discovered it was testing different prices on different people.
Where it bites
Much more in necessity goods than in discretionary purchases. The expectation of fairness grows with economic sophistication and shapes how price changes, price differences and discrimination are received.

The book's reason: the brain handles buying with the same circuits used for sharing within families and friends, so people bring expectations of fair sharing to every transaction. Breaking them can turn into public outrage, not just a lost sale.

7 Overconfidence in future economic efficiency

People buy some products expecting to behave a certain way later, and they are consistently bad at predicting their own future behaviour. The gym study: members on a $70 monthly plan went about 4.3 times a month, paying over $17 a visit, when a 10-visit pass cost $10 a visit. They were also 17% more likely to stay past the first year than annual members, despite paying extra for the right to cancel at any time. The cause was overconfidence in their own future self-control and efficiency.

8 Small pie bias

From negotiation research. Negotiators keep underestimating the size of the bargaining zoneThe range between the seller's lowest and the buyer's highest acceptable price.Small pie bias makes negotiators think it is narrower than it is.: they think they are splitting a smaller pie than really exists. So they overestimate the share of the surplus they are getting, and sellers settle for too low a price. They also tend to anchor their expectation near their own walk-away price.

How to overcome it
Form an expectation of the buyer's reservation priceThe highest price a buyer will accept, or the lowest a seller will.Anchoring and small pie bias both act on it., then use the negotiation to seek disconfirming information. Open with an offer outside what you think the buyer will accept, watch the reaction, update your estimate, and move the deal closer to the buyer's true reservation price.

9 Promotional influence

What you say, and who you say it to, decide how good a price you can capture.

1 The message
Price-oriented promotionFocuses on the brand and its price, typically in local circulars run with distributors. Leads to lower prices and higher price sensitivity.
Value-oriented promotionFocuses on the brand and its features or benefits, typically national advertising run by the manufacturer. Leads to lower price sensitivity.
2 The market attracted
Advertising enlarges the market by drawing in new buyers, and advertising-sensitive buyers tend to be more price sensitive. So advertising indirectly makes the market as a whole more price sensitive.
In negotiations
Leaning on key selling points lets sellers reach higher final prices.

  • Meaning, accessibility, encoding, framing
  • When prices feel fair and unfair; necessity vs discretionary
  • The gym study numbers

Prospect Theory and Related Effects

Smith, Chapter 5 · part 3 of 3

10 Prospect theory

Economics assumes people maximise utility in every transaction. Study after study shows predictable ways they do not. Prospect theory, set out by Kahneman and Tversky in 1979, tries to define the heuristics behind that predictable irrationality by asking how people choose among risky prospects. Since every purchase carries some risk, it explains many buying behaviours and so many pricing opportunities. Newer research links it to brain circuits shaped by evolution.

Which offer would you accept?

Option 1Option 2
The offer50% chance of losing €10,000Losing €4,500 with certainty
Expected loss€5,000€4,500
What many chooseThis one, the gamble

The sure loss is cheaper on average, yet many people take the gamble. Facing a loss, people seek risk. That is a utility-maximising rule broken in a predictable direction, which is exactly what prospect theory describes.

The four findings

Four findings
Losses weigh heavier than gainsLosing a sum hurts more than gaining the same sum pleases. The curve for losses is much steeper than the curve for gains.
Inflection at the point of referenceGains and losses are judged from a reference point, the perceived status quo. Because losses from it are avoided and gains above it discounted, buyers show a status quo biasThe tendency to stick with what you already have.It follows from losses weighing more than gains., replacing items with copies of the last one.
Diminishing sensitivityWe notice changes, not absolute levels. The gap between 3° and 6° is easier to feel than between 13° and 16°; a gain of 100 vs 200 feels bigger than 1,100 vs 1,200. The same holds for losses.
Risk aversion in the positive frame, risk seeking in the negativeAlso called the reflection effect: choices about losses mirror choices about gains. People avoid risk when it could cost them something pleasant and take risk when it could spare them something painful. Certainty makes losses feel worse and gains feel better.

Prescriptions

The slides and the book each turn the findings into pricing advice. They overlap, so learn them side by side.

From the slidesWhy it works
Minimise pain, highlight gainA $10 loss hurts more than a $10 gain pleases, so shrink the price in the buyer's mind and enlarge the benefit.
Bundle pain, unbundle gainTwo $5 losses hurt more than one $10 loss, and two $5 gains please more than one $10 gain. Charge one total; list every benefit separately.
Reframe pains as gainsPeople fear a 5% chance of missing out on $50 more than they welcome a 95% chance of getting it. Present the offer as something gained.
Price guaranteesPromising to match or beat the lowest price removes the buyer's doubt about getting the best deal, so they buy sooner and with confidence. Result: higher volume, and competition shifts away from price.
Manage the reference pointPut the product in the buyer's hands, as bazaar sellers do, to trigger the endowment effect. Or ask them to imagine the gain: what would you do with $5,000 more? Our offer saves you that over 12 months.
From the book book onlyWhy it works
Unbundle gains, bundle lossesItemise the benefits; quote one single price.
Avoid surcharges, give discountsA surcharge feels like a penalty, a discount like a gain. Raise the regular price and offer a cash discount rather than adding a card surcharge.
Shift pain to indirect costsTime and effort hurt less than visible money. Making customers drive further, queue or assemble helps explain IKEA, Walmart and Ryanair.
Three overarching insights

Organisms get used to steady states. The response to each further change gets smaller. Pain is more urgent than pleasure.

Effects related to prospect theory

11 Reference price effects

The prices a customer has seen before in a category set the price they now expect. Buyers often cannot remember what they recently paid, but whatever they do recall still shapes their willingness to pay, their reference price. Mostly studied in frequently bought consumer goods, it is expected to hold elsewhere too.

The formula
RPt = αPt−1 + (1 − α)Pt. The reference price is a weighted blend of the last observed price and the current one. A large α means past prices dominate; a small α means the current price does.
Promotions
A discount resets expectations lower, which dampens demand later at full price. Full-price periods push expectations back up, which boosts demand during the next promotion.
The trap
Cycling between promotions and regular prices exaggerates both the spike and the slump. A firm reading only a profit-sensitivity analysis may keep promoting, with ever deeper discounts, until price and profit collapse. That is why promotions cannot be managed on numbers alone.

12 Endowment effect

People value something more once they own it. In the original experiments students were handed items at random and allowed to trade to get what they preferred, and far fewer trades happened than chance would predict.

The slide's mug study: decorated mugs worth about $5 went to a third of the group. Owners asked on average $7.12 to sell; non-owners would take only $3.12 instead of one. Simply owning the mug raised its value. This is why bazaar sellers put goods in your hands, car dealers push test drives, and trials work.

13 Anchoring

People set price expectations from information they meet early in the decision, and then find those expectations hard to change, since that means relearning or finding evidence the first impression was wrong. Anchoring strongly sets reservation prices. In negotiations the first price heard becomes the anchor, so opening higher tends to close higher.

14 Comparison set effect

The alternatives a customer is weighing shape the price they expect. Cheaper alternatives in the set pull expectations down; dearer ones push them up. So adjust the set towards a favourable group: compare a small car with a larger, pricier car, not with a motorcycle. And widen what is compared: a product with a higher upfront price but lower lifetime cost should be judged on total cost of ownership, not purchase price.

15 Framing effect

How a transaction is framed changes willingness to pay. The book's example: asked what they would pay for the same drink, people name a higher price if a friend is buying it at a smart hotel bar than at a grubby corner shop. Positive frames beat negative ones, so stress benefits over costs, and itemise the positives.

16 Order bias

Related to anchoring. The order in which prices are shown changes what people pick and what they will pay. With fast-moving consumer goods the highest average price came from presenting prices in descending order. Some studies found no significant effect for household appliances, but it is still suspected to hold beyond FMCG.

Ascending orderDescending order
NAV at $39N360 at $79
NIS at $69NIS at $69
N360 at $79NAV at $39

The same three products: on average buyers choose a dearer one when the list starts from the top. It links to loss aversion: having seen the best option first, dropping to a cheaper one feels like giving up benefits. Hence the common practice in cars, mattresses and other durables, and in business markets too, of showing the most expensive model first and working down.

17 End-benefit effect

Price sensitivity depends on how much the product contributes to the end benefit the customer really wants. A product that delivers a large part of that goal meets lower price sensitivity than one that is merely ancillary. So promote the link to the customer's goal rather than the features. Nutrisystem sells not packaged diet food but weight loss, and beyond that looking better, feeling healthier and making a good impression at special occasions.

Summary

Three closing points
  • Price cannot be optimised by numbers alone, without the qualitative influences
  • Many psychological, decision-bias, neurological and behavioural effects shape how customers perceive prices, evaluate offers and choose between them
  • Sales and marketing executives can use these effects to win price acceptance and capture more profit
