Service Innovation

Zeithaml, Chapter 8 · part 1 of 3

What service innovation is

The slides give two definitions. Learn the first one as the formal one.

Service innovation
The introduction of a new or significantly improved service concept, customer interaction channel, delivery system or underlying technology. It creates fresh value for customers and builds new capabilities for the organization.
As open innovation
A type of open innovationInnovation that draws ideas from outside the firm, especially customers, not only from its own staff.Hence the customer input in the definition. focused on developing and improving services, which brings in customer input and interactivity. It is proactive: the aim is to stay ahead of customer expectations and gain a competitive advantage.

The four examples from class, each one a service that used to need a visit:

Examples
Mobile bankingTransfer money, pay bills, recharge a phone and pay digitally from a smartphone, without going to the bank.
TelemedicineConsult a doctor over a video call or a digital platform instead of travelling to a hospital or clinic.
Ride-hailing appsBook a ride, track the driver, see the estimated fare and pay, all inside one app.
Digital hotel servicesDigital check-in, a mobile phone as the room key, and room service ordered through an app.

The book's opening case book only is PetSmart. It began as a warehouse-style pet store squeezed on margins by Walmart, then moved into grooming, training, day care and PetsHotels under a vision of "total lifetime care" for pets. Services became its growth and profit engine, but only after new store layouts and its own 400-hour groomer training programme.

Challenges of service innovation

Why is innovating a service harder than innovating a product? Services are intangibleCannot be touched, seen or tried out before buying.Why a service is hard to describe, and why words fail. and process based, so they are hard to describe. They are often cocreatedMade together by the provider and the customer as the service happens.A haircut needs you in the chair, telling the stylist what you want. with the customer, so no two deliveries are quite the same. Because a service cannot be touched or tried out in advance, people fall back on describing it in words, and words alone carry four risks.

Oversimplification
Reducing a complex service to a simple explanation and losing important details.

Class example: a university assumes students want online classes only to save time, and goes fully online, ignoring their need for face-to-face contact, practical learning and networking.

Book example: calling portfolio management “buying and selling stocks” is like calling the space shuttle “something that flies”. One person pictures a bird, another a helicopter.

Incompleteness
Deciding with incomplete information about customers, technology, competitors or the service environment. People leave out the parts of the service they do not know well.

Class example: a hospital launches online appointments but forgets elderly patients who struggle with smartphones.

Subjectivity
Deciding from personal opinion, feeling or assumption instead of reliable evidence. Everyone describes a service through their own experience and their own department.

Class example: a hotel manager who likes technology assumes guests want self-service check-in, and buys kiosks without asking them.

Biased interpretation
Reading feedback or research one-sidedly because of existing beliefs, expectations or interests. No two people define “quick” or “flexible” the same way.

Class example: a ride-sharing firm gets complaints about long waits, calls them isolated incidents and looks only at the positive reviews.

Exam tip

Give each risk its one-line meaning, then one example. The class examples are the safest to use, since they are the ones your teacher wrote.

Important considerations for service innovation

Because services are intangible, process based and built on interaction between customers and employees, service innovation is not the same as product innovation. Two things need attention.

Involve customers and employees

Customers know their needs and frustrations. Employees know the practical difficulty of delivering the service. Both should be involved when a service is designed or improved, because both take part in it.

What it achieves
Understand customer needsCustomers can say what they expect and what annoys them.
Identify service problemsEmployees can point to delays, operational difficulties and complaints that keep coming back.
Develop practical solutionsWith employees involved, new ideas are ones that can actually be delivered.
Increase acceptanceCustomers value a service built around their needs, and employees support changes they helped shape.

The book adds book only that contact employees are physically and psychologically close to customers, so they are good at spotting unmet needs. Customers can go further than giving input: they can help design the concept and the process, especially where they carry out part of the service themselves. Bank of America tested new branch services as live experiments with real customers in its Atlanta branches before rolling them out.

Employ service design thinking and techniques

Service design thinking
A human-centered approach to creating or improving a service: understand user needs, define the problem, develop ideas, test solutions, then refine the service.
Service design techniques
The practical tools used to apply that approach.
Why use it
Focuses on the customer experienceIt looks at the whole customer journeyEvery step a customer goes through with a service, from first contact to the end.Design thinking looks at all of it, not one moment., not one interaction.
Identifies service failuresIt shows where delays, confusion or dissatisfaction happen.
Encourages innovationIt helps teams come up with creative answers to service problems.
Reduces risk and costTesting on a small scale before launch catches weaknesses early.

The book book only sets out five principles that sit at the centre of service design thinking.

Five principles
User-centeredExperience and design the service through the customer's eyes.
CocreativeInclude all stakeholders in the design process.
SequencingSee the service as a sequence of interrelated actions.
EvidencingMake the intangible service visible through physical artifacts.
HolisticConsider the entire environment of the service.

Service designers talk about the customer journey, touchpointsThe points where the customer comes into contact with the firm: a desk, an app screen, a phone call.A blueprint marks each one., personasMade-up but realistic profiles of typical customers, used to design for real needs.One of the service designer's terms., cocreation and service prototypes. Their methods include customer journey mapping, storytelling, service safaris, shadowing, storyboards and service role-play. The main technique is service blueprinting, which is part 3 of this guide.

Types of service innovation

New services are not all new to the same degree. The slides list five types of service offering innovation, running from the most new to the least.

Five types
1Major innovationA completely new service, often creating a new market or meeting a need in a new way. Online telemedicine.
2New service to a market already servedA service new to the organization's existing market, even though other organizations already offer it. A traditional bank starts mobile banking for its customers.
3Service line extensionA new option or variation added to the existing service line. A dine-in restaurant adds home delivery and takeaway.
4Service improvementMaking an existing service better in quality, speed, convenience, reliability or performance. The most common type. A hotel adds online check-in.
5Style changeChanging the look, presentation or atmosphere without really changing the core service. The most modest type, but highly visible. A bank redesigns its branches.
Slides and book differ

The book lists six types, not five. It adds start-up businesses: new services for a market already served by existing products that meet the same need, like online banking or ride sharing against taxis. The book calls the second slide type “new services for the currently served market”. Use the slide's five in the class test, and mention start-ups if you have room.

Other kinds of service innovation book only

Innovation does not only mean changing the offering. The book names three more routes.

Innovating around customer roles
Redefining the customer's role as user, buyer or payer. Netflix changed how customers get, pay for and return films: first by post on a subscription, now by streaming. Watching at home stayed the same; the whole process around it changed.
Innovation through service solutions
Customers want their problem solved, not a single product. Solutions are sets of customer-facing processes: defining requirements, customizing and integrating goods and services, deploying them, and supporting the customer afterwards. Xerox managing all of a client's documents is one.
Interconnected products
Products with sensors send data over the Internet of ThingsEveryday products with sensors that send data over the Internet.The data becomes a service once it solves a customer problem., and that data becomes a service once someone works out what it can do for the customer. Proteus put sensors in pills so families can check a patient has taken the medicine.

Service Innovation and Development Process

Zeithaml, Chapter 8 · part 2 of 3

The nine stages

The steps follow new product development for goods, adapted for services. They split into two phases. Front-end planning decides which service concept to develop; implementation brings it to life. Between stages sit checkpoints, drawn as stop signs: the idea must meet that stage's test or it is dropped.

Managers say the weakest part is the fuzzy front endThe early planning stages of innovation, before the concept is fixed.Fuzzy because it is still abstract and unclear., called fuzzy because it is abstract, and even more so for an intangible, variable service. In practice the process is not always linear book only: steps can overlap, run at the same time, or be skipped for a simple service. This is flexible product developmentRunning development stages in parallel or skipping some, instead of strictly one after another.Common in fast-moving technology industries., common in technology industries. Even then, the checkpoints should still be passed.

Front-end planning

Stages 1 to 5
1Business strategy development or reviewReview the organization's mission and vision first. New services must fit inside them. PetSmart's mission of “lifetime care of pets” led straight to training, grooming and overnight care.
2New service strategy developmentDecide what kind of new services suit the firm's goals, capabilities and growth plans: which markets, which types of service, what time horizon, what profit criteria.
3Idea generationBrainstorming, ideas from employees and customers, lead user researchStudying customers who are ahead of the market and already improvise their own solutions.Their needs today are everyone's needs tomorrow., studying competitors, even working with outside partners. Watching customers use the service (empathic designGetting ideas by watching how customers actually use a service.Useful when customers cannot say what they need.) helps when they cannot put their needs into words. Contact staff are a strong source. Checkpoint: screen the ideas against the new service strategy.
4Concept development and evaluationAgree exactly what the concept is and which customer need it fills, because different people picture it differently. Write a description of the problem, the reasons for the service, the process and its benefits, and the roles of customers and employees; a concept-level blueprintA blueprint with little detail, showing only the basic steps of the service.Good for explaining the service; a detailed one is for fixing it. helps. Checkpoint: test the concept with customers and employees: do they understand it, like it, and feel it meets an unmet need?
5Business analysisEstimate economic feasibility and profit: demand, revenue, costs, operational feasibility, including hiring, training, technology and facility costs. Checkpoint: test for profitability and feasibility.

Implementation

Stages 6 to 9
1Service development and testingEveryone with a stake takes part: customers, contact employees, and marketing, operations and HR. The concept is refined until a detailed service blueprint can be drawn. IDEO built a life-size foam model of a Marriott TownePlace lobby and suite so guests and managers could react to it. Checkpoint: conduct a service prototype test.
2Market testingHard for services, because a new service is tied to the existing delivery system and may have only one location. Alternatives: offer it to employees and their families first. A pilot runA rehearsal of the full service before launch, to check the operation works.“There is simply no substitute for a proper rehearsal.” is essential to check the operation works; skipping it makes the launch the first real test. Checkpoint: test the service and the other marketing mix elements.
3CommercializationThe service goes live. Two goals: win the acceptance of the many staff who will deliver it day to day, and monitor every part of the service through a full service cycle: calls, transactions, billing, complaints, delivery, costs.
4Postintroduction evaluationReview what commercialization showed and change the delivery process, staffing or marketing mix to match the real market response. No service stays the same, so the review should be formal.

Prototype testing at the Mayo Clinic book only

Mayo's Center for Innovation is a working clinic where new service ideas are tried on real patients and doctors, with their consent. Long check-in queues were the problem: the first self check-in kiosk prototype was a sheet of paper standing in for a screen, then a dead screen, then a working touch screen, with feedback from patients and staff at every version.

Finding growth opportunities book only

To shape a new service strategy, the book uses Ansoff's matrix. Grow with current or new customers, using existing or new services. The matrix sparks ideas, and later works as an idea screen if the firm picks one or two cells to focus on.

Four cells
Share buildingExisting services, current customers.
Market developmentExisting services, new customers. KFC expanding into China and then Africa.
Service developmentNew services, current customers. PetSmart's grooming and PetsHotels.
DiversificationNew services, new customers. Procter & Gamble opening Tide Dry Cleaners and Mr. Clean Car Wash.

Service Blueprint

Zeithaml, Chapter 8 · part 3 of 3

What a service blueprint is

Service blueprint
A visual diagram that maps the whole process of delivering a service, from the customer's side through to the behind-the-scenes operations. It helps a business understand and improve its service by showing the steps, the touchpoints with customers, and the places that need improving.

The book's wording: a picture or map of the customer experience and the service system, drawn so everyone involved can understand it objectively, whatever their role. It shows four things at once: the process of delivery, the points of customer contact, the roles of customers and employees, and the visible evidence of the service. Words alone fail for the reasons in part 1; a blueprint replaces the words with a picture. It is most useful at the design stage.

Blueprint components

Four action areas, with the physical evidence above them and three lines between them. The labels and symbols are not rigid; the flexibility is one of the technique's strengths.

Physical evidence
Across the top. Everything tangible the customer sees at each step: for a lawyer, the office décor, the documents, the lawyer's clothes.
Customer actions
Every step, choice and interaction the customer goes through while buying, using and judging the service. The whole customer experience is in this row, and drawing starts here.
Onstage contact employee actions
What the contact employee does in front of the customer. The lawyer's first interview and the meetings.
Backstage contact employee actions
What contact employees do out of the customer's sight to support the onstage work. The lawyer preparing for the meetings.
Support processes
Internal services and people that support the contact staff: legal research, document preparation, a secretary arranging meetings.
Three lines
1Line of interactionDirect contact between customer and organization. Wherever a vertical line crosses it, a service encounterAny moment the customer deals with the firm, in person or otherwise.Chapter 4's moment of truth. has happened.
2Line of visibilityThe most important one. It separates what the customer can see from what they cannot, and onstage from backstage work. A doctor examining you is above it; the doctor reading your chart beforehand is below.
3Line of internal interactionSeparates contact employees from the support staff and activities behind them. A vertical line across it is an internal service encounter.

Blueprint examples from class

Read each one left to right along the customer row, then follow the arrows down to see who and what makes each step happen.

A restaurant

The guest sees all eight of their own steps, but the kitchen, the cleaning and the payment system do work no guest sees. Notice the arrow that goes up: the server confirming satisfaction feeds back into the guest's dining.

A hospital visit

Here the support row is busy: every desk the patient visits sets off paperwork, database entry, insurance processing or billing behind the line of visibility.

The book's own examples book only are an express mail delivery, where the customer sees only three steps (calling, handing over the package, receiving it) while a long chain of dispatch, sorting and flights stays hidden, and an overnight hotel stay, where the guest is far more involved and meets many different frontline staff.

Blueprints for self-service technology book only

For a website or kiosk with no employees, the contact-person rows are dropped and the area above the line of visibility becomes onstage technology actions: what the screen shows and does. If a service mixes people and technology, like airline check-in at a kiosk with staff handling bags, the blueprint keeps a technology row and both employee rows. The book's DVD rental kiosk example shows a customer calling customer service about a scratched disc, a backstage contact person action.

Why a service blueprint is important

Six reasons
1Visualizes the entire service processShows the sequence of activities from the customer's first contact to the end of the service.
2Identifies service failures and bottlenecksA step where work piles up and slows the whole service down.A blueprint makes them easy to spot.Helps managers find delays, errors and points where customers may become dissatisfied.
3Clarifies employee roles and responsibilitiesSeparates customer-facing work from backstage tasks, so employees know what is theirs.
4Improves coordination between departmentsShows how employees, departments and support systems depend on each other.
5Supports service innovation and improvementLets the firm redesign processes, bring in technology and raise quality before making the change.
6Enhances customer satisfactionFixing the problems it reveals gives the customer a smoother, more reliable experience.

Reading a blueprint book only

How you read it depends on what you want to learn.

Read itTo understandAsk
Left to right, along the customer rowThe customer's experienceHow does the customer start the service? What choices do they make? What evidence do they see?
Left to right, around the line of visibilityContact employees' rolesWho meets the customer, when and how often? Is the customer passed from person to person?
VerticallyHow the parts fit togetherWhat backstage and support work keeps each customer step going? How are handoffs made?
As a wholeRedesignHow complex is it? Where are the failure points, bottlenecks and pain pointsMoments in the service that frustrate or annoy the customer.Redesign starts with these.?

Building a service blueprint

Drawing a blueprint is a team job, never one person's: it needs people from several functions and information from customers.

Six steps
1Identify the process to be blueprintedAgree the starting point and level. A concept blueprint shows the basic service; a detailed one can zoom in on one problem step, like package sorting.
2Identify the customer or customer segmentSegments usually experience the service differently, so detailed blueprints are drawn one segment at a time.
3Map the process from the customer's point of viewChart what the customer does and experiences. Starting here keeps the focus off steps with no customer impact. The start and end may surprise you: salon customers saw the service begin with the phone call to book.
4Map contact employee actions and/or technology actionsDraw the lines of interaction and visibility, then map what contact staff do onstage and backstage. For technology, map its actions above the line of visibility.
5Link contact activities to needed support functionsDraw the line of internal interaction and link contact work to internal support. Steps with no link to the customer may turn out to be unnecessary.
6Add evidence of service at each customer action stepAdd what the customer sees at each step. A photographic or video blueprint helps check the evidence fits the firm's positioning.

Blueprinting in action: ARAMARK book only

Repeat visits to ARAMARK's Lake Powell houseboat resort were falling. A photo and video blueprint showed managers that guests had to work hard on holiday: shopping, carrying food down a steep bank, anchoring and cooking. New services followed, from carrying bags to the boat up to onboard chefs and hired captains. Complaints fell by 50 percent and repeat business rose 12 percent.
