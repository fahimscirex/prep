The Scope and Challenge of International Marketing

Cateora, Chapter 1

Why international markets matter

LO1-1. The book opens by admitting that when it first predicted "a one-world economic and market system" almost 25 years ago, nobody foresaw 9/11, SARS, the 2008 housing collapse, Brexit, Trump, COVID-19 or the war in Ukraine. World trade fell 11 percent in 2009, 13 percent between 2014 and 2016, and about 10 percent in 2020. The lesson the chapter draws is blunt: expect the unexpected. You still plan and forecast, but markets, especially international ones, are ultimately unpredictable. Flexibility means survival, and it is best managed through strong relationships and broad portfolios of businesses.

Four trends are singled out as the ones that will shape international business beyond today's "bumpy roads":

  • The rapid growth of the World Trade OrganizationThe institution created at Marrakech in 1994 that encompasses GATT and extends it, sets trade rules for 164 members, hears disputes and issues binding decisions.Institution versus agreement is the contrast to draw with GATT. No enforcement arm, but the winner may retaliate. Doha Round cancelled 2016. and new free trade agreements around the world.
  • The trend toward acceptance of the free market system among developing countries in Latin America, Asia and eastern Europe.
  • The burgeoning impact of the Internet, mobile phones and other global media on the dissolution of national borders.
  • The mandate to manage the resources and global environment properly for the generations to come.

The consequence for any firm: every American company is international, at least to the extent that its performance is conditioned in part by events abroad. Being international "is no longer a luxury but a necessity for economic survival."

The internationalization of U.S. business

LO1-2. Competition now arrives on all fronts. Familiar "American" names are foreign owned: 7-Eleven and Firestone (Japan), Budweiser (Belgium), Ben & Jerry's and Smith & Wesson (U.K.), Purina and Gerber (Switzerland), Smithfield Foods and the Waldorf Astoria (China). Foreign direct investment in the United States exceeds $4.6 trillion, led by the U.K., Japan, the Netherlands, Canada and France. The flow runs both ways: a Conference Board study of 1,250 U.S. manufacturers found multinationals grew twice as fast in sales as purely domestic firms and earned higher returns on equity and assets. Firms such as Intel (78 percent), Exxon and Apple (about 60 percent) earn most of their revenue outside the United States.

The self-reference criterion and ethnocentrism

LO1-4. The key to success is adaptation: a conscious effort to anticipate the influences of both foreign and domestic uncontrollables on a marketing mix and then adjust the mix to minimise their effects. The primary obstacles are two related habits of mind.

Self-reference criterion (SRC)
An unconscious reference to one's own cultural valuesThe importance a culture places on things and ideas; the first of the five elements of culture.Hofstede's dimensions are the measurement tool. A key term, so be able to say why values sit under the other four elements., experiences and knowledge as a basis for decisions. Ethnocentrism
The notion that people in one's own company, culture or country know best how to do things. A particular problem for American managers after the 1990s boom, and generally when managers from affluent countries work with less affluent markets.

The personal-space example: Americans keep a distance that feels right without thinking; someone from a closer-standing culture approaches, the American backs away, and each concludes the other is rude. Both are victims of their SRC. The SRC can stop you noticing a difference, make you discount it, or make you react offensively: refusing food or drink is polite in the United States and an insult in Asia or the Middle East.

Failures traced to the SRC: Starbucks in the Forbidden City (six years to correct); Zara's striped blouse with a yellow six-pointed star. Successes from setting it aside: McVitie's biscuits individually wrapped in gift boxes at three times the price for Japan; Unilever reformulating and repackaging detergent for Brazil (simpler formula, plastic not paper because clothes are washed in rivers, small low-priced packs); the two-mutton-patty Maharaja Mac; Vicks renamed Wicks because the original is crude German slang.

Cross-cultural analysis, the four steps

01

Define the problem or goal in home-country cultural traits, habits or norms

Write down what you assume, as your own culture frames it.

02

Define it in foreign-country traits, habits or norms

Through consultation with natives of the target country. Make no value judgments.

03

Isolate the SRC influence

Examine carefully how your own assumptions complicate the problem.

04

Redefine the problem without the SRC influence

Solve for the optimum business goal situation.

The book's own illustration is the American sales manager in Tokyo who cancelled the daily early-morning meeting because that is how things are done at home. Sales fell sharply; Japanese sales representatives are motivated mostly by peer pressure. Restoring the meetings restored performance. Note the warning that closes the section: undetected similarities cause no problems, but "the one difference that goes undetected can create a marketing failure."

Developing a global awareness

LO1-5. To be globally aware is to have (1) tolerance of and a willingness to learn about cultural differences and (2) knowledge of cultures, history, world market potential, and global economic, social and political trends.   • Tolerance is understanding differences and accepting and working with others whose behaviors may differ. You need not adopt another culture's ways, "but you must allow others to be different and equal." Less punctual does not mean less productive, only different.
  • Knowledge of cultures and history explains behavior in the marketplace and the boardroom; Latin American reluctance toward foreign investment or Chinese reluctance to open fully make sense with a historical perspective.

Stages of international marketing involvement

LO1-6. Once a company decides to go international it must decide its degree of involvement and commitment. Research finds three factors favor faster internationalization: high-technology or marketing-based resources; a small home market with large production capacity; and key managers who are well networked internationally. Five stages describe involvement, but they are not a ladder: a firm may begin at any stage or be in more than one at once. High-tech firms with short product life cycles often treat the whole world, home included, as a single market from day one.

01

No direct foreign marketing

The company does not actively cultivate customers abroad, but its products may reach foreign markets through trading companies, foreign buyers who make contact, or domestic wholesalers who sell abroad without its knowledge. An unsolicited order from a website often piques the interest that starts everything.

02

Infrequent foreign marketing

Temporary surpluses from swings in production or demand are sold abroad as goods become available, with no intention of continuous representation. As domestic demand absorbs the surplus, foreign sales are withdrawn. Little change in organization or product lines. Few firms fit this today because customers want long-term relationships, and returns are limited.

03

Regular foreign marketing

Permanent productive capacity is devoted to foreign markets, served through intermediaries or the firm's own sales force or subsidiaries. Focus is still domestic, but products may be adapted and profit expectations move from "a bonus" to dependence on foreign sales. Meter-Man, a 25-person Minnesota maker of agricultural measuring devices, went from exploring exports in 1989 to a third of sales in 35 countries: "going international has become critical to our existence."

04

International marketing

Fully committed and involved. The firm seeks markets all over the world and sells products that result from planned production for markets in various countries, generally including production outside the home market. It becomes an international or multinational marketing firm. Fedders, the largest U.S. room air-conditioner maker, faced a seasonal, mature home market and entered China through the joint venture Fedders Xinle, redesigning a lightweight split-type unit because in China an air conditioner is a status symbol, not a box that cools a room.

05

Global marketing

The most profound change is orientation: the company treats the world, including its home market, as one market. Segments are defined by income levels, usage patterns or other factors that span countries, not by national borders. The transition is often catalysed when more than half of revenue comes from abroad. Coca-Cola's mid-1990s redesign made the U.S. business just part of one of six international divisions: almost half of revenue but only about 20 percent of profit.

The first two stages are reactive and often reflect no strategic thinking about expansion. Stages three to five correspond to the three strategic orientations below.

The three strategic orientations

The consensus of researchers is three relatively distinct approaches to strategic decisions in firms involved abroad. The book's own summary of them is the cleanest exam answer:

Regular foreign marketing
International marketing is seen as ancillary to domestic operations. Foreign sales are welcome but secondary.
Multidomestic or international marketing
International marketing is a crucial source of revenue, but the firm treats each market as a separate entity, with its own adapted product, promotion and plan.
Global marketing
The globe is the marketplace. Market segments are no longer based solely on national borders; common consumer characteristics and behaviors are applied as segmentation variables across countries. The marketing mix is standardized where culturally feasible and cost effective.

A global orientation does not mean strict adherence to one strategy. Procter & Gamble pursues a global strategy for disposable diapers but a multidomestic strategy for detergents in Asian markets; the choice depends on product and market. The book's own stance is an environmental/cultural approach to international strategic marketing: it does not teach marketing principles, it shows how the cultural environment changes the task. It notes that on the KOF Globalization IndexA 23-variable index of countries' economic, social and political links to the rest of the world.The U.S. ranks 24th overall, 14th on political globalization but 66th on economic. Low international tourism is the gap the book says it exists to fill. the United States ranks only 24th, strong on political globalization (14th) and connectivity but weak on economic globalization (66th) and international tourism. Americans, it says, generally have little contact with other cultures, "the very gap on which this book focuses."

Question 6 versus question 7The book asks you to differentiate a global company from a multinational company (a multinational treats each country separately; a global company treats the world as one market) and separately to differentiate the three international marketing concepts (the three orientations above). Keep the two questions apart.

Cases

GP

Global Commerce Causes Peace

The chapter opener. Boeing counts customers in more than 150 countries and 140,000 employees in more than 65; the 787 uses parts from Australia, France, India, Italy, Japan, Russia and Sweden. Five billion people own mobile phones, led by Samsung, Apple and Huawei. SodaStream's plant in Rahat, Israel, employs Jews, Palestinians, Bedouins and Druze together; PepsiCo bought the firm for $3.2 billion in 2019. Postage stamps in France and the United States, and Adam Smith in 1776, made the same point.

What it illustrates. International marketing as a producer of peace, not just profit: commerce requires people from many countries to work together, and the personal relationships and mutual understanding that result are the foundations of peace and prosperity.

If it is the question. It would ask you to argue that trade promotes peace, or to critique the claim. Use Boeing as the large-firm case and SodaStream as the small-firm case, and add the book's honest caveat that Boeing's military sales are 20 to 30 percent of its business.

CB 1.1

Blanca Nieves, La Cenicienta, y Bimbo

Bimbo, Mexico's most popular bread and the logo of a little white bear, is a disparaging word in English. Grupo Bimbo, with 130,000 employees in 33 countries, has bought Sara Lee, Oroweat and Mrs. Baird's. CB 1.3

Risks and Payoffs in International Marketing

After the 2009 crash the U.S. ATVM programme lent half a billion dollars each to Tesla and Fisker. Fisker built about 2,400 Karmas and went bankrupt in 2013; a Chinese buyer revived the brand as the $130,000 Karma Revero. Tesla posted its first profit in 2014, has sold over a million vehicles, opened stores in Europe and China, launched right-hand-drive models for the U.K., Hong Kong, Japan and Australia, and repaid its loan early. It sells through company-owned stores and cannot sell in dealership-only states like Texas and Maryland except online.

What it illustrates. Same programme, same product category, opposite outcomes. International expansion carries real risk and real payoff, and channel structure (company stores versus dealers) is itself constrained by local law, an uncontrollable.

If it is the question. A question would ask why one succeeded and one failed, or what uncontrollable elements Tesla faces abroad: battery and charging infrastructure, dealer laws, and vulnerability of luxury goods to a downturn.

Protectionism

LO2-3. Executives live in "a world of tariffs, quotas, and nontariff barriers designed to protect a country's markets." Nations use legal barriers, exchange barriers and psychological barriers; businesses erect private market barriers; and the market structure itself can be a barrier, as with Japan's complex distribution system, which keeps products out but cannot legally be called a trade barrier. China has been accused of holding its currency artificially low to boost exports.

Protection logic and illogic

Protectionists offer countless reasons, but they reduce to twelve: (1) protection of an infant industry, (2) protection of the home market, (3) need to keep money at home, (4) encouragement of capital accumulation, (5) maintenance of the standard of living and real wages, (6) conservation of natural resources, (7) industrialization of a low-wage nation, (8) maintenance of employment, (9) national defense, (10) enhancement of business size, (11) retaliation and bargaining, and, in the current climate of authoritarianism, (12) the corrupt goals of despots.

Which arguments economists acceptOnly three: infant industry, national defense, and industrialization of underdeveloped countries. Resource conservation is becoming increasingly valid. Temporary protection for an orderly transition can be defended, but it usually becomes permanent and breeds inefficiency. Question 10 asks you to support or refute each argument; this paragraph is the answer key.

The cost is paid by consumers who do not know they are paying it. A study of 21 protected U.S. industries found consumers pay about $70 billion a year in higher prices, and the cost of saving one job averaged $170,000, more than the worker's wages and benefits. The Trump tariffs cost the average household an extra $250 in 2019. Protectionism is politically popular during high unemployment but "rarely leads to renewed growth in a declining industry."

The counter-evidence in Exhibit 2.6: consumer purchasing power grew over 40 percent in the United States and Canada from 1990 to 2020, 19 percent in Mexico, and over 1,000 percent in China. The two U.S. free-trade breakthroughs were NAFTA (1994) and PNTR for China (2000), followed by China's WTO entry in 2001. The authors find no evidence that NAFTA cost American jobs; the economic divide, they argue, came from internal decisions about wealth distribution, taxes and labor relations.

Tariffs

A tariff is a tax imposed by a government on goods entering at its borders, used to raise revenue, to discourage imports, or both. U.S. customs duties come in three types:

Ad valorem duties
A percentage of the determined value of the imported goods.
Specific duties
A stipulated amount per unit of weight or other measure of quantity.
Compound duty
Both together: a tax per pound plus a percentage of value.

Tariffs increase inflationary pressures, special-interest privileges, government control of economic matters, and the number of tariffs (they beget others via reciprocity). They weaken balance-of-payments positions, supply-and-demand patterns, and international relations (they can start trade wars). They restrict manufacturers' supply sources, consumer choice, and competition. They are arbitrary, discriminatory and need constant administration. The "pasta war": the United States raised tariffs 40 percent on European spaghetti over export subsidies; Europe hit U.S. walnuts and lemons, then fertilizer, paper and beef tallow, until it dropped the subsidies.

Easing trade restrictions: the Omnibus Act, GATT, WTO, IMF and World Bank

The Omnibus Trade and Competitiveness Act of 1988

LO2-5. Written when Japan was the number one "trade problem" (China has since taken over), the act addresses trade deficits, protectionism and the fairness of trading partners, on the realization that "we must deal with our trading partners based on how they actually operate, not on how we want them to behave." It covers three critical areas:

Market access
The president may restrict a country's sales in the U.S. if it imposes unfair restrictions on U.S. products, and may ban U.S. government procurement from nations whose procurement rules discriminate against U.S. firms.
Export expansion
Recognizes that some problems are self-inflicted: export controls, the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (FCPA) and export promotion. Export licenses became easier and faster; computer-based license tracking and the National Trade Data Bank (NTDB) were created.
Import relief
A menu of remedies for U.S. businesses seriously injured by fairly traded imports: temporary relief while they adjust and regain competitiveness.

World Trade Organization

Created at Marrakech in April 1994, the WTO is an institution, not an agreement as GATT was. It has 164 members, all with equal representation in a ministerial conference that meets at least every two years to elect a director general. It sets rules, hears disputes through panels of experts, and issues binding decisions: the winner may retaliate with sanctions if the loser does not change. It has no means of enforcement, but international pressure is expected to force compliance, and members must accept all agreements, not just the ones they like. Ratification was contested in the U.S. over loss of sovereignty and lack of a veto. China joined in 2001, Russia in 2012. The Doha Round, begun 2001, was cancelled in 2016; President Trump was the WTO's biggest critic even as Canada filed complaints against U.S. tariffs.

Skirting the spirit. To join, China cut tariffs on 5,000 product lines and dropped quotas and licenses; U.S. firms then noticed a surge in technical standards and inspection requirements (safety inspections on jigsaw puzzles; certification requiring paid on-site visits by Chinese officials for foreign but not domestic goods). Frustrated with Doha, countries turned to bilateral free trade agreements: the U.S. has 20, including Peru, Colombia, Panama and South Korea.

History and Geography: The Foundations of Culture

Historical perspective in global business

LO3-1, LO3-2. Here culture is defined as a society's accepted basis for responding to external and internal events. To interpret behavior in a country a marketer needs its history and geography. History helps define a nation's "mission": how it perceives its neighbors, its place in the world and itself, and it shapes attitudes toward the role of government and business, relations between managers and managed, and attitudes toward foreign corporations. History also influences business decisions: foreign direct investment, market entry, even personnel choices.

China: why the leadership is wary of foreign influence

American history "really begins with China": the Boston Tea Party was a protest at Britain's ban on Yankee traders dealing directly with Canton. Three episodes the book insists you know:

First Opium War and the Treaty of Nanjing, 1839 to 1842
British tea drinking created a huge deficit paid in silver. The East India Company's answer was opium from British India: easy to ship, high value to weight, addictive. When the emperor destroyed the Canton inventories, Britain blockaded the ports. At Nanjing China ceded Hong Kong and $21 million and opened Xiamen, Fuzhou, Ningbo and Shanghai to foreigners. Most important, China recognized for the first time its loss of great-power status.
Second Opium War, 1857 to 1860
British and French forces destroyed the summer palace in Beijing; the treaty gave foreign traders more freedom and specifically permitted Christian evangelism throughout the realm.
Taiping Rebellion, 1851 to 1864
Hong Xiuquan, a failed civil-service candidate who met Protestant missionaries and declared himself Christ's brother, was crowned "Heavenly King" and marched north with religious zealots. Foreigners raised an army against him. The death toll was 20 to 40 million, the most horrific civil war in history (the 1949 revolution killed "only" 2 million). Hence Chinese wariness of foreign influence in general and religious movements in particular.

Seven centuries of the shogun feudal system, isolation until Commodore Perry in 1853, the threat of colonial domination, World War II. Geography and global markets

LO3-5, LO3-6. Geography, "the study of Earth's surface, climate, continents, countries, peoples, industries, and resources," is an uncontrollable that receives scant attention. It is more than memorizing capitals: it is understanding how a society's culture and economy are shaped as a nation supplies its needs within the limits of its physical makeup.

Climate and topography

  • Altitude, humidity and temperature extremes change product function: U.S. construction equipment needs extensive modification for the Sahara; a Taiwanese shipment of glasses packed in hay arrived in shards because the hay dried and shrivelled in the Middle East.
  • Within one market: Ghana runs from desert heat to rainforest humidity. Bosch-Siemens washing machines need a 1,600 rpm spin in sunless Germany and Scandinavia but only 500 rpm in Italy and Spain.
  • Seasons: JCPenney's Chilean expansion failed because when it bought winter stock for North America it needed summer stock south of the equator. It sold its one store.
  • Barriers: the Andes run 4,500 miles at 13,000 feet, precluding Pacific-to-Atlantic trade routes. Once prosperity comes, barriers are overcome: the Channel Tunnel (resisted for decades as protection), the 21-mile Lötschberg and 36-mile Gotthard tunnels under the Alps.

Resources

The locations of minerals and energy are geographic accidents; no nation's demand coincides with its supply. Oil and gas provide over 60 percent of world energy. Dynamics of global population trends

LO3-7. World population passed 8 billion and is expected to reach about 9.8 billion by 2050, though one UN scenario has it peaking near 8 billion and declining after 2040. All scenarios agree that 85 percent will live in less-developed regions by 2050; the ILO says 1.2 billion jobs must be created by 2025, mostly in cities.

Controlling population growth
Procreation is one of the most culturally sensitive, uncontrollable factors. Prerequisites: adequate incomes, higher literacy, education for women, universal healthcare, family planning, improved nutrition, and above all a change in beliefs about large families. India's population will exceed China's by 2050; Indira Gandhi's mass sterilization campaign reportedly cost her the election; Nigeria's Muslim north and Catholic east both favour large families. Some economists hold that fertility falls only with prosperity: Spain went from six children to 1.5 as its economy grew.
Rural/urban migration
Driven by access to education, healthcare and jobs; a manual worker in urban Brazil earns almost five times a rural farm labourer. Over half the world is now urban, 61 percent by 2030, with 27 cities over 10 million, 23 of them in less-developed regions; Tokyo has 37 million. Unregulated growth breaks sanitation and water: Mexico City consumes 16,000 gallons a second against 2,640 from its aquifers; 768 million people lack clean water, almost 3 billion lack sanitation. IKEA is shifting from suburban big boxes to smaller urban stores.
Population decline, aging and disease
The industrialized world is shrinking and aging. Replacement needs about 2.1 children per woman; no major industrialized country reaches it. Over-65s were 2 to 3 percent of population before the Industrial Revolution, 14 percent in the developed world today, 25 percent in 30 countries by 2030. The 85-plus group grows sixfold to 175 million by 2050. In 1998 Japan crossed the threshold where pension withdrawals exceeded contributions. Kenya's young pyramid means education costs; Britain's top-heavy one means pensions with fewer earners. COVID-19 killed about 6 million; the U.S. rate is over 260 per 100,000, China's under one. Two American epidemics: opioids (20 per 100,000 a year, partly from aggressive pharmaceutical marketing) and metabolic syndrome from a diet of 126.4 grams of sugar a day, subsidized by government.
Worker shortage and immigration
The UN's strongest argument: free immigration eases both the population explosion in poor countries and the worker shortage in rich ones. Europe's over-60s go from 20 percent (1998) to 35 percent (2050); Spain opened its borders to South Americans of Spanish descent. Europe needs 1.4 billion immigrants over 50 years, Japan and the United States 600 million by 2050. But the Syrian refugee flow was a key catalyst for Brexit and other nativist policies.

World trade routes and communication links

LO3-8. Trade routes bind the world together, minimizing distance, natural barriers, lack of resources and differences between peoples. Routes established by the 1500s still matter: Spanish galleons carried silver to Manila and returned with Chinese silk, offloaded in Mexico and carried overland to the Atlantic; today the Panama Canal (being widened) does the crossing, and Latin America's ties to Europe and Asia date from that era. Most trade still runs among Europe, North America and Asia, but the fastest-growing routes are among developing nations, and China plans an overland Silk Road to Europe. Arctic melt is opening a Northwest Passage that may compete with the canal.

Communication is the underpinning of all commerce: knowing where goods exist and are needed, and communicating instantly across distance. Telegraph, telephone, television, satellites, mobile phones, the computer, the Internet, now blockchain. Each revolution spawned new business models; firms that failed to respond ceased to exist. The Internet and mobile revolutions "will be no different", and the biggest changes are yet to come. The Corinth canal (1893) replaced dragging ships across a 6-kilometre isthmus.

Culture's pervasive impact

LO4-1. Culture deals with a people's design for living. Three definitions the book offers, each useful in a different answer:

The traditional definition
Culture is the sum of the values, rituals, symbols, beliefs and thought processes that are learned and shared by a group of people, then transmitted from generation to generation. It resides in the individual's mind, but large collectives can be like-minded.
The anthropological definition
Culture is the human-made part of the human environment: the sum total of knowledge, beliefs, art, morals, laws, customs, and any other capabilities and habits acquired by humans as members of society.
Three metaphors
Hofstede: the software of the mind, a problem-solving tool. Edward Hall: an invisible barrier that managers keep bumping their heads against. Ambassador James Day Hodgson: a thicket, tough to get through, but effort and patience often lead to success.

Culture is pervasive in all marketing activities: pricing, research, promotion, channels, product, packaging, styling, and the marketer's efforts become part of the fabric of culture. Markets are the result of the three-way interaction of a marketer's efforts, economic conditions, and all other elements of the culture. Marketers adjust to cultural demands but also act as agents of change whenever a product is innovative: "the use of something new is the beginning of cultural change."

Evidence that culture shapes consumption

  • Birthrates. Singapore's spikes in 1976, 1988 and 2000 are Years of the Dragon, considered lucky, with implications for sellers of diapers, toys and schools in successive years. Japan's fertility fell 20 percent in 1966, the Year of the Fire Horse, because women born then are believed to lead unhappy lives and perhaps murder their husbands; it happens every 60 years and returns in 2026.
  • Consumption patterns (Exhibit 4.2). The Dutch spend most on cut flowers; Germans, British and Japanese eat the most chocolate; Spaniards and Japanese the most fish; Italians the most pasta (brought from China by Marco Polo in 1270); the French and Italians drink the most wine. Spain halved cigarette consumption in six years after its 2011 smoking ban, "a huge cultural shift that the world seldom sees."
  • Consequences (Exhibit 4.3). Fish versus red meat shows in heart disease. Japan has the highest stomach cancer death rate (salty foods such as soy sauce, plus the bacterium Helicobacter pylori from post-war conditions) and therefore the world's most advanced treatment: culture affects production as well as consumption.

The Dutch and flowers: origins to elements in one story

Geography: rivers and bays made a trading nation; rain or snow 200 days a year made a gray one, so Dutch ships brought tulips from the Ottoman Empire in 1561 as "spice for the eyes." History: tulip mania priced a single black bulb at a million dollars in today's money and produced the first futures market and the first great market bust when the government intervened in 1637; today a black tulip costs a dollar. Technology and economics: botanist Carolus Clusius learned to manipulate colours and the trade went international. Social institutions: every Easter the Pope says "Bedankt voor bloemen"; the government sponsors the Floriade every ten years (next 2032); the Aalsmeer auction, 150 football fields and 20 million stems a day, sets world flower prices with the descending-price "Dutch auction", and the Netherlands holds 60 percent of world exports. Definitions and origins of culture

LO4-2. The best marketers understand not only cultural differences but their origins, because that deeper knowledge lets them notice differences in new markets and foresee changes in current ones. Exhibit 4.4 shows the causal chain: humans adapt to changing environments through innovation; individuals learn culture from social institutions through socialization (growing up) and acculturation (adjusting to a new culture, as immigrants and indigenous people do) and through role modeling, imitation of peers. Five origins:

Geography
Broadly defined to include climate, topography, flora, fauna and microbiology. Jared Diamond: innovations spread faster east to west than north to south (the Silk Road, not the Sahara or Panama), which with more virulent germs explains Euro-Asian dominance. Philip Parker: strong correlations between latitude and per capita GDP, so ambient temperature can be a segmentation variable. New rituals evolve almost immediately after HIV, SARS or COVID-19.
History
Tobacco as the Virginia colony's cash crop shaped U.S. trade policy; the Declaration of Independence was influenced by the same-year publication of The Wealth of Nations; the 2003 Iraq conflict bred Mecca Cola, Muslim Up and ColaTurka; Germany's mistrust of propaganda after World War II limits marketing practices; the post-war baby boom still drives consumption.
The political economy
Four approaches competed for the twentieth century: colonialism, fascism, communism, and democracy/free enterprise. Fascism fell in 1945, colonialism after the war, communism in the 1990s. Politics and law shape institutions and values; Chapters 6 and 7 develop it.
Technology
Which innovation most changed U.S. institutions and values in a century? Not jets, television or the Internet: the Pill. Birth control freed women for careers and men for children; over half of U.S. marketing majors are women. The Catholic Church forbade it in 1930 and 1968. Technology does not solve everything: U.S. healthcare leads in technology yet trails in longevity.
Social institutions
Family, religion, school, the media, government and corporations: how people relate, organize, teach the next generation and govern themselves. Detail below.

The social institutions, one by one

  • Family. Women postpone marriage (over 25 percent of Japanese women aged 35 to 39 never married; Japan pays $900 a month per child and one province runs the Fukui Marriage Hunting Cafe). Nepotism is a problem in American firms and an organizing principle in Chinese and Mexican ones. Sex ratios: 113 boys per 100 girls aged 1 to 14 in India, 116 in China, 30 million Chinese bachelors. Individualism is taught the first night an American infant sleeps in her own bassinette. In collectivist cultures a promotion aimed at the family or friendship group beats one aimed at individuals; Canadian travel ads show a woman alone for English speakers and a couple or group for French speakers.
  • Religion. The first institution outside the home. Protestants confess directly through prayer, Catholics through priests, so some scholars argue Protestantism engenders egalitarian thinking. Ignorance offends: South Africa banned an Axe ad of angels tossing halos; Chanel destroyed dresses embroidered with Quran verses copied from a book on the Taj Mahal; Chevrolet's logo was deemed to resemble a cross. Over a billion people embrace Islam.
  • School. Literacy is a potent force: per the World Bank no country has succeeded economically with under 50 percent literacy. Communicating with a literate market is far easier than through symbols and pictures. Days in school: 180 in the U.S., 245 in China, 220 in South Korea, 210 in Japan; PISA leaders are China, Singapore, Macao and Hong Kong. Chinese officials now worry that too much school produces narrow minds.
  • The media. Media time has replaced family time. Children aged 0 to 8 averaged nearly an hour a day on a mobile device in 2021, up from 5 minutes in 2011; 39 percent of Black parents versus 19 percent of white parents see educational benefit in it.
  • Government. Holds relatively little sway compared with the early, direct institutions; values are set before and during adolescence. Governments try anyway: France's $800 birth bonus, Japan's $225 million for day care, bans on hijabs, burkas and minarets, Ireland tightening alcohol laws because drinking costs 2 percent of GDP. Some own the media and use propaganda; Iran is ruled by clerics.
  • Corporations. Most innovations are introduced to societies by companies, often multinationals; merchants have been the primary conduit for the diffusion of innovations since the Silk Road. This makes companies change agents, the subject of the chapter's last section.

Rituals

Patterns of behavior and interaction that are learned and repeated. Big ones: weddings (Monsoon Wedding, grooms on white horses in Delhi), funerals, graduation, the hajj (2 million a year), Varanasi's puja (60 to 80 million pilgrims). Symbols: language and aesthetics

Edward T. Hall: culture is communication, with "languages" of time, space, things, friendships and agreements. Learning to interpret symbols begins at birth.

Language
For some a social institution with political weight: France's agency against Franglais, Canada's secession disputes. Copywriters must worry about idiomatic and symbolic meaning, not literal translation: tambo is a roadside inn in Bolivia, a dairy farm in Argentina and a brothel in Chile; Papua New Guinea has 750 mutually unintelligible languages.
Linguistic distance
Languages form family trees. Counting from English: German 1, Danish 2, Spanish 3, Japanese 4, Hebrew 5, Chinese 6, Thai 7. Crossing wider distance raises transaction costs and predicts lower trade and different values; as distance from English grows, individualism falls. Cultural change

LO4-4, LO4-5. Culture is dynamic, "a living process", yet also conservative and resistant to change, which seems paradoxical. Cultures more open to change belong to more successful economies. Change comes from war (Japan after 1945), disaster, or most often from a society solving problems created by changes in its environment; culture is "the accumulation of a series of the best solutions to problems faced in common." Societies find answers by accident, by invention, and above all by borrowing.

Cultural borrowing
A responsible effort to learn from others' cultural ways in the quest for better solutions. The American breakfast: an orange from the Mediterranean, a cantaloupe from Persia, waffles by a Scandinavian technique from Asia Minor wheat, maple syrup from Native Americans, eggs of a bird domesticated in Indochina, bacon cured by a northern European process, news in Semitic characters on Chinese paper, thanks to a Hebrew deity in an Indo-European language that she is "100 percent American." She is right: each borrowing was adapted and molded into uniquely American habits. Culture is learned; heritage is what separates humans from other animals.
Similarities: an illusion
Shared language or heritage does not mean a product or message transfers. British and Americans both speak English: lift versus elevator, a bathroom is where you bathe, one hoovers rather than vacuums, The Spy Who Shagged Me means nothing to Americans. Edward Hall warns the two have a harder time because similarity is assumed. There is no "European consumer"; U.S. regions and reunified Germany still differ. Never rely on "if it sells in one country it will sell in another."
Resistance to change
Acceptance depends on the degree of interest in the subject and how disruptive the innovation is to present values and behavior. Rapid industrialization in Europe changed attitudes to time and working women, so instant foods and labor-saving devices that would have been resisted are accepted. Leaders resist too: Hu Jintao urged "Chinese cultural production" against Western dominance; Xi and Putin scorn Western values.

01

Cultural congruence

Market products similar to ones already on the market in a manner as congruent as possible with existing norms, thereby minimizing resistance. Much successful, highly competitive marketing needs no change at all.

02

Unplanned change

Introduce a product and hope for the best.
