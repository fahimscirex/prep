Promotion and Integrated Marketing Communication

The course starts from one of the 4Ps and works outward. PromotionAny sales initiative or effort a company undertakes to promote its products and services.It is the P of the 4Ps that marketing communication grew out of. is the P that talks to the customer. A company today talks through a dozen channels at once, and those channels can easily say different things. IMCA concept under which a company carefully coordinates its many communication channels to deliver a clear, consistent, compelling message about itself and its brand.The point is to stop different channels contradicting each other. Same message across all platforms. is the discipline of making them all say one thing.

Promotion, the promotion mix, promotional management

Promotion
Any sales initiative or effort a company undertakes to promote its products and services. It is the communication P of the 4Ps: product, price, place, promotion. Product, price and place decide what you are offering; promotion is the only one whose job is to tell somebody about it.
Promotion mix
The combination of all the promotion tools a company uses. No single tool does everything, so the mix is a blend: advertisingAny paid form of non-personal presentation and promotion of goods, services or ideas by an identified sponsor.The largest tool of promotion. Paid, non-personal, identified sponsor are the three tests. builds awareness cheaply across many people, personal selling closes a complex sale one person at a time, sales promotionA short-term incentive to encourage immediate purchase or sale of a product or service.Discounts, coupons, cashback, free samples, BOGOF. moves stock this month. The mix question is which tools, in what proportion, for this product and this customer.
Promotional management

Managing that mix. The professor listed the manager's actual jobs:

  • Organizing, planning and analysing the promotional tools, and selecting the best ones for the situation
  • Setting and allocating the budget across the tools chosen
  • Selecting the media the message will run in
  • Selecting the celebrityA person widely recognized by the public for their personal achievement.Used as a source or spokesperson to lend a message credibility and attention. or spokesperson who will carry it
  • Designing the message itself
  • Buying the media time and space

The five classic tools sit at the centre of the mix, and the professor added the newer digital ones alongside them. All thirteen get their full treatment at step 5 of the planning process, in Chapter 3.

Advertising
Paid, non-personal communication from an identified sponsor. Reaches the most people for the least cost per person, but cannot answer a question or close a sale.
Personal selling
One person talking to one customer. Expensive per contact, but it adapts to the person in front of you and can ask for the order.
Sales promotion
A short-term incentive to buy now: a discount, a coupon, a free sample. It changes behaviour this week, not attitudes this year.
Public relations and publicity
Communication the company does not pay for directly, carried by a third party such as a newspaper. Cheap and highly credible, because it does not look bought, but the company does not control what is said.
Direct marketing
Communication aimed at a named individual, asking for a direct response. Measurable, because you know exactly who replied.
The digital tools

Added by the professor to the classic five, and treated as separate tools in this course:

  • Influencer marketing, word of mouth, social media marketing
  • Search engine marketing, content marketing, blog marketing
  • Viral marketing, mobile marketing, attention marketing

What IMC is

Two definitions were given. The professor's is the one to reproduce in an exam; the textbook's is worth recognising if it appears in a question.

Professor's definition
Integrated marketing communication is a concept under which companies carefully coordinate various communication channels in order to provide a clear, consistent, compelling and concise message about the company and its brand, and the result is measurable. Compressed to one line: no conflicting message, the same message across all platforms.
Textbook definition (AAAA)
A concept of marketing communications planning that recognizes the added value of a comprehensive plan that evaluates the strategic roles of a variety of communication disciplines, such as general advertising, direct marketing, sales promotion and public relations, and combines them to provide clarity, consistency and maximum communications impactThe qualitative value of an exposure through a given medium.BBC carries more impact than BTV for the same ad..
What the problem actually is
A company whose television advertising promises premium quality, whose Facebook page runs a permanent discount, and whose salespeople describe the product as cheap and cheerful, has told the customer three different things. None of the three is wrong on its own. Together they leave the customer with no clear idea what the brand is. IMC is the answer to that, and the coordination is the work.

Brand equity, and why IMC drives it

Brand equityThe value a brand adds to a product beyond the product itself.Driven by awareness, recognition, recall, image and quality, and the customer relationship. is the value the brand name adds beyond the product itself: the reason a customer will pay more for the same thing with a label on it. IMC builds it, because equity is made of things communication creates.

Brand awareness
Whether the customer knows the brand exists at all. Nothing else on this list can happen first, which is why awareness is the usual first objective of a campaign.
Brand recognition
The customer can identify the brand when they see or hear it. It needs a cue, so it matters most at the shelf, where the pack is in front of them.
Brand recall
The customer remembers the brand on their own, with no cue. Harder to build than recognition, and worth more, because it is what happens when someone is asked to name a soft drink.
Image and perceived quality
What the customer believes the brand is like, and how good they assume it is. Built by consistent messages over time, which is exactly what inconsistent channels destroy.
Value of the product and the customer relationship
The real benefit delivered, and the strength of the ongoing relationship. Communication cannot substitute for these, but it is how they are established and maintained.

Why IMC grew

Five reasons were given, and these are the ones to learn.

Change in consumer media behaviour
Audiences no longer gather around three television channels. They are spread across streaming, social platforms and messaging apps, and they skip advertising when they can. Reaching the same person now takes several channels, which is what creates the coordination problem.
Technological advancement in communication
New channels appeared faster than companies could organise around them. Each new one was often handed to a different team or agency, so each spoke with its own voice.
Allocation of budget among channels
With many channels available, someone has to decide how the money is divided. That decision cannot sensibly be made channelThe route the message takes: personal (word of mouth, personal selling) or non-personal (print, broadcast). by channel in isolation, so it forces a single integrated plan.
Shift in the promotional budget
Money moved away from mass media advertising and toward sales promotion, direct marketing and digital. Advertising stopped being the automatic centre of the plan, so the plan needed a new centre, and integration became it.
Evolution of advertising agencies and their accountability
Clients began demanding measurable results rather than creative reputation. Agencies responded by offering coordinated services across channels instead of advertising alone, because a measurable result needs the whole programme pulling together.

A longer version of the list runs to ten. It keeps the five above and adds these:

  • Rise of digital and social media, which multiplied the number of channels again
  • Fragmentation of mediaThe audience is spread thinly across many channels instead of gathered around a few mass media.One of the main reasons coordination became necessary., so no single medium reaches a mass audience any more
  • Cost efficiency and return-on-investment pressure, which rewards removing duplicated effort
  • Growth of data and marketing technology, which makes coordinated targeting possible
  • Globalization of brands, which makes one consistent worldwide message valuable

Why IMC is important

Strategic integration of the communication functions
Coordinating the tools avoids duplication, because two teams stop buying the same audience twice. It also creates synergy: an advertisement that has built awareness makes the salesperson's first call easier, so the two tools together achieve more than the sum of their separate effects. The result is marketing that is both more efficient and more effective.
A rapidly changing environment
Marked by the professor as the most important reason. Consumers, technology and media are all moving at once, so a promotional plan fixed around one channel goes out of date quickly. Integration is what lets the plan shift channels without losing its message.

Read the table as one movement. Communication used to be a small number of companies buying a small number of mass media, on long contracts, with little measurement. It is now many channels, narrowly targeted, bought on performance, with the retailer and the data holding the power. Every row is a reason coordination became necessary.

Evolution

IMC started in the 1980s. Before then there was no such concept, because there was nothing to integrate: mass media meant television, radio and newspapers, companies used few channels, and one advertising agencyAn independent organization that creates, produces and places advertisements on behalf of the client.Grey and Asiatic are the local examples given. handled nearly all of it. After 1980 companies began using many channels at once, found that the messages conflicted, and recognised that coordination was needed. Agencies took on that coordination role, and the discipline got its name.

Trends and challenges

Emerging trends

What is changing in the field now:

  • Personal marketing and data-driven personalization, where the message is built for the individual rather than the segment
  • Artificial intelligence, in targeting, buying and producing the creative work
  • Customers who are very knowledgeable, because they research before they buy and can check any claim
  • Fragmented mass media, so audiences must be assembled from many small sources
  • Influencer marketing, borrowing someone else's audience and credibility
  • Real-time and reactive marketing, responding to events as they happen
  • Video and immersive technology, including augmented and virtual reality
  • Purpose and ethics, with customers judging what the company stands for
  • Neuro-branding, using measures of attention and emotion to test creative work
  • Agile and integrated working, and interactive content that the audience acts on rather than watches
Branding challenges

The professor called branding the most challenging task now, for five reasons:

  • Reduction of the customer's attention span, so the message has less time to land
  • Changes in the consumer decision journey, which no longer runs in a straight line from awareness to purchase
  • A sharp increase in buying through online retail channels, where the brand competes on a search results page rather than a shelf
  • A shift in advertising and promotion expenditure to digital channels, which are measurable but crowded
  • The rise of many-to-many communication, where customers talk to each other about the brand and the company cannot control what is said

    Three organisational systems for promotion

    A company can arrange its own promotional work in three ways. The professor marked this topic as important. The right choice follows from size and from how many brands there are to manage, and each system buys one thing at the cost of another.

    Centralised
    Promotion is run from one place, with one manager responsible for all of it. It suits small enterprises, where there is one product line and not enough work to justify separate teams. You gain control and consistency, because one person sees everything, and you need fewer staff. You lose speed and closeness to individual brands.
    Decentralised
    Each brand, division or product line runs its own promotion. It suits large companies with many brands, channels and products, where one central manager could not know them all well enough. You gain attention and speed on each brand. You lose consistency and efficiency, because the divisions compete for funds and duplicate each other's work.
    In-house agency
    The company builds its own advertising agency inside itself, as a separate department. It suits the very largest advertisers, where the volume of work is enough to keep a full creative and media team busy all year. You gain control, coordination and cost savings, because there is no agency margin. You lose the outside perspective and the breadth of experience an agency gets from many clients.

    How agencies gain clients

    Presentation
    A proposal to a prospective client, and the chance to demonstrate the agency's experience and capabilities. Often competitive, with several agencies pitching for the same account.
    Referrals
    Recommendations from existing clients, from media firms, or from others in the trade. This is why agencies maintain relationships with people who are not currently paying them.
    Solicitation
    The agency goes looking: researching prospects and approaching them directly rather than waiting to be invited.
    Public relations
    Being visible and well regarded in the industry press and at industry events, so that prospects already know the name.
    Image and reputation
    Awards, famous campaigns and a track record. This is the slowest route and the strongest, because the client comes to the agency already convinced.

    The IMC Planning Process

    This is the syllabus. The professor taught from one flowchart, the model of the IMC planning process, and worked down it as far as the Advertising box. Everything in this chapter is a step of that chart. Chapter 4 is the Advertising box opened up.

    The model

    Read it top to bottom. The first four steps are analysis: they tell you who you are talking to, what you may say, and what you can spend. The fifth is the programme itself, where the tools are chosen and each one is given objectives, a message and media. The last two boxes, implementing and monitoring, were not reached before the midterm. The dashed arrows down the left mean the results of the programme feed back into every earlier step, so planning is a loop rather than a line.

    Step 1: Review of the marketing plan

    The professor set this as a board question: what is a marketing plan, what does it contain, and why review it?

    What it is
    The guideline for how a product will be marketed. It works at two levels and both must be reviewed. The strategic level is STPSegmentation, targeting, positioning: the strategic core of the marketing plan., segmentation, targeting and positioning, which decides who the customer is and what the product means to them. The tactical level is features, discounts and product differentiation, which decides what is actually offered.
    Contents

    The contents listed in class:

    Executive summaryThe plan in brief, for people who will not read the rest
    Current market situationWhere the product stands today
    SWOT and PESTELThe opportunity and issue analysis: what helps and what threatens
    Risk analysisWhat could go wrong, and how likely it is
    Competitor analysisWho else is in the market and what they will do
    Marketing objectivesWhat the plan is trying to achieve
    Marketing strategyWhich is STP
    Action planWho does what, and when
    Financial projectionWhat it costs and what it returns
    ImplementationHow it will be carried out
    Control and feedbackHow it will be measured and corrected
    Why review it
    Because the promotional plan is a servant of the marketing plan, not a separate thing. The marketing plan names the target, so it decides who the advertising talks to. It sets the positioning, so it decides what the advertising may claim. It fixes the objectives, so it decides what the promotion has to deliver. A promotional plan that is not aligned with it will not be successful and will not be accepted by management.

    Having read the plan, you now examine the situation you are planning into. It has two halves.

    Looking at the company itself, to find out what it is actually capable of:

    Agency evaluation and selectionWhether the current agency can do this particular job

    External analysis

    Looking outward at the market. This half is mostly consumer behaviour, which is why the professor asked why it is necessary to review consumer buyer behaviour in IMC:

    Consumer behaviour analysisHow the customer decides
    Segmentation and target marketingWho the customer is
    Market positioningWhat the product should mean to them

    Consumer behaviour, and why IMC needs it

    The answer to the professor's question is that each stage of the buying process needs a different kind of communication. Knowing which stage the customer is at tells you what the advertising has to do.

    Need recognition

    The customer notices a gap between where they are and where they want to be. Nothing else can start until this happens, so advertising at this stage works by creating or dramatising the gap. The six triggers named in class:

    Out of stockThe simplest trigger: the old one is finished
    DissatisfactionExpectation was greater than performance
    New needs or wantsCircumstances change and the need is new
    Related product purchasesBuying one thing creates the need for another
    Market-induced recognitionMarketing itself creates the felt need
    New productsA need the customer did not know they had
    Information search

    The customer looks for ways to solve the problem. The IMC job here is to be present and credible in whichever source this customer actually uses, so the planner has to know which one is most prominent for them:

    Personal cuesFriends, family, people they trust
    Commercial cuesAdvertising, salespeople, packaging, the company's own channels
    ExperienceTheir own past use of the product
    Evaluation of alternatives
    The customer compares the options against the things they care about. Advertising at this stage argues, rather than announces: it gives reasons to prefer this brand over the shortlist.
    Purchase decision
    The choice is made. Sales promotion and point-of-purchase work hardest here, because the job is to convert an intention into an act before the customer changes their mind.
    Post-purchase behaviour
    The customer decides whether they were right. Cognitive dissonancePost-purchase doubt about whether the decision was right.Promotion after the sale gives psychological support to reduce it. is the doubt that follows a significant purchase, and it needs a promotional message as psychological support: advertising aimed at people who have already bought, reassuring them that they chose well. Get this wrong and there is no repeat purchase.

    Four groups of factors shape all of this. They are worth knowing as groups, because an exam answer that names the group and one example inside it is complete.

    Cultural
    Culture, sub-culture and social class. The broadest and slowest-moving influences, and the ones that decide what is even acceptable to say.
    Social
    Reference groups, family, and roles and status. The people around the customer, whose opinion they weigh, sometimes without noticing.
    Personal
    Occupation, economic situation, personality, lifestyle, and age and life-cycle stage. What is true of this individual rather than their group.
    Psychological
    Motivation, perception, learning and attitude. What happens inside the customer's head when the message arrives, and why two people can see the same advertisement and take away different things.

    Assael's grid is the practical version of all this. It crosses how much the customer cares about the purchase with how different the brands actually are, and each cell needs a different kind of communication.

    Complex buying behaviour
    High involvement, many differences between brands. An expensive, unfamiliar, risky purchase such as a laptop. The customer researches, so advertising must inform in detail and give them material to justify the choice.
    Dissonance-reducing
    High involvement, few differences. Expensive but the brands are much alike, such as floor tiles. The customer buys quickly then worries. Advertising works hardest after the sale, reassuring.
    Variety seeking
    Low involvement, many differences. Cheap, and switching is part of the fun, such as snacks. The leader advertises to keep the shelf and build habit; challengers use sales promotion and trial offers to interrupt it.
    Habitual buying
    Low involvement, few differences. Bought on autopilot, such as salt. Advertising builds familiarity through repetition rather than argument, because there is no argument to make.

    Market segmentation

    Dividing the market into groups of customers with shared characteristics. It is essential because all customers are not the same, and a message written for everyone persuades no one. The four bases:

    Segment, differentiatedA separate offer for each of several segments

    Step 3: Analysis of the communication process

    Before spending money on a message, the planner has to understand how a message actually travels and where it can fail.

    Sender and receiver
    The two participants. The sender is the advertiser, the company itself. The receiver is the audience, the respondent, the customer. Everything between them is where things go wrong.
    Encoding

    The sender turning an idea into a message. The same idea can be encoded several ways, and the choice matters because it decides what the audience has to do to understand it:

    VerbalWords, spoken or written
    GraphicPictures, colour, layout
    MusicalA jingle or a soundtrack
    AnimationDrawn or generated imagery
    Decoding
    The receiver working out what the message means. DecodingThe receiver working out what the message actually means.If encoding and decoding do not match, the message fails. is done with the receiver's own experience and culture, not the sender's, so a message that is obvious in the boardroom can be meaningless or offensive to the audience. Communication succeeds only where the sender's and the receiver's fields of experience overlap.
    Channel

    The route the message takes. The choice trades reachThe percentage of the target audience exposed to the ad at least once.How many. against credibility:

    Personal channelsWord of mouth, personal selling. Few people reached, high trust, and the message can adapt
    Non-personal channelsPrint media, broadcast media. Many people reached, less trust, and the message is fixed
    Response and feedback
    What the receiver does, and what gets back to the sender. The response may be acceptance, rejection, or nothing at all. Viral marketing is the case where the response is the audience passing the message on themselves. Feedback is how the sender learns any of this happened, which is why measurement is built into the definition of IMC.

    The progression a response goes through

    A response does not arrive all at once. It moves through three stages, and naming the stage a campaign is targeting is what makes an objective specific.

    Cognitive
    The thinking stage. The audience becomes aware of the brand and takes in information about it. Advertising that informs works here, and the measure is awareness and knowledge.
    Affective
    The feeling stage. The audience forms a liking, a preference, a conviction. Advertising that persuades works here, and the measure is attitude and preference.
    Behavioural
    The doing stage. The audience acts: trial, then purchase. Sales promotion and point-of-purchase work hardest here, and the measure is the action itself.

    The AIDA ladder in Chapter 4 is this progression in more detail: awareness and expectation are cognitive, beliefs and attitude are affective, and trial and loyalty are behavioural.

    The celebrity as source

    The professor set two questions here: what is a celebrity, and how are celebrities selected. The source is part of the message, because the audience judges what is said partly by who says it.

    What a celebrity is
    A person widely recognized by the public for their personal achievement. Choosing one is a major task rather than a detail, because the celebrity's meaning transfers to the brand, and a mistake transfers too.
    Risks of using one

    Four ways it goes wrong:

    • The celebrity overshadows the product, so the audience remembers the star and forgets the brand
    • The celebrity is overexposed, endorsing so many products that the endorsement means nothing
    • The target audience is not receptive to celebrity endorsers at all, and finds the whole device unconvincing
    • The celebrity's behaviour poses a risk, because their scandal becomes the brand's scandal
    Celebrity credibility

    The extent to which the audience sees the source as having relevant knowledge, skill or experience, and trusts them to give unbiased information. A credible source persuades by being believed. It has two dimensions, and the professor's words for it split between them:

    ExpertiseKnowledgeable, skillful, experienced. Whether they would actually know
    TrustworthinessEthical, honest, believable, unbiased. Whether they would tell you straight
    Celebrity attractiveness

    The second source characteristic, which persuades not by being believed but by being liked. It has three parts:

    LikeabilityAffection for the source from their appearance, behaviour or personality
    FamiliarityThe audience knows them, through exposure over time
    SimilarityThe audience sees a resemblance between the celebrity and themselves
    Celebrity and brand matching
    The celebrity's personality must fit the brand's. A local brand takes a local celebrity and a global brand a global one, because the scale has to match too. Where the fit is wrong the audience notices the transaction rather than the endorsement.
    Celebrity and audience matching
    The celebrity must fit the people being sold to, not the people making the advertisement. A young target market takes a young celebrity; a target aged sixty takes someone older.
    Budget and conflict of interest
    What the celebrity costs, set against what they add. And whether they have already signed with a competitor, which would hand your spending to the other brand.

    Step 4: Budget determination

    Four methods were taught. They are not equally good, and the differences are the point: the first three set the budget from something other than the job to be done, and only the last one starts from the job.

    Affordable method
    The company works out what it must spend on production and operations, and gives promotion whatever is left. The task to be performed is never considered, so under-spending and over-spending are both likely. Common among small enterprises, where the logic is that you cannot get into trouble spending money you have. It is safe in an accounting sense and poor management in a marketing sense.
    Percentage-of-sales method
    The budget is a fixed percentage of salesSet the budget as a fixed percentage of current or forecast sales.Say 3 or 5 percent. Used by medium companies., say three or five percent, taken either from current sales or from forecast sales. The most commonly used method, particularly in large firms, because it is simple and keeps spending proportionate. Its flaw is that it reverses cause and effect: it treats advertising as a result of sales, so when sales fall the budget is cut at exactly the moment more support is needed. Typically used by medium enterprises.
    Competitive parity method
    The budget is set to match what competitors spend, using industry sales data and published competitive figures. The argument for it is that it uses the collective wisdom of the industry and keeps the market stable, because nobody starts a spending war. The argument against is that it assumes the competitors know what they are doing and that their situation is yours, and it guarantees you can never out-communicate them.
    Objective and task method

    The only method that starts from the work. Three steps:

    1. Determine the promotion objectives, such as awareness or market share
    2. Determine the tasks needed to achieve them
    3. Estimate the cost of performing those tasks, and that total is the budget

    The IMC planner must know which method the company follows, because it decides whether the budget can be argued with. Six factors affect how large the budget needs to be:

    Stage in the product life-cycle
    A new product needs heavy spending to create awareness from nothing; an established one needs less, to remind.
    Product durability
    A product bought once a decade needs different support from one bought weekly.
    Hidden product qualities
    If the benefit cannot be seen, advertising has to explain it, which costs more.
    Product price
    A high price raises the customer's perceived risk, so more communication is needed to justify it.
    Differentiation
    A product much like its rivals must spend more to be noticed at all.
    Purchase frequency
    Frequently bought products need continuous presence to stay in mind at the moment of choice.

    Advertising

    The one tool the professor worked through in full, using the five-question template from step 5: definition, uniqueness, objectives, major decisions, evaluation. It is the heaviest chapter in the course and the one to know best.

    What advertising is

    Advertising is any paid form of non-personal presentation and promotion of goods, services or ideas by an identified sponsor. It is the largest tool of promotion. Three words in that definition are doing the work, and an exam answer should take them one at a time.

    Paid
    The sponsor buys the time or the space. This is what separates advertising from publicity, where a newspaper carries the story because it judges the story worth carrying. Paying buys control over what is said and when, and costs credibility, because the audience knows it was bought.
    Non-personal
    It goes to many people at once with no individual contact, so there is no opportunity to answer a question, read the customer's reaction, or adapt. That is the trade for reaching a large audience cheaply.
    Identified sponsor
    The audience is told who is speaking. This is a legal and ethical requirement as much as a practical one, and it is what makes advertising accountable for its claims.
    What can be advertised
    Not just goods. The ten entities of marketing are products, services, ideas, persons, places, experiences, events and organizations among others, and all of them are advertised. The form can be video, printed or audio, which is why the term paper asks for one of each.
    Platforms

    Where advertising runs. The platform must match the brand image, which is the choice from Chapter 1:

    BroadcastTelevision, radio
    PrintNewspaper, magazine, journal
    Outdoor and physicalBillboard, poster, leaflet, a wall, a vehicle, a jersey, point-of-purchase displays
    PackagingInner and outer, reaching the buyer at the moment of use
    DigitalWebsite, social media

    Uniqueness: what advertising can do that no other tool can

    Impersonal and non-personal
    The audience is not obliged to pay attention, and nothing compels a response. This is a real weakness: a salesperson in front of you gets a reply, an advertisement does not. Everything else about advertising is arranged to overcome it.
    Public presentation
    Because everyone sees the same message, the product gains a kind of legitimacy. A publicly advertised product is understood to be a standard, legitimate offering, and the buyer knows others will recognise their choice.
    Amplified expressiveness
    Advertising can dramatise the company and its product through artful use of print, sound, colour and motion. It can make an ordinary product feel significant, which no price list or datasheet can do.
    Pervasiveness
    The message can be repeated many times, and the buyer can compare the messages of competing sellers. The same advertisement can run for fifteen years. Repetition is also how advertising says something about the seller: a company that advertises heavily is understood to be large, successful and confident.

    Functions and objectives

    The functions are what advertising does; the objectives are what a particular campaign is set up to achieve. They overlap heavily, and the professor taught them as two versions of one list.

    Informing
    The basic, first function. Telling the market a new product exists, explaining how it works, suggesting new uses, and correcting false impressions. Dominant when a product is new, because nobody can want something they have not heard of.
    Persuading
    The second function. Building selective demandDemand for a particular brand, such as Nestlé coffee.Most advertising builds selective demand. for this brand rather than the category: encouraging switching, changing perceptions of the brand's attributes, and convincing the customer to buy now. Dominant once competitors exist.
    Reminding
    Keeping the brand in mind when the customer is not buying, and keeping it top of mind for the moment they are. Coca-Cola keeps running new advertising for a product everyone already knows, precisely so that it is not forgotten. Off-season reminders work the same way.
    Adding value
    Changing how the product is perceived, so that the same physical product is experienced as better. Innovation, quality improvement and perception are the three ways value is added, and advertising handles the third.
    Assisting other company efforts
    Advertising makes the other tools work better. It establishes brand credibility so the salesperson is not starting from nothing, and it announces the sales promotion that a coupon then delivers.

    How advertising works

    Advertising does not convert a stranger into a loyal customer in one exposure. It moves them one rung at a time, and each rung needs a different message, which is why the campaign objective has to name a rung.

    Unawareness to awareness
    The customer does not know the brand exists, then does. Pure reach and repetition work here; there is nothing to argue yet.
    Expectation and trial
    The customer forms an idea of what the brand would be like, then tries it. Advertising creates the expectation; sales promotion often triggers the trial, which is why the two tools are planned together.
    Beliefs and belief reinforcement
    Having used the product, the customer forms beliefs about it. Experience now does most of the work, and advertising reinforces what the experience taught.
    Attitude and attitude reinforcement
    Beliefs harden into an overall attitude toward the brand, which advertising keeps reinforcing so that it does not decay or get displaced by a competitor.
    Brand loyalty
    The customer chooses the brand without reconsidering. This is the destination, and it is why reminder advertising continues long after everyone knows the product.

    The ladder as a whole is also labelled the AIDA model.

    Classification of advertising

    Advertising is classified by who it is aimed at, because that decides the medium, the message and the measure.

    National advertising
    Run nationwide by the brand owner, to build the brand itself rather than to move stock in one shop. It talks about what the brand is and what it stands for.
    Retail or local advertising
    Run by a retailer in one region, to bring people into a particular store. It talks about price, location and opening hours, and it is judged on traffic this weekend.
    Primary versus selective demand
    Primary demandDemand for the product category as a whole, such as coffee.Contrast selective demand. is demand for the whole product category, as in an advertisement that tells you to drink coffee. Selective demand is demand for one brand, as in one that tells you to drink Nestlé coffee. Building primary demand helps every competitor, so it is normally only worthwhile for a category leader, or for a trade association advertising on behalf of a whole industry. Nearly all brand advertising builds selective demand.
    B2B advertising
    Aimed at people buying on behalf of an organization, for use in its own operations. It is informational and specification-led, and often runs in trade publications and catalogues, because the reader is looking for a supplier rather than being interrupted.
    Professional advertising
    Aimed at licensed professionals such as doctors and engineers, to get them to use or specify the product for the people they advise. The professional is not the buyer, but they control the choice.
    Trade advertising
    Aimed at wholesalers and retailers, to persuade them to stock and push the product. It argues in the trade's terms: margins, discounts, allowances, commission and turnover, not consumer benefits.

    What makes advertising effective, and creative

    Desirable qualities

    From the slides, what separates advertising that works from advertising that merely runs:

    • It should never promise more than it can deliver, because a promise the product fails is worse than no promise
    • It must be informative, giving the audience something they did not have
    • The message must be clear and not complex, because the audience gives it a second of attention
    • It must align with the overall marketing strategy, or it pulls against the rest of the plan
    • It must highlight the product's value and the consumer's benefit, not the company's pride in itself
    • It must be creative, and find a unique way to break through and gain attention
    • It must be persuasive, and give a reason to act

    Universal standards of creative advertising

    The professor marked this very important. D'Arcy Masius Benton and Bowles's universal advertising standards are a checklist of nine questions to ask of any advertisement before it runs. The first five are the ones usually quoted; the full checklist has nine.

    Does it position the product simply, with unmistakable clarity?
    The audience must see in a flash what the product is for, who it is for, and why they should care. Without a clear position no creative work can begin.
    Does it bolt the brand to a clinching benefit?
    The advertising must be built on the most compelling consumer benefit, not on some unique but insignificant feature. Before deciding how to say it, be sure you are saying the right thing.
    Does it contain a Power Idea?
    The Power IdeaThe core creative idea that turns the strategy into a dynamic creative concept.Third of the D'Arcy Masius Benton and Bowles standards. is the vehicle that transforms the strategy into a creative concept.     Does it design in brand personality?
    The brand should come across as a consistent character the audience can recognise across every advertisement, not just a set of claims.
    Is it unexpected?
    It must do something the audience has not seen from this category, or it will be filtered out with everything else.
    Is it single-minded?
    One message. An advertisement that makes three points communicates none of them.
    Does it reward the prospect?
    The audience should get something for their attention: information, entertainment, or a feeling. Attention is a payment and it has to be earned.
    Is it visually arresting?
    The visual has to stop someone who was not looking for it.
    Does it exhibit painstaking craftsmanship?
    Execution quality is itself a signal. Careless work implies a careless company.

    Message decision: appeal and execution

    Message strategy is what the advertisement says. It has two parts: creating the message, and selecting the appeal. Creating the message means focusing on customer benefits, with a statement that is plain, straightforward and tied to the positioning. The appeal is the underlying content of the message, the reason it gives the audience to respond.

    Rational appeal
    Speaks to the audience's self-interest and shows that the product delivers a practical benefit: quality, value, performance, economy. A buy-one-get-one-free offer is a rational appeal. Works best for high-involvement purchases where the customer is actually comparing, and for business buyers who must justify the decision.
    Emotional appeal

    Works on feeling rather than argument. Used where the brands are much alike, or where the benefit is hard to prove. The feelings named in class fall into two groups:

    PositiveAssociating the brand with a feeling the audience wants: affection, love, joy, happiness, pleasure, comfort, nostalgia, excitement, stimulation, pride, achievement, self-esteem, ambition, prestige
    NegativeShowing the problem the product removes: fear, sorrow, grief, embarrassment, and the need for safety and security
    Moral appeal
    Directed at the audience's sense of what is right: the environment, corporate social responsibility, well-being, ethical issues. Used for social causes, and by brands whose positioning is built on values.

    Evaluating advertising effectiveness

    Marked by the professor as a likely question. Evaluation measures against the objective that was set in decision one, which is why that objective had to be specific.

    Surveys
    Asking the audience directly, before and after the campaign, to measure what changed in their heads: awareness, recall, attitude, purchase intention.
    Communication effect
    Whether the advertising communicated what it was meant to communicate. This is what a communication objective is measured against, and it is the fair test of advertising, because it isolates the part advertising controls.
    Sales effect
    Whether sales moved. The measure management wants, and the hardest to attribute, because price, distribution, competitors and the economy all move sales too. This is why campaigns usually set communication objectives and treat sales as the longer-term test.

    After the Midterm Line: Sales Promotion

    The midterm syllabus ends with advertising. Sales promotion was taught just after that line, and it is here for two reasons: it is the second tool box of the model, and it follows the same five-question template, which makes it the best check that you can apply the template to a tool other than advertising.

    Sales promotion

    What it is

    A short-term incentive to encourage the immediate purchase or sale of a product or service: a mass-communication technique that offers a short-term incentive. The contrast with advertising is the point. Advertising works on attitudes over months; sales promotion works on behaviour this week, by changing the deal rather than the perception. The forms named in class:

    Price-basedDiscount, price-off, cashback, rebate, refund, allowance, commission
    Quantity-basedExtra quantity, buy one get one free, bonus pack
    Trial-basedFree samples, coupons
    Loyalty-basedMembership and frequency programmes
    Uniqueness

    What sales promotion can do that the other tools cannot:

    • It invites immediate purchase. Advertising asks the customer to remember; a promotion asks them to act now, and gives a reason to not wait
    • It draws attention, because an offer interrupts a routine choice
    • It is measurable. Coupons redeemed and units moved are counted directly, unlike a change in attitude
    • It gives a real incentive or reward, such as a free tiffin box or toothbrush, and that reward must integrate with the product rather than being an unrelated bribe
    Objectives

    What it is used to achieve:

    Stock clearanceMoving inventory that is ageing or overbought
    TrialGetting new customers to try the product for the first time, the hardest single step in the buying process
    Repeat purchaseMembership and frequency programmes that make the next purchase more attractive than switching
    Increase consumptionMore sales from existing users
    Increase market sharePulling volume from a competitor
    Enhance the IMC processGiving the other tools something concrete to announce
    Get the trade to stockA retailer objective rather than a consumer one

    Why sales promotion grew

    Spending shifted from advertising to sales promotion over several decades, and the reasons are mostly about power moving down the channel and brands becoming harder to tell apart.

    Growing power of retailers
    A few large chains control access to the shelf, and they demand trade promotion in exchange for it. The manufacturer's budget follows that demand.
    Declining brand loyalty
    Customers switch more readily than they did, so a promotion can actually move them, where once it would only have subsidised a purchase they would have made anyway.
    Increased promotional sensitivity
    Customers have learned to wait for offers and to respond to them, which makes promotion effective and simultaneously trains them to expect it.
    Brand proliferation
    Too many similar brands in each category. When brands are hard to distinguish, a price incentive is the fastest way to be chosen.
    Fragmentation of consumer markets
    Mass advertising reaches a smaller share of the market than it used to, so budget moves to tools that act at the point of purchase.
    Short-term focus of marketers
    Managers are judged on quarterly results, and promotion delivers measurable volume inside a quarter in a way that brand advertising does not.
    Increased accountability
    Management demands proof of return. Promotion can show it and advertising struggles to, so promotion wins the argument for the budget.
    Competition and clutter
    Heavy competition and a crowded media environment make it harder to be noticed by advertising alone, so companies reach for an offer.
    Technological advancement
    Scanner data, loyalty cards and digital coupons made promotions easy to target, deliver and measure.

    Tools

    Consumer-oriented, a pull strategy

    Aimed at the final buyer, to make them ask for the product and so pull it through the channel:

    SamplesRemove the risk of trial entirely
    CouponsDiscount only for the price-sensitive customer who bothers to use them
    PremiumsAn extra item given with the purchase
    Contests and sweepstakesBuy attention and engagement rather than give a discount
    Refunds and rebatesPaid after purchase, so fewer claim them than respond to them
    Bonus packsExtra quantity at the same price
    Price-off dealsA straight temporary reduction
    Frequency programmesReward cumulative purchases
    Event marketingAssociate the brand with an occasion
    Trade-oriented, a push strategy

    Aimed at wholesalers and retailers, to make them stock, display and push the product:

    Contests and incentivesFor the retailer's own staff
    Trade allowances and discountsFor buying or displaying the product
    Point-of-purchase displaysSupplied by the manufacturer
    Training programmesSo the retailer's staff can sell the product properly
    Trade showsWhere the trade comes to be sold to
    Cooperative advertisingThe manufacturer pays a share of the retailer's advertising

    Major decisions

    Five decisions, with the slides breaking the third one open into five more.

    1 Establish the sales promotion objective
    Which of the objectives above this promotion is for. Trial and stock clearance need completely different mechanics.
    2 Select the sales promotion tools
    Consumer-orientedSales promotion aimed at the final buyer to pull the product through the channel.Samples, coupons, premiums, contests, rebates, bonus packs. or trade-orientedSales promotion aimed at retailers and distributors to push the product through the channel.Allowances, POP displays, training, trade shows, cooperative advertising., and which tool within that. Follows directly from the objective.
    3 Develop and implement the programme

    The slides expand this step into five decisions of its own:

    Size of the incentiveToo small and nobody acts, too large and the margin is gone
    Conditions for participationWho qualifies, and how the offer is kept from being abused
    How to promote and distribute itThe offer is worthless if the customer never learns of it
    Length of the programmeLong enough to reach everyone, short enough to still feel urgent
    EvaluationSales effect, repeat purchase, loyalty, market performance
    4 Determine the length of the programme
    Too short and it does not reach everyone; too long and customers stop treating it as a reason to hurry, which removes the whole point of a short-term incentive.
    5 Evaluate the programme
    Against sales effect, repeat purchase, loyalty and market performance. The critical question is whether the volume was incremental or simply bought forward from customers who would have purchased anyway.
