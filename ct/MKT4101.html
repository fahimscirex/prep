Customer Perception and Satisfaction

Zeithaml, Chapter 4 · part 1 of 3

What customer perception is

Customer perception of services is the customer's subjective assessment of, and feelings about, a company, a brand or its offerings. It covers their thoughts, emotions and opinions about the brand and its products or services. It can be positive or negative, and it forms whenever the customer interacts with the brand, its products or its services. It matters because it shapes customer loyaltyThe customer's tendency to keep coming back to the same firm.Driven by satisfaction, but only strongly at the very top of the scale., brand image and, in the end, business success.

The book adds two points that sit underneath every answer in this chapter. First, perceptions are always judged relative to expectations, and because expectations shift, the same service can be rated differently over time, from person to person and from culture to culture. Second, everything here is about the customer's perception of the service, not some objective standard of what the service ought to be.

Perceptions of quality and satisfaction

The chapter turns on one figure. Satisfaction is the broad judgement, and service quality is only one of the things that feeds it.

Service quality
A focused evaluation of the five dimensions: reliability, responsiveness, assurance, empathy and tangibles.
Customer satisfaction
The broader judgement. It is influenced by service quality, product quality and price, and also by situational factors and personal factors.
Customer loyalty
The outcome. Satisfaction leads to loyalty, which is why companies care about satisfaction at all.
Watch the arrow

The slide's redraw of this figure points the last arrow from loyalty into satisfaction. The book points it the other way: satisfaction leads to loyalty. Draw it the book's way.

Satisfaction versus service quality book only

People use the two words as if they meant the same thing. Researchers agree they do not: they have different causes and different outcomes. Satisfaction is the broader concept, and perceived service quality is one component of it. A health club shows the difference. Its service quality is judged on whether the equipment works, how responsive the staff are and how skilled the trainers are. Satisfaction with the club also takes in the quality of the products sold in its shop, the membership price, the customer's own mood, and even the weather on the drive there.

Transaction versus cumulative perceptions book only

Transaction-specific
The perception of one encounter, such as how a bank customer was treated by one teller on one visit. Best for diagnosing a service problem and fixing it straight away.
Cumulative
The overall perception built from every encounter over time: branch visits, online banking, ATMs in many cities, and even banking as an industry. A better predictor of loyalty, because loyalty comes from all the experiences together, not from one.

The two views are complementary, not competing. Single encounters are the building blocks; the cumulative view is the wall.

Customer satisfaction

Two definitions were given in class. Customer satisfaction measures how well a company's products, services or overall experience meet or exceed customer expectations and needs. Put more simply, it measures how happy customers are with a company's products, services and capabilities.

The book's formal definition comes from Richard Oliver: satisfaction is the consumer's fulfillment response. It is a judgment that a product or service feature, or the product or service itself, provides a pleasurable level of consumption-related fulfillment. In plain terms, it is the customer's evaluation of whether the product or service met their needs and expectations. Failing to meet them produces dissatisfaction.

What satisfaction can feel like book only

Satisfaction is not one feeling. Depending on the service it shows up as:

ContentmentA passive response to routine services the customer barely thinks about.
PleasureFor services that make the customer feel good.
DelightFor services that surprise the customer in a positive way.
ReliefWhen the service removes something negative.
AmbivalenceWhen the experience was a mix of positive and negative.

Satisfaction is also a dynamic target. It is usually measured at one point in time as if it were fixed, but it moves through the service cycle, and for a new service the expectations it is judged against are themselves still forming.

What determines customer satisfaction

Beyond quality and price, five determinants are named, and they are on the slide exactly as the book lists them.

Five determinants
1Product and service featuresThe customer's evaluation of the specific features. For a resort hotel: the pool, golf access, restaurants, room comfort, staff courtesy, price. Customers trade features off against each other, price against quality against friendliness.
2Customer emotionsStable moods the customer brings in, such as being on holiday, and emotions the service itself creates. Emotions matter most in high-emotion services such as birth, marriage, illness and death. In river rafting, the guide's effect on the rafters' emotions drove their satisfaction with the whole trip.
3AttributionsThe causes a customer believes lie behind an outcome.Who they blame changes how satisfied they end up. for service success or failureAttributions are the perceived causes of events. When the outcome surprises the customer, they look for the reason, and who they blame changes how satisfied they are. A dieter who fails to lose weight asks whether it was the plan or themselves.
4Perceptions of equity and fairnessWas I treated as well as other customers? Did I pay a fair price for what I got and the effort I spent? Fairness matters most after a service failure.
5Other customers, family members and coworkersSatisfaction is shaped by other people: a family's reactions to a holiday, the other rafters on the boat, or how colleagues use and talk about a new piece of software.

National customer satisfaction indexes book only

Many countries track satisfaction nationally, as a measure of the quality of economic output to sit beside the usual measures of quantity. Sweden was first, with the Swedish Customer Satisfaction Barometer in 1989, followed by Germany, the United States and Switzerland. The American Customer Satisfaction Index (ACSIThe American Customer Satisfaction Index, a national measure of satisfaction across about 200 firms.Run by the University of Michigan, updated quarterly.), developed at the University of Michigan, tracks about 200 firms quarterly, with roughly 250 customer interviews per company. Each firm's score is built from its customers' perceptions of quality, value, satisfaction, expectations, complaints and future loyalty. The pattern worth remembering: goods cluster at the top of the ACSI table and services fill the bottom, with subscription television and Internet service providers lowest of all.

Outcomes of customer satisfaction book only

Satisfaction is linked to loyalty and profit. Firms that beat competitors on the ACSI have earned superior returns at lower risk, and the ACSI stock portfolio has consistently outperformed the S&P 500. But the relationship with loyalty is not a straight line. It is only strong at the very top of the scale.

Xerox found that customers rating it 5, very satisfied, were six times more likely to repurchase than those rating it 4. Enterprise Rent-A-Car found the top rating made a customer three times more likely to rent again than the second-highest. The lesson is that merely satisfying customers is not enough to keep them; the firm has to more than satisfy, or delight, them. At the other end, dissatisfaction is strongly linked to defection, and loyalty can collapse once dissatisfaction passes a threshold or touches a critical attribute.

Consequences of customer dissatisfaction

The slide lists five consequences. This list comes from the slide rather than the book, but every item follows from the book's satisfaction-loyalty link.

Five consequences
1Negative word of mouthCustomers telling other people about their experience.Bad word of mouth reaches people the firm never met.The dissatisfied customer tells others, and that warns off customers the firm never even met.
2Reduction in loyalty and customer lifetime valueThe total value of everything a customer would buy from the firm over the whole relationship.Losing a customer loses all of it, not just the next sale.The customer buys less, then leaves, so the value of the whole future relationship is lost, not just one sale.
3Lead generation becomes harderWith a damaged reputation, winning new prospects costs more.
4Lower salesFewer repeat purchases and fewer new customers together.
5Employee churnStaff who face unhappy customers every day burn out and leave, which then makes the service worse again.

Service Quality

Zeithaml, Chapter 4 · part 2 of 3

What service quality is

Service quality is how well a service meets or exceeds customer expectations: the customer compares the performance they perceived with what they expected. Efficiency, courtesy and responsiveness matter most, which separates it from product quality, where the focus is on tangible features. For pure services such as health care, banking or education, service quality is the main thing customers judge.

Outcome, interaction and physical environment book only

Outcome quality
The technical result. For a lawsuit, how the case ended; for a restaurant, the meal.
Interaction quality
How the result was delivered: calls returned on time, courtesy, listening, empathy.
Physical environment quality
The surroundings: the decor of the law office or the restaurant.

The five dimensions

Customers do not judge quality as one thing. They judge it on five dimensions, found by Parasuraman, Zeithaml and Berry and later measured with the SERVQUALThe survey scale built to measure the five service quality dimensions.Covered properly in chapter 5; here you only need the name. scale. An easy way to hold them is the initials RATER.

Five dimensions
ReliabilityPerforming the promised service dependably and accurately. Usually the most important of the five. FedEx built its position on it.
ResponsivenessWillingness to help and to provide prompt service. Customers judge it by how long they wait for help, answers or a fix.
AssuranceEmployees' knowledge and courtesy, and their ability to inspire trust and confidence. Matters most where the service feels risky: banking, insurance, medical, legal.
EmpathyCaring, individualised attention. Making customers feel understood and treated as individuals, which is where small firms can beat large ones.
TangiblesThe appearance of facilities, equipment, staff and communication materials. New customers lean on these most.
Not every dimension applies every time

Customers use the dimensions that fit the situation. Empathy hardly matters at an ATM, and tangibles do not matter in a phone call to book a repair.

E-service quality

Online, the dimensions change. E-service quality (E-S-QUALThe scale for e-service quality: how well a website supports shopping, buying and delivery.Seven dimensions, four core and three for recovery.) is the extent to which a website makes shopping, buying and delivery efficient and effective. Seven dimensions were found: four for ordinary use, and three that only come into play when something goes wrong.

Core dimensions

Used when there are no problems.

EfficiencyThe ease and speed of accessing and using the site.
FulfillmentHow far the site's promises about delivery and item availability are kept.
System availabilityThe correct technical functioning of the site.
PrivacyHow safe the site is and how well it protects customer information.
Recovery dimensions

Used when the customer has a problem or question.

ResponsivenessEffective handling of problems and returns through the site.
CompensationHow far the site compensates customers for problems.
ContactAvailability of help by phone or from an online representative.

Customer effort book only

A separate driver of loyalty. Research on customer-service contacts found loyalty depended less on delighting customers than on how little effort they had to spend to get a problem solved. High effort drives customers away; delight does not reliably keep them. So firms should prevent repeat calls, handle the emotional side of the contact, avoid forcing a channel switch, and genuinely solve the problem. The limit: for a wedding, a degree or a special holiday, customers expect to invest effort, and other things decide their loyalty.

Service Encounters

Zeithaml, Chapter 4 · part 3 of 3

Service encounter, or moment of truth

A service encounter is any moment when the customer deals with the service firm: in person, by phone, or through some other medium. It is a social interaction, and it is where something changes hands between provider and customer, whether goods, information or both. The book calls encounters the building blocks of customer perceptions, because each one is where a promise is kept or broken, and the customer's overall view of the firm is assembled from them.

The encounter cascade book only

Encounters link into a chain, and the chain becomes the customer's whole experience. Some services have a handful of encounters; Disney counts about 74 in a park visit, and one bad one can sour the whole day. Early encounters weigh most: Marriott found four of its top five loyalty drivers happen in the guest's first ten minutes.

Why every encounter matters book only

First encounters
With a new customer, the first contact is often the only evidence they have. A rude phone call can lose the job before the firm's real skill is ever seen.
Repeated encounters
Each one adds to a composite image. Mostly good builds a strong image; a mix of good and bad leaves the customer unsure and open to competitors.
Key encounters
Some count more than others. In hospitals, nursing contact predicts satisfaction better than meals or discharge.
Momentous encounters
A single event can end a long relationship, or bind a customer for life. The book's example: a bank refused to validate a long-time customer's parking ticket over a few cents, and he moved his million-dollar account to a rival.

Types of service encounters

Three types. A customer may meet the firm through any one of them, or all three.

Remote
No human contact at all: an ATM, a website, an automated phone line, or a bill arriving by post or e-mail. With no person to judge, the customer judges the tangible evidence and how well the technical system works.
Technology-mediated
A real person, reached in real time through technology: a phone call, live chat or text exchange. Judged on tone of voice, the employee's knowledge, and how efficiently the issue is handled. In text, word choice and tone carry everything.
Face-to-face
Direct personal contact between employee and customer. The hardest to get right: verbal and non-verbal behaviour, dress, equipment and setting all count, and the customer's own behaviour shapes the result too.

Sources of satisfaction and dissatisfaction

Thousands of encounter stories, gathered with the critical incident techniqueA research method that collects customers' own stories of very good and very bad encounters.The four themes came out of thousands of these stories., sort into four themes. Each theme starts from a different trigger.

Four themes
1RecoveryThe employee's response to a failure of the service delivery system: a missing room, a delayed flight, the wrong item sent. What the customer remembers is how the employee responded.
2AdaptabilityThe employee's response to a special need or request. Customers judge how flexible the employee and the system are, and are pleased when the firm bends for them.
3SpontaneityUnprompted and unsolicited employee actions, with no failure and no request behind them. Good ones feel like a pleasant surprise; bad ones include rudeness, ignoring, discrimination.
4CopingThe employee's response to problem customers who will not cooperate. Rarely satisfying for the customer, and customers almost never tell these stories about themselves.

Dos and don'ts for each theme

ThemeDoDon't
RecoveryAcknowledge the problem · explain causes · apologise · compensate or upgrade · lay out options · take responsibilityIgnore or blame the customer · leave them to fend for themselves · downgrade · act as if nothing is wrong · pass the buck
AdaptabilityRecognise how serious the need is · acknowledge · anticipate · try to accommodate · adjust the system · explain rules · take responsibilityIgnore · promise but not follow through · show unwillingness to try · embarrass or laugh at the customer · avoid responsibility · pass the buck
SpontaneityTake time · be attentive · anticipate needs · listen · provide information · show empathyShow impatience · ignore · yell, laugh or swear · steal from customers · discriminate

Technology-based encounters

The same logic carries over to websites, kiosks, apps and automated phone lines, with some differences book only. Customers still expect the core promise kept: the technology must work and meet their need quickly. Their biggest complaint is weak recovery, when a failure forces them to phone the firm, the very thing they were avoiding. They also expect personalisation, which is why Amazon and Netflix recommend from each customer's history. And they adopt the technology only when it beats the alternative, mainly on convenience.

Not everyone adopts, though. Customers fall into five groups by how they take to technology.

Five categories
Skeptics (38%)Somewhat detached from technology; neither strongly for nor against it.
Explorers (18%)Highly motivated to try new technologies.
Avoiders (16%)Resistant to new technologies and not motivated to try them.
Pioneers (16%)Hold strong positive and strong negative views about technology at once.
Hesitaters (13%)Not very innovative, so less likely to use new technologies.
