The Nature of the Pricing Process

Slides: Chapter 01, Introduction to pricing strategies and practices · Book: Hanna and Dodge, Chapter 1 · plus Kotler on value-based pricing and Smith on who sets price

This chapter answers three questions. What is a price, and who in the company decides it? What five things must you know before setting one? And what are the three basic ways to arrive at a number?

Why pricing matters

The book opens with a quote from McKinsey consultants Marn and Rosiello: "The fastest and most effective way for a company to realize its maximum profit is to get its pricing right." The right price raises profit faster than selling more units would. The wrong price shrinks it just as fast. Many managers, without realizing it, leave money "on the table" at the point where the product meets the customer.

Pricing is the most visible decision management makes, because it hits performance and profit directly. Getting it wrong in either direction costs money. Too high a price deflates demand, and, as IBM found, it can create a price umbrellaWhen a leader's high price leaves room underneath it for competitors to come in cheaper and take customers.IBM is the book's example. A price that is too high does not just lose sales; it invites competition.: a gap underneath your price where competitors can enter cheaper and take share. Too low a price creates an opportunity costMoney you could have had but gave up. In pricing: the gap between what the market would have paid and the lower price you actually charged.Setting a price too low does not show up as a loss on any statement, which is why the book calls it money 'left on the table'.: the difference between what the market would have paid and what you actually charged. That money is gone and never shows up in any account.

Firms have won with opposite approaches. Fort Howard Paper, Dell Computer and MCI built a positional advantageA position in the market that competitors find hard to copy, won either by low price or by being different.Dell and MCI won it with low prices; Maytag, John Deere and Federal Express won it with differentiation and charge a premium. Both routes work. on low prices. Maytag, John Deere and Federal Express built one on being different, and charge a substantial premium. The book's summary: successful pricing "bridges the gap between internal costs and external market demand." Four things define a firm's options: its cost structure, its competitive status, its marketing strategies (especially what it competes on) and its organizational capabilities.

Critical factors in the pricing decision

The book names five things a marketer must understand before setting a price. The slides list the same five.

1 · Effective cost information

You need to know where costs come from and how they are shared out, and not only manufacturing costs: marketing, distribution, design and technology costs too. Many companies still use old cost-allocation systems that spread overhead on the basis of direct labour, which distorts the picture now that factory support, marketing and design cost so much more than labour. One better approach treats every activity in the company as support for making and delivering products, so all costs are product costs. Another separates scale-related costsCosts that fall as you make more of the same thing.The book's rule of thumb: they drop 15 to 25 percent per unit every time volume doubles. Contrast with variety-related costs, which rise 20 to 25 percent each time variety doubles., which fall as you make more (down 15 to 25 percent per unit each time volume doubles), from variety-related costsCosts that rise as you offer more different versions, models or options.Every time variety doubles, these rise 20 to 25 percent. Old accounting systems hide this by spreading overhead on direct labour, so a busy product line looks cheaper than it is., which rise as you offer more versions (up 20 to 25 percent each time variety doubles). A company that does not separate them will think its crowded product line is cheaper than it is.

2 · Customer valuation

Do not compete on price alone. Customers pay for the package, and different market targetsThe customer groups a company chooses to serve. Measuring perceived value is hard, for three reasons: differences between competing offers are hard to measure objectively, product qualities keep changing, and customers systematically distort features and benefits into their own perception of value. The answer is to accept the criteria customers actually use, and to monitor them continuously.

3 · Market targets

The total market can be split into segments because groups of customers differ. Those differences allow product and pricing differentials: no segment need buy the same product or pay the same price as another. This is where extra profit comes from, since some segments will pay more for a version of the package. The book's example is the San Luis Sourdough Company, which sells bread to supermarkets and speciality stores on three tiers: 97 cents wholesale for plain delivery (level 1); $1.02 if the store may return unsold day-old bread (level 2); another 3 cents if the company also stocks the shelf, bar-codes each bag and accepts returns (level 3).

The limit is the Robinson-Patman ActA U.S. law that makes it illegal to charge different buyers different prices for the same product, unless the difference can be justified.The three defences: the products are different, the costs of serving the buyers are different, or the buyers do not compete with each other. It comes up again with quantity, seasonal and promotional discounts in Chapter 7., which makes it unlawful to charge different buyers different prices for the same product. To avoid a charge of price discrimination you must show one of three things: the products are different; the same product costs different amounts to sell to different customer groups; or the customers do not compete with each other.

5 · Pricing strategies

Price is part of the marketing mix and serves the marketing objectives, which come from the firm's overall objectives. The question is which pricing strategy fits the situation. The book offers six questions as a guide for choosing:

Scale
Are purchases big enough to justify pricing separately for individual customers?
Consumer knowledge
Can customers judge the product's value in money terms and tell price levels apart?
Demand
Does price play an important role in the customer's decision?
Information
Can the marketer accurately measure how customers value the product and how much they will buy?
Competitive substitute
Are there close substitutes in the category that customers compare prices against?
Patronage
Will customers favour competitors for reasons that have nothing to do with price?

First, tactics that pay in the short run are discarded in favour of long-run customer relationships: raising prices on products in short supply, for example, is replaced by pricing that keeps customers loyal, because replacing a lost customer costs about five times what keeping one costs. Three major pricing approaches

The first two come from Kotler's Principles of Marketing; the book's own treatment of cost-based pricingSetting price by adding a desired profit to what the product costs to make and sell.Simple and common with small firms and regulated utilities, but it ignores what customers will pay, so the price can end up too high or too low. is folded in below.

Cost-based pricing

Cost-based pricing starts from what the product costs and adds the profit you want. You need three cost ideas. Fixed costA cost that stays the same no matter how much you produce, such as rent or salaries. Also called overhead.Spread over more units it gets smaller per unit, which is why volume matters so much to cost-based prices., also called overhead, does not change with output: rent, salaries. Variable costA cost that rises and falls directly with how much you produce, such as materials.Fixed plus variable equals total cost. changes directly with output: materials, packaging. Total cost is the two added together at a given level of output. Spread the fixed cost over more units and the cost per unit falls, which is why volume assumptions matter so much to a cost-based price.

The book explains why this approach is risky for most companies. It ignores the market. Cost plus a desired return can land too high, so volume is lower than it should be and customers drift to cheaper substitutes, or too low, so the firm earns less than it could. Either way the firm misses its profit potential because it never asked how the market would respond. Cost-based pricing survives where it fits: small firms, regulated industries like utilities and car insurance, where a government body approves price changes and acts as a stand-in for the market, and price-inelastic markets like petrol, where a rise in crude oil is simply passed through at the pump.

Competition-based pricing

Competition-based pricing sets price by looking at what competitors charge for similar products, then positioning at, above or below them. Chapter 7 covers it as the "meeting competition" objective: when price is a given, the firm works backwards and manages its costs to fit.

Constraints on pricing practices

Costs and market valuations are the main determinants, but four other things limit what a firm can do: the industry it is in, the structure of the market, general economic conditions, and the law. A surprising rule from the book: the more competitive the industry, the easier the pricing decision, because the market sets the price. That is why firms in competitive industries turn to nonprice competitionCompeting on anything other than price: advertising, positioning, customer satisfaction, selection.The book's observation: the more competitive an industry, the more firms turn to this, because competing on price alone leads nowhere., advertising to position the product and, more recently, competing on customer satisfaction. Category-bustingA retailer offering so huge a selection in one category that customers stop comparing on price.Selection and availability take the pressure off price. Economic conditions amplify pricing choices. It is hard to resist raising prices in good times and cutting them in a recession, but doing either weakens the firm's relationship with the market. Global firms face several economies at once. And the legal framework has spread from preserving competition into foreign trade, public policy and national interests.

Ethics in pricing

Setting a price can conflict with social values because the seller wants the best long-run return, which may be higher or lower than the market would set at that moment. High prices attract more comment, since they suggest excess profit. Low prices are questioned too, since they suggest less value or a trick. The dispute is really about the gap between cost and price, and critics quote profits in absolute rather than relative terms. Four practices the book says the public questions:

  • Price steps within a product line that are not justified by steps in value (a proliferation of models).
  • Branded goods priced far above generic equivalents (pharmaceuticals).
  • Disproportionately high prices for replacement parts (foreign cars).
  • "New" products priced above the value of what actually changed (false obsolescenceMaking a product seem out of date so people buy the 'new' one, when the change is worth less than the extra price.One of the four pricing practices the book says the public questions.).

Two practices are plainly wrong rather than debatable. A false referenceQuoting an 'original price' or 'comparable value' that misleads, or was never real, to make the current price look like a bargain.Unlike arguments about profit, this one is clear cut: it is deceptive and often illegal. uses "original price," "previously priced at" or "comparable value" to mislead, and the reference may simply be invented. Market Interpretations of Price

Slides: Chapter 2 · Book: Hanna and Dodge, Chapter 2. The slides follow the book section by section.

Chapter 1 looked at price from the company's side. This chapter looks at it from the customer's side: what a price tells people, and how they use it to judge quality, sort products and spot bargains. Everything here rests on one idea: price is information, not just a cost.

Two roles of price: allocation and information

The chapter opens with a sports economist's finding: there is "no relationship whatsoever between ticket prices and owners' costs." Ticket prices are set by what the market will bear. Owners cannot pass on star salaries until fans' perception of the game rises. Price, in other words, is set by perception, and price has two jobs.

Allocation role
Price decides who can buy a product and how much, which means it sets total demand. This has a social edge. Effective pricing that expands the market lets more people enjoy what society produces. Ineffective pricing can split society into haves and have-nots and shut out the poor.
Information role
Price sends signals. It positions a product by quality and by the social status of owning it, and it often shows what the buyer wants to be recognized for. Together with deals and promotions, price is therefore a powerful stimulus that can trigger buying.

Price as part of the marketing mix

Price is the only element of the mix that brings money in. Product, promotion and distribution all cost money. (Pricing below competitors to win sales is an implicit expense too, since it gives up profit.) Competition forces firms to serve customers better through superior products, more service and more effective pricing, and effective pricing is judged by product positioning and total profit over the product's life.

Table 2.1 in the book shows how quality and price combine to affect return on investmentProfit as a percentage of the money invested.Finance executives judge prices by it. Target return pricing in Chapter 7 is built around hitting a set ROI.. Firms with high relative quality and low relative price earn the highest returns. Firms with high quality and high price come a close second, with a 34 percent return, more than twice the 15 percent of low-quality, low-price firms. The worst performers are medium quality at a medium price: 2 percent. The lesson is that quality is what pays, and combining it with a competitive price pays most.

The elements of the mix are synergistic: together they produce more than the sum of the parts, provided each supports the others. Three kinds of fit:

Consistency
The logical fit between elements. A low price on a premium product is inconsistent, and so is selling a premium product through cheap stores. Premium products have excellent quality, prices above competitors and selective distribution through high-quality outlets; ordinary products have less quality, lower prices and mass distribution.
Integration
Elements actively working together. Heavy advertising plus a high price: the margin pays for the advertising, and the advertising creates the positioning that justifies the price (Ralph Lauren, Cadillac, Pella windows, Perrier). Also promotional pricingTemporary price cuts, rebates, cash-back deals and similar incentives used to boost sales for a while.Retailers use it for store traffic; manufacturers use it to win market share and get people to switch brands., where a lower price is combined with advertising, and front-load pricing, where the initial price includes the product plus extended service.
Leverage
Using each element where it earns the most. If rebates build share better than advertising or better distribution, invest in rebates. If price cuts start giving diminishing returns, shift the money elsewhere. A price discount is an investment measured in sales dollars not collected. Beer and soft drinks are markets where price is the element that gets leveraged.

Price as purchasing information

A price looks like the one concrete, objective fact about a product. To the customer it is neither: it is interpreted, and the interpretation is subjective. Customers use price psychologically in three ways: to signal quality, value and status; to sort products into categories; and to form impressions, especially the impression of getting a bargain.

Consumer interpretations of price

People's Express airline and MCI both changed their industries with drastically discounted, no-frills service. Competitors answered not with lower prices but by packaging price with quality and customer service. People's Express went out of business and MCI had to change repeatedly to match AT&T's service. What both forgot is that customers see several differences between competing offers, of which price is only one, and that price has more than economic meaning. Low prices attract customers, but competing only on low price is rarely sustainable.

Time implications

Customers, individual and industrial, treat delay in getting a product as a time cost. So products that are readily available command a premium. Atlas Door gets a premium for industrial doors by delivering in weeks instead of the industry's four months. Norwalk Furniture grew sales and its dealer network by cutting delivery to three weeks against a norm of two to three months. (JIT) systems, keeping almost no stock and relying on suppliers, now rank on-time delivery of quality goods above price. A firm can win by shortening delivery time instead of cutting price.

Psychological implications

Because customers use price to tell products apart and judge quality, a genuinely better product must be priced higher than lesser ones, or it will be read as lesser. Wilson Sporting Goods learned this with its Sting tennis racket. It looked and played like the Prince Graphite, but a manufacturing innovation let Wilson price it $100 lower, and the low price told buyers it was worse. Sales were poor until Wilson repositioned it for people who wanted graphite but thought they could only afford aluminium.

Some customers pay more for quality. Others pay more to avoid the risk of a cheap brand turning out badly: the higher price is a form of insurance when they cannot judge quality themselves. This matters more as the product concept broadens from physical features to customer service, and firms known for high service can charge more. Pricing Decisions by Manufacturers

This chapter is about the manufacturer, the company that makes the product and sells it to distributors and retailers. Three questions: where does a product's price sit within the company's range of products? What is the company trying to achieve with price? And what are the six named strategies and five named discounts a manufacturer can use?

The pricing environment

The chapter opens with Jack Welch of General Electric: globalization is a fact of life, growth is slower, "value buying is everywhere," and "if you're going to have the lowest cost and the highest quality product available, you've got to engage every mind that you have." Industry has changed: global competition, sharper focus on customers, more flexible operations, and changing customers and markets.

A manufacturer's prices affect the whole of society: consumers, government, labour, distributors and financial markets. Oil price rises moved world economies; the pricing of Procter & Gamble, DuPont, Unilever, Mitsubishi and Nestlé sends signals about economic expectations to everyone else. Pricing and product decisions are tied together. Companies now try to carve out a niche, a small viable segment served with a unique strategy, and price becomes a positioning tool that differs from segment to segment.

For any product the lower limit of the price range is set by costs and the upper limit by what the market will bear. Where the product actually sits between those limits is a management decision made product by product, and two things decide it: the segment or segments the company chooses to serve, and the product's position in the company's portfolioAll of a company's products seen together, each playing a different role.Not every product has to earn the same profit. Pricing objectives

Logically, pricing should start from the company's objectives. In practice many firms have no formal pricing objectives at all, only unstated, intuitive ones. Pressed, an executive will name profit, but investigation often reveals other implicit aims such as holding market share or keeping the factory busy. And companies that say they maximize profit are usually satisfiers, not maximizers. A landmark study of twenty large U.S. corporations (Lanzillotti) found five main pricing goals:

2 · Maintain or enhance market share

Firms seek share because large-share companies are generally more profitable. Share strategies are offensive or defensive. An offensive strategy aims to raise profitability by cutting price to win share: Henry Ford priced the Model T low against his engineers' advice, took half the market, and the volume drove costs down through scale and experience until the low price was profitable. 3 · Meet or prevent competition

The firm prices at about the industry average, adjusted for any special features of its product, company or distributors. Typical users are smaller firms that prefer to follow a leader, and large firms selling standardized or commodity products with no differentiation to exploit.

Pricing strategies

A pricing strategy is the firm's price position relative to competitors. A high-price strategy differentiates the product and builds a quality image; it goes with profit maximization. A low-price strategy aims for a larger market share by penetrating new or existing markets. Whichever is chosen shapes the rest of the mix: product features, distribution, advertising and promotion. Six named strategies follow. The first two are high-price, the other four are low-price, in increasing order of aggression.

01

Skimming

Charge the highest possible price in the short run. The product is an innovation or a desirable variation on what exists, so it enjoys a temporary monopoly and demand is price inelastic at first. It is short-run because competitors will arrive with similar products and end the premium. Why do it: R&D and launch promotion are expensive and small early production runs cost more per unit, so high margins recover the investment fast; the high price builds a quality image; and it is easier to lower a price later than to raise it. It recovers R&D fast, builds a quality image, and it is easier to cut a price later than to raise one. also segments the market: cut the price in steps and each cut brings in the next segment. Dealers like it because a high price means a high markup for them. It works best for conspicuous products (digital watches at launch), where quality differences are large or hard to judge, and for designer labels.

02

Prestige pricing

Unlike skimming, the high price is meant to stay high for the product's whole life, because the price itself carries prestige and quality and is part of why people buy. Luxury cars, cameras, perfumes and liquor: much of the satisfaction is the prestige of owning something expensive. Cutting the price has repeatedly reduced sales, because the volume gained from new buyers is smaller than the volume lost from upper-income buyers for whom the image is gone. Rising incomes and the quest for quality have dulled price sensitivity, and the book suggests the term should become quality pricing, which focuses the customer on how the product satisfies expectations rather than on status.

03

Penetration pricing

A low price to infiltrate a market and win a large share. 04

Expansionistic pricing

An exaggerated form of penetration: a very low price to build a mass market, sometimes at competitors' expense, where demand is highly elastic. Toyota and Honda entered the United States with standardized low-cost versions of the Corolla and Civic, then added more expensive models once accepted. 05

Preemptive pricing (stay-out pricing)

A low price set to make the market unattractive to would-be competitors, so they stay out. Best when the firm has no patent, no differential advantage and entry is easy. Delaying rivals gives the firm time to build share, cut costs through scale and experience, and become the recognized original. RCA chose it in 1955 for colour television: the low price bought time to improve the colour-tube technology, cut costs and establish leadership before competitors rushed in.

06

A price set low, usually below cost, to eliminate competitors. The firm accepts losses to harm rivals financially; once they are gone, prices go back up to profitable levels. Discounts and allowances

A manufacturer depends on good relationships with its distributors, who negotiate hard for the best price and terms. To pay them for their services and keep their cooperation, manufacturers offer discounts off the list price. There are five: quantity, trade (functional), seasonal, promotional and cash. Each is granted for a specific purpose, and a buyer who meets all the conditions can receive all five in a single transaction.

Quantity discounts

A quantity discountA lower price for buying more, based on units or on dollar value.Saves the seller shipping, billing and handling costs. Noncumulative quantity discount
Based on the size of a single order. Purpose: bigger orders. Pricing of Services

Slides: Lecture 04 · Book: Hanna and Dodge, Chapter 13. The slides follow the book closely.

Services are priced differently from goods because they are different: you cannot see one before you buy, store one for later, or make two exactly alike. This chapter covers the six characteristics that cause the trouble, how to cost a service, and the five strategies and five tactics for pricing one.

Characteristics of services

The chapter opens with Marshall McLuhan: "The process is the product. We say airline when we mean air transportation... The use of nouns obscures the fundamental nature of services, which are processes, not objects." A service cannot be grasped mentally or identified physically; it has to be experienced. It is not made and shipped; it is sold first and then produced and consumed. Its qualities can only be judged after purchase, during the experience. Goods and services combine: goods give tangibility to services (a souvenir from a show), and services augment goods (customer service with a car). Both create value, but six differences create special problems that need special marketing effort.

Intangibility
The fundamental difference. Every product mixes tangible and intangible elements; a service is dominated by the intangible. Compare travel counselling with a diamond. A diamond's worth is in cut, clarity, colour and carat, and the customer can picture it. Travel service is information and arrangements; the only tangible parts are tickets and forms. Products sit on a scale (Figure 13.1): travel is more intangible than interior decorating, which is more intangible than a home security system; among goods, cosmetics are less tangible than appliances. Pricing implication: the more intangible a service, the less customers can compare it with competitors, so the seller has more latitude in pricing. A financial database has more pricing freedom than a maintenance service. The cost of that freedom is that value is harder to communicate and the price harder to justify.
Simultaneous production and consumption
Goods are produced, sold, then consumed. Services are sold, then produced and consumed at the same time. So there is usually no inventory (information services are the exception), and the customer is part of the production process. The delivery system either goes to the customer or brings the customer to a location. Pricing implication: when the service travels, fixed costs are recovered through a standard service-call charge or hookup fee; when the customer travels, location costs are handled as in a factory.
Perishability
Services cannot be stored. An empty airline seat or a vacant motel room tonight cannot be sold tomorrow, so supply and demand are hard to synchronize. For peak demand (a convention at a motel): hire part-timers, work overtime, serve regular customers first and make others wait, turn business away, subcontract. For low demand (a lawn service in winter): cut prices, offer other services that use the idle resources, sell and advertise harder. Each of these adds direct cost and lowers profit contribution, and the extra cost of handling a peak rarely equals the extra cost of building up a trough. Pricing implication: the service marketer works with two estimates of profit contribution. For market-oriented pricing, use the lower one to find the break-even pointThe sales volume at which total revenue just covers total cost, so profit is zero.Fixed costs divided by the contribution each unit makes. Above it you profit, below it you lose.; for cost-plus pricing, use the higher direct cost.
Heterogeneity
Service performance varies from provider to provider, customer to customer and day to day: health care, a phone line, a beautician's attention. The more labour-intensive the service, the more it varies, because staff exercise judgment. Hair stylists have wide latitude; airlines and spectator sports are standardized. Pricing implication: the more customized the service, the less its direct costs can be standardized. One answer is standard costs for a menu of options, priced by the package chosen. But the value customers put on the price-value trade-off is theirs to decide, and no two customized services look alike to them: lawn treatment options are standard across companies, but the advice, care, call-backs and little extras of the person doing it are not, and those add value in the customer's mind. Where staff influence is low, as with software, price options separately and in packages (Table 13.1 below).
Complexity
The number of steps, options and sequences in a service. Medical services are highly complex; lawn care is simple. Services must be seen as interdependent systems, not disconnected parts.
Divergence
How much those steps vary because staff use discretion and adapt to the situation. Pricing thinks in terms of raising or lowering each.

Pricing decisions in services

Like goods marketers, service marketers must deliver features and benefits at an attractive price. Attractive means one of two things: a competitive price, lower than competitors' for the same level of service, or a premium price that is more than offset by added value. Whatever the service, customers evaluate three things: quality (does it achieve the promised or expected result?), consistency (is it the same every time?) and reliability (does the provider always deliver what was promised?). An attractive price is not simply a low one; it is strong value against those three.

Cost considerations

Service pricing has traditionally been cost-based rather than market-oriented. Because ordinary accounting does not measure the value of the people who deliver a service, service firms developed human-resource costing formulas. The job-jacketA costing method that charges each client for the time actually spent on their account, plus a share of overhead and idle time.Common in law and consulting. The weakness: time is a poor measure of skill, since a better lawyer may take fewer hours. method allocates people costs to each account by the time actually spent on it, and adds overhead plus the cost of unused time in proportion to that time.

The job-jacket example, worked

A lawyer earns $100,000 a year and has 2,000 chargeable hoursThe hours a professional can bill to clients in a year.Yearly salary divided by chargeable hours gives the hourly cost rate. $100,000 over 2,000 hours is $50 an hour. Two difficulties. First, is a time charge based on salary a fair measure of the effort in a service? People differ in education and experience, so a more competent person who finishes faster charges less than a less competent one who takes longer. Time standards, as in car repair, help a little, but service tasks vary too much. Second, fixed costs are hard to allocate when volume swings. Many people-intensive firms avoid both problems by charging a multiple of direct time costs. The deeper limitation is that a cost-based price may not match what the customer thinks the service is worth: too high and sales are lost, too low and profit is lost, and perhaps sales too, because customers expected more. Even equipment-intensive services, where allocation is easier, must match supply to demand, and the book lists the ways they fail: buying the wrong kind of capacity (airlines and jumbo jets), not adding overall capacity, creating imbalance between primary and support activities at a resort, or undercutting themselves with cheaper services that parallel dearer ones.

Market considerations

Because service costs are hard to tie to units of service, and because price strongly affects how often a service is used, a service price is more often based on the market's perception of value than on cost. The answer many successful service firms have found is to innovate constantly: each new feature adds value, and adding associated goods adds tangibility and identity (Walt Disney does both). Value pricing for services means estimating what the market feels the service is worth, so innovations must match the target market's main concerns. ServiceMaster, a contract housekeeping and maintenance provider, keeps enhancing its service for cost-conscious hospitals, controlling cost and price without cutting quality. Rural/Metro, a fire-protection and emergency-medical provider, competes on price and service level with remote-controlled equipment, non-union firefighters and an emphasis on prevention. Where possible, break the service into parts and analyse the benefit of each: which parts are essential, which can go, which additions would add value. Holiday Inns decided quality was essential, removed the extras found at expensive hotels, and improved quality through consistency.

Service pricing strategies

Price is "undoubtedly the most troublesome aspect" of marketing services, because both value and cost are hard to pin down. So service firms often just follow their major competitors, matching or slightly undercutting them. In many industries this produces price tiers: an upscale restaurant and an economy restaurant. Beyond following, the book gives five strategies.

Cost-based pricing

Cost-plus pricing charges the client the cost of the service plus a set percentage. It is common in professional services such as law, consulting and medicine, where the firm assumes it delivers distinctly better quality to its existing clients than competitors do, and that clients choose it for that reason; a law firm sells its staff's expertise and success rate. Costs also dominate over market considerations when the service has high material content, which makes it more tangible and dilutes the share of the bill that is for performance (repair, installation and maintenance are priced labour plus materials), and when the service is equipment-intensive rather than people-intensive, since equipment brings economies of scale and capacity considerations (multi-screen cinemas, jumbo jets, 24-hour data processing). Entry barriersThings that make it hard for new competitors to enter a market, such as heavy equipment costs or technology.Where barriers are high, costs matter more than the market in setting service prices. push the same way: where heavy equipment (excavation), many locations (airport car rental) or technology (data processing) keep competitors out, cost plays a bigger role than the market. A data-processing firm will use some form of cost-plus; a dry cleaner will not. The limitation is the usual one: it ignores what customers will pay and what competitors charge.

Demand fluctuation pricing

Demand for services swings up and down largely outside the firm's control. Differential pricingCharging different prices to different customers or at different times for the same service.For services it appears twice: lower off-peak prices to shift demand, and different prices by willingness to pay as a tactic. charges lower prices in non-peak periods to shift demand out of the peaks and possibly raise total demand: cinema matinees, evening and weekend phone rates, weekend hotel rates, off-peak electricity. The risk is that the lower price cuts total profit: creating off-peak demand can produce incremental losses if the cost of handling the shifted demand exceeds the extra revenue, as with airlines' cheaper Sunday fares.

Developmental pricingA special low-priced version of a service created for off-peak periods to bring in extra demand.Market-oriented, not cost-oriented. It is market-oriented, not cost-oriented: its purpose is to increase demand. Since the basic lodging costs are already covered by peak-season prices, the off-season price need only cover the incremental cost of the package. The added profit (package price less incremental cost, plus what guests spend while there) can raise the resort's profitability or be used to lower peak prices and become more competitive. The risk is a flip-flop: if the conventions that filled July and August simply move to the cheap season, the resort does the same business for much less revenue. The slides' summary: off-peak pricing should create or shift demand, but only if the extra contribution exceeds the incremental cost, and demand that merely shifts without growing is a loss.

Pricing tactics for services

Tactics are essentially the same for services as for goods. Five are named:

Different prices for the same work according to each customer's willingness to pay. The most common tactic in pest control.
Discount pricing
Temporary reductions to increase business, attract trial or build a low-cost image. A restaurant discounts early meals so new customers sample the food and, it hopes, return at full price.
Guaranteed pricing
Payment is linked to achieving specified results.
Loss-leader pricing
A reduced price to win the first order, hoping for further business at the regular price.
Offset pricing
A low price on the core service and relatively high prices on the necessary extras.

Pricing During Inflationary and Shortage Conditions

Slides: Module 05, Bangladesh context · Book: Hanna and Dodge, Chapter 14. The slides follow the book and add Bangladesh examples.

Everything before this chapter assumed costs were roughly stable. This chapter asks what a manager does when costs are rising fast and inputs are scarce. The answer is not simply "raise prices." It is a whole programme: better information, honest accounting, a deliberate decision about who absorbs the cost, protection built into contracts, cost cutting, and careful handling of government, customers and dealers.

Why inflation and shortages are a marketing problem

The chapter opens with Kotler and Levy on demarketingMarketing designed to reduce demand, used when a firm cannot supply everyone.Kotler and Levy's term, in the chapter's opening quote: excess demand is as much a marketing problem as excess supply.: "excess demand is as much a marketing problem as excess supply." When there is not enough to go round, a company still faces hard decisions about which customers to serve and how to serve them. Inflation and scarcity, the book says, are a permanent part of the business environment, because world demand for raw materials keeps growing, mineral and commodity prices spiral, and developed countries depend on developing ones for basics like oil. The overriding worry is the upward push on inflation, which governs the pace of economic activity.

For pricing, inflation is a trap. Wages and materials rise, so some firms feel they have no choice but to pass the increases into prices, and then discover that volume falls. If competitors delay their increases, the firm loses customers. If every supplier raises prices together, customers switch to substitutes or other materials. Shortages add uncertainty about future costs and availability. Traditional pricing based on stable historical costs stops working. A firm's survival depends on how quickly it recognizes the changes and how well it adapts. The rest of the chapter is the list of responses.

Cost and information management

The first response is better information, not higher prices.

The firm needs a system that spots cost changes for each product, product group, market, key customer and operation, tracking the actual flow of expenses in each cost centreA part of the business whose costs are tracked separately.Under inflation you need to see costs move in each centre, not rely on old averages.. Economic and marketing intelligence matter more under inflation. Management needs to know where the economy is heading (indexes, indicators, price measures) to decide whether to expand, cut back or diversify; and it needs information on the expected supply and cost of materials, labour and energy, which may point to substituting one material or process for another. Companies must also watch changes in how customers behave: demands for more quality, greater price consciousness, preference for energy-saving products, interest in product information and safety. Absorb costs or pass them on?

The central decision. Absorb the cost increases and accept a lower gross marginThe difference between the selling price and the cost of the goods sold.Absorbing a cost increase means accepting a thinner gross margin to protect price and volume., protecting price and volume. Or pass them on as higher prices, protecting margin but risking demand and market share. Three dangers of passing them on: if competitors do not follow, the firm loses ground; if the whole industry raises prices together, customers may leave for substitute materials or products; and firms raising prices to keep up with inflation are themselves feeding it, as when rising health-care costs raise insurance costs, which raise business costs, which raise prices charged back to the health-care industry. The book's judgment: the best decision under inflation may be to absorb at least part of the increase to hold market position.

Industries with heavy fixed overheads may find flexible pricing useful: the willingness to cut prices aggressively to hold market share. Unlike traditional pricing, which sets a price expected to deliver a long-run target return, flexible pricing is a short-run system based on accurate, complete information about the market and the firm's position in it. When a price increase is being considered, the severity of competition and the presence or absence of unused capacity matter most. The Bangladesh example: a manufacturer with idle capacity protects key accounts with selective temporary price adjustments instead of a blanket increase.

How should a manager decide on a price increase? The slides' six questions

How severe is the increase in the firm's cost base? How price-sensitive is the target customer? How strong is competition, and how fast are competitors changing prices? Does the firm have unused capacity? Can costs be reduced, inputs substituted or specifications simplified instead? And would the increase damage long-term customer relationships more than it protects short-term margin?

Price protection systems

When there is a long gap between quoting a price and delivering, as in construction and commercial aircraft, costs can change in between. Several systems protect profit:

Surcharge
An extra charge that builds increases in specified primary-material costs into the price at delivery, usually calculated from a weighted index of wholesale prices for that material.
Indexed price
Part of the price is tied to movements in an index of the relevant material costs.
Open-end prices
No firm price is quoted until after delivery; the final price reflects material and labour cost changes since the quotation. Phrases such as "price subject to change without notice" do the same job for firms that print catalogues and price lists in advance.
Multiplier factor
A factor built into base prices and adjusted periodically. Relatively easy to run, and it protects profit without reprinting every price.
Escalation clause
A contract clause allowing the price to change under specified inflationary conditions. Especially valuable when manufacturing needs a long lead time between sale and delivery.

Bangladesh applications from the slides: construction projects, where quotation, procurement and completion are far apart; industrial equipment with imported components whose cost changes before delivery; institutional and business-to-business contracts, where escalation clausesA contract clause that lets the price rise if specified costs rise before delivery.Vital when there is a long gap between sale and delivery, as in construction or aircraft. allocate clearly defined input-cost risks; and large orders, where a formula-based adjustment is more transparent than an unexpected revision. Any mechanism should state the trigger, the reference index or input, and the adjustment method clearly.

Cost reduction

In normal times profit is maintained through higher prices, lower costs, more volume, or a mix. Under inflation, cost increases cannot be fully passed on and prices cannot be raised fast enough to keep up, so cost reduction becomes the main pricing strategy, especially if the goal is to hold price and protect volume. The book's list:

  • Shorten the product line. Drop the "losers" and marginal products so their materials and components go into profitable items. Weak products contribute a little profit but consume a disproportionate share of resources, including management time. General Electric competes only in markets where its position allows a given level of profit.
  • Find lower-cost substitutes, as long as the quality reputation is protected. Car makers replaced steel with aluminium, plastics and rubber; many firms have raised R&D spending on substitute materials.
  • Simplify the product. "Tune it down," redesign around standard components, and reduce model changes. The Bangladesh example: a furniture or consumer-goods producer redesigning around standard, locally available components when imports get expensive. The aim is not the cheapest possible product but lower total cost while protecting customer value.
  • Keep customers rather than chase new ones. It costs about $1 to keep a customer and about $6, mostly advertising, to win a new one. Reduce personal selling and become selective, limiting customers to those with the highest long-term potential (selective sellingConcentrating sales effort on the customers with the best long-term potential and dropping the rest.Part of cost reduction under inflation. It rests on the rule that keeping a customer costs about a sixth of winning a new one.).
  • Improve quality. Contrary to popular belief, better quality lowers cost. Motorola found that improving product and process quality gave the lowest manufacturing cost, by shrinking the hidden plantThe people, floor space and equipment tied up in fixing things that should have been done right the first time.Motorola estimated it at 25 to 35 percent of total production cost. Improving quality shrinks it, which is why quality lowers cost.: the people, floor space and equipment devoted to fixing what should have been done right first time, estimated at 25 to 35 percent of production cost. Quality also cuts the ill-defined cost of a lost customer, and high-quality firms achieve differentiation competitors cannot match. So cost reduction must never mean cutting quality indiscriminately; reducing defects and returns protects margin better.
  • Review packaging and sales aids critically: inner wraps, colourful packaging, outer cartons, fibre cases.

Government, communication and channel relationships

Pricing during inflation is also a matter of institutions and relationships.

Government policies and regulations

Regulation matters more in turbulent times. Price controlsGovernment limits on how much prices may rise.They can stop a firm passing on the full cost increase, for example allowing only the rise in certain approved costs. or guidelines can stop a firm pricing above the expected rate of cost inflation, for example allowing increases only for certain approved costs such as materials, components or salaries. Allocation of strategic commodities and quantity restrictions affect operations directly. Export controls shrink the firm's geographic market; import controls may open domestic opportunities; environmental controls raise manufacturing costs and reduce the supply of basic resources. Monetary and fiscal policy affect demand indirectly. The pricing executive must know the rules, anticipate changes and prepare contingency plans.

Centralizing pricing decisions

Centralizing pricingMaking pricing decisions at head office rather than leaving them to individual salespeople or regions.Gives control and speed, which flexible pricing needs. A price response to a competitor may have to be made in days. gives greater control, less deviation from the firm's objectives, and faster response to the market. It matters most under flexible pricing, because a decision to cut prices aggressively to follow a competitor must be made promptly or share is lost. The Bangladesh version: a national company sets central pricing rules and gives field teams limited, clearly defined authority for local conditions.

Public information and customer communication

Customers and the public resist change and want explanations, especially for prices. Explain the firm's actions promptly and give advance notice of changes wherever possible; this builds the image of a concerned participant in the economy. Advertising can show customers how to get more use from the product and how to make the best use of scarce materials, which reduces resistance to higher prices. Explain, rather than let customers rely on rumours; in Bangladesh, communicate significant price and pack changes consistently through retailers, official channels and customer-facing materials.

Closer relationships with resellers

Monitor the movement of goods through the channel and tell dealers about policy changes promptly, which is essential in good times and mandatory in turbulent ones. The salesperson carries the price change to resellers: explaining how costs rose, showing how the company cut its own costs to limit the increase, and offering cheaper substitutes from the range. Counselling resellers on their own problems turns shortages and price rises into a chance to build goodwill. Review cooperative advertising allowances, credit terms, discounts, freight charges and service policies; inflation may be the moment to negotiate longer contracts, better payment terms, and fewer discounts and services. And listen to what dealers report about customer and competitor reactions to price changes; that information shapes the next pricing decision.

Avoiding scarcity tactics

Some manufacturers are tempted to exploit the psychology of scarcityThe way buyers change behaviour when they believe a product is running short: hoarding, panic buying, paying more.Using it deliberately can create a real shortage and damage inventories, sales and trust. The book says avoid it.: when buyers believe a product is running short they hoard and panic-buy, which can create a real shortage and drive prices above normal. Using this deliberately is dangerous and in the long run damages inventories, production, sales and profitability. The slides pose the class discussion: is a higher price always unethical during a shortage, and when does legitimate cost recovery become exploitative pricing?

A need for a pricing strategy during inflation

A firm without a deliberate inflation and shortage pricing policy is "at the mercy of every shortage" and every cost change. Without one, firms short of scarce materials scramble to buy at any price and hope to recover it by charging more, and the result is lost volume, or restrictive government legislation to curb the trend. It is hard to resist "demanding what the traffic will bear," but the cumulative effect can endanger the economic system itself, causing collapse and unrest. So every company needs an inflation pricing plan, and the book goes further: producers of essential materials may need industry-wide cooperation on pricing designed to serve the social needs of the economy. The final principle on the slides: develop a deliberate policy rather than react opportunistically to each shortage.
